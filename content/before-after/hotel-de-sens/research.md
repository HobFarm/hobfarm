# Hôtel de Sens research dossier

Status: verified draft, checked August 21, 2026  
Primary public object: `src/content/articles/hotel-de-sens-before-and-after.mdx`

## Editorial finding

The transformation is not simply "ruin to restoration." The building remained in active commercial use while the surrounding block pressed close around it. Municipal acquisition, clearance around the site, a restoration lasting from 1929 to 1961, and conversion into the Bibliothèque Forney changed both the fabric and the way the building occupied the street.

The supplied image is a verified public-domain press photograph made by Agence Meurisse on May 26, 1920. The matching BnF record is Meurisse 80722: `https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b9033733v`.

## Verified history

### Origin and construction

- The Ville de Paris dates construction from 1475 to 1519.
- Tristan de Salazar, archbishop of Sens, ordered the building.
- The city explains his Paris position through the jurisdiction of Sens over the bishopric of Paris at the time.
- The current Ministry of Culture POP record identifies the building at 1 rue du Figuier and its current use as the Bibliothèque Forney.

### Occupants and uses

- Salazar's successors used the residence little.
- Marguerite de Valois, known as Queen Margot, occupied it for several months.
- The archbishops left in 1622.
- The property was rented to private occupants until the Revolution.
- After sale as national property, documented nineteenth-century uses included a transport company, laundry, food-canning operation, hare-hair cutter, Confiturerie de Saint James, and glass depot.
- The 1869 Pierre Emonts photograph independently makes the Saint James jam works visible through signage above the entrance.

### Purchase, protection, and restoration

- The City of Paris bought the building in 1911. The city's current history describes its condition as extremely dilapidated.
- The Ministry of Culture POP record lists a historic-monument classification order dated March 20, 1912.
- A Gustave Ferdinand Boberg drawing dated 1926 is explicitly titled as demolition of old houses opposite the Hôtel de Sens. It documents change to the surrounding block, not construction on the hotel.
- Restoration began in 1929 and ended in 1961.
- The sources reviewed here establish that date range but do not provide a dependable element-by-element list of medieval fabric, later additions, replaced masonry, reconstructed ornament, or roof work. Public copy should use "restored" and avoid declaring any individual feature original or reconstructed without another source.

### Bibliothèque Forney

- Paris adopted the principle of moving the Bibliothèque Forney into the building in 1929.
- The collections began moving in 1961, 32 years later.
- Forney opened in 1886 from a bequest intended to support artisan education.
- Current specialties include fine arts, decorative arts, graphic arts, crafts, fashion, advertising, and design.

## Marais and urban context

The Ville de Paris describes the Hôtel de Sens as a late-fifteenth-century residence whose structure later accommodated coach service, rental, and jam-factory uses. The city's current program also frames the surrounding urban environment as extensively remade in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

The image sequence adds visual evidence without replacing that history:

- The 1898–1900 Atget view shows neighboring construction close to a corner turret.
- The 1911 and 1920 photographs show a market structure or wall at the left and commercial frontage at the right.
- The 1926 Boberg drawing records demolition opposite the hotel.
- The 2016 and 2017 photographs show a clearer street presentation, repaired roofs and windows, pale masonry, and the library entrance.

The visual conclusion that the building became more exposed and monumental is an editorial inference from the documented sequence. It should not be written as though a single planning decree created every visible change.

## Before-and-after pair

### Before

Agence Meurisse, May 26, 1920. BnF/Gallica, public domain. This is the exact scene supplied for the feature.

### After

Guilhem Vellut, August 28, 2016. CC BY 2.0. This is the strongest reusable modern street-front image found because it shows both turrets and the central pointed entrance from a close frontal position.

### Comparison limit

The cameras are not identical. The modern position is closer and the lens perspective differs. The prepared comparison changes scale and crop only; it does not warp the architecture. Use "near-corresponding view" or "approximate match," never "same camera position."

## What changed

Documented or directly visible:

- Commercial and industrial occupation gave way to public library use.
- The municipality acquired the building in 1911.
- Restoration ran from 1929 to 1961.
- The surrounding block underwent demolition and redevelopment.
- Roofs, windows, stone surfaces, and openings appear repaired in the modern photographs.
- Market walls, commercial signs, advertisements, and curbside activity visible in 1920 are absent from the modern pair.

Editorial interpretation:

- The modern presentation isolates the building as a monument more than the 1920 street did.
- The most consequential visual change may be the removal of ordinary city fabric around the hotel, not a single architectural feature.
- Library use preserves the building through continued public function rather than treating it only as a sealed period room.

## What persisted

- The paired street turrets.
- The large pointed entrance and smaller pointed opening.
- The steep central roof mass and major gables.
- The asymmetric arrangement of windows and roof openings.
- The basic street-facing silhouette.

Persistence does not prove that every visible stone or detail is original. The restoration record needs a specialist architectural source before those claims can be made.

## Open questions

These are not blockers for the present feature. They are limits on more granular claims.
