## Editorial center

The article begins with a small scene and follows the wires outward.

Howie asks about the future of rock and roll, says Bruce Springsteen is "fucking the whole thing up," and answers his own question with "New Wave!" Chong barely follows the manifesto. The movie moves on.

The article does not.

The central question is:

**Why would Howie reasonably call New Wave the future of rock and roll in 1981?**

The answer is not that one genre replaced every other form of rock. The answer is that a dense network of people and techniques was changing how records could be composed, produced, performed, marketed, filmed, and understood. Many of those methods became ordinary by the middle of the decade.

The article should make five things clear:

1. New Wave was an unstable umbrella in 1981, not one coherent sound.
2. Brian Eno is a major connective node between experimental composition, Roxy Music, Bowie, Fripp, Talking Heads, Devo, and studio-based systems.
3. Giorgio Moroder is another major node, especially for sequenced pulse, synthesizer-driven pop, disco, Japan, film music, and the production language that later reaches Daft Punk.
4. The British, European, and American scenes form a feedback loop. London, New York, Akron, Los Angeles, Cologne, and Munich exchange people, records, technology, rhythm, performance ideas, and visual language.
5. Howie was substantially right about the future and wrong about the casualty. Springsteen did not need to disappear for the experimental vocabulary to spread.
