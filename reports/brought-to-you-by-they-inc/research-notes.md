# Research Notes: Brought to You by They, Inc.

## Editorial purpose

This article is a first-person field report about thirty years of public events becoming compulsory team products. It is not a complete media history and does not argue that all claims, outlets, institutions, or political groups are equivalent.

The published article now states this limit near the beginning. It treats the thirty-year span as the narrator's vantage point, not as a representative sample of every political group, country, medium, or audience. Its standard is equal inspection rather than equal blame. Documented differences in truth, reach, power, and harm remain part of the account.

The research pass had three jobs:

1. Establish the narrow facts needed for each personal or historical specimen.
2. Distinguish the documented event from the narrator’s interpretation and the recurring corporate satire.
3. Preserve uncertainty instead of filling gaps with a convenient story.

The article uses four evidence labels internally:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary, official, or well-designed research source.
- **Historical interpretation:** a defensible explanation drawn from several sources, stated as interpretation rather than a settled fact.
- **Narrator recollection:** personal experience supplied by the author brief, including the Las Vegas lunchroom, the senior-year government class, news-production work during the Schiavo case, and the 2016 return from Vietnam.
- **Satirical metaphor:** They, Inc., its departments, its product language, and lines such as “Everyone wanted the list until the list contained everybody.”

## Search method

The research process followed the method advocated in the article:

1. State the narrow claim.
2. Search for the earliest recoverable or primary record.
3. Sort timelines before reading later summaries.
4. Compare official records with an independent account or framing study.
5. Count origins rather than copies.
6. Mark what remains disputed.
7. Cut an example when its provenance cannot support the intended use.

Tracking parameters were removed from publication links.

## Mechanism-level evidence and limits

The They, Inc. metaphor rests on several documented mechanisms operating at different stages of the supply chain:

- Audience demand can shape outlet slant. Gentzkow and Shapiro found economically meaningful demand for ideologically congenial news and publisher responses to that demand.
- Repetition can increase perceived truth. A 2026 meta-analysis covered 182 studies, 366 effect sizes, and 31,184 participants. It found a small but robust illusory truth effect after correction for small-study effects, with substantial variation across methods and material.
- Accuracy and social incentives can change partisan judgments. Across four U.S. experiments, financial accuracy incentives improved accuracy and reduced partisan bias by about 30 percent. Incentives to identify news political allies would like reduced accuracy. The researchers also found an unequal baseline between liberals and conservatives in their samples, so the article does not convert a shared mechanism into a claim of equal performance.
- People generally retain useful discernment. A 2025 systematic review and preregistered meta-analysis covered 67 experimental articles, 303 effect sizes, nearly 195,000 participants, and 40 countries. Participants generally distinguished true from false news and erred toward skepticism. They were more skeptical of politically discordant news.
- News can improve knowledge. A preregistered 2025 field experiment with 3,395 social-media users in France and Germany found that following politically balanced news sources on Instagram and WhatsApp improved current-affairs knowledge and belief accuracy.
- Misinformation interventions can help and create spillovers. Three experiments in the United States, Poland, and Hong Kong found that fact-checking, media-literacy tips, and coverage of misinformation reduced false beliefs while also increasing skepticism toward accurate information.

Sources:

- [NBER: What Drives Media Slant?](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12707)
- [Nature Communications: Systematic review and meta-analysis of the illusory truth effect](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-70041-x)
- [Nature Human Behaviour: Accuracy and social motivations shape judgments of misinformation](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01540-w)
- [Nature Human Behaviour: Spotting false news and doubting true news](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02086-1)
- [Nature Human Behaviour: Following news on social media boosts knowledge, belief accuracy and trust](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02205-6)
- [Nature Human Behaviour: Prominent misinformation interventions reduce misperceptions but increase skepticism](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01884-x)

These findings establish a boundary on the article's metaphor. The article does not claim that reporting is useless, audiences are passive, corrections never work, or everyone believes everything.

## High-confidence factual checks

### 2006 lettuce outbreak

The CDC’s December 14, 2006 update reported 71 illnesses associated with Taco Bell restaurants in five states: New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, Delaware, and South Carolina. It identified shredded lettuce as the most likely source. Nevada was not among the reported states.

Source:

- [CDC Stacks: Multi-state Outbreak of E. coli O157 Infections, November–December 2006](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/25166)

### Audience demand and outlet slant

Gentzkow and Shapiro found economically meaningful demand for ideologically congenial U.S. newspaper coverage and strong publisher responses to consumer preferences. The paper does not prove that every outlet simply gives readers what they want. It supports the narrower point that audience demand can help shape the product.

Sources:

- [NBER paper page](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12707)
- [NBER working paper PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12707/w12707.pdf)

### Clinton impeachment

The House impeached Bill Clinton on December 19, 1998. The Senate acquitted him on February 12, 1999. Pew’s retrospective found that public approval remained resilient while many people had limited interest in following the process closely.

Sources:

- [Congress.gov: H.Res. 611 actions](https://www.congress.gov/bill/105th-congress/house-resolution/611/all-actions)

The certified Electoral College result was Bush 271, Gore 266, with one abstention. Gore received more popular votes. During the recount dispute, legitimacy judgments and interpretations of the counting process divided sharply by party.

### Iraq weapons of mass destruction

The federal WMD commission concluded that the U.S. intelligence community was wrong in almost all prewar judgments about Iraqi weapons of mass destruction. Official speeches before the war publicly asserted weapons programs, mobile biological facilities, and links to terrorist networks. Pew’s retrospective connects fear and false beliefs to public support for the war.

### AI labor

The ILO estimates occupational exposure, not certain job loss. Its 2025 working paper places one in four workers in occupations with some exposure and 3.3 percent of global employment in the highest category. Transformation is described as more likely than simple wholesale replacement.

### AI electricity and water

Berkeley Lab estimated data-center electricity use at about 4.4 percent of U.S. electricity in 2023 and projected a range of 6.7 to 12 percent by 2028. A separate 2025 Berkeley Lab study found workload-level water-use variation exceeding 10,000-fold.

Sources:

### Elon Musk as a political signal

Pew’s January 2025 survey found a large partisan gap in favorable views of Musk: 73 percent among Republicans and Republican-leaning independents, compared with 12 percent among Democrats and Democratic leaners. Peer-reviewed and working-paper research also finds that views of Musk affect Tesla interest and sales.

Sources:

- [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/02/19/how-americans-view-elon-musk-and-mark-zuckerberg/)
- [Humanities and Social Sciences Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05242-8)
- [NBER: The Musk Partisan Effect on Tesla Sales](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34413/w34413.pdf)

The article uses the Pew gap and avoids treating the causal sales estimate as settled.
