# Dragon’s Lair article: research notes

Checked August 15, 2026.

## Central finding

The sources support treating Dragon’s Lair as both a technical landmark and a constrained transaction. The Strong documents the 1983 Cinematronics debut, LaserDisc playback, fifty-cent play, timing and memorization, and three-life structure. A contemporary Washington Post report describes the controller-to-disc system and records Don Bluth’s placement of the work between arcade and film industries. D23 documents the Disney career and 1979 departure that preceded Don Bluth Productions.

The article’s main value judgment comes from the author’s recollection: two quarters could buy one short Dragon’s Lair lesson or two attempts elsewhere. That comparison is not presented as measured average play time. A trained player could finish on one credit; a new player faced a costly memorization loop.

## Release chronology

- 1977: Atari VCS, later called Atari 2600.
- 1983: Dragon’s Lair and Spy Hunter.
- 1985: Gauntlet and Ghosts ’n Goblins.
- 1986: Rampage.
- 1988: LED Storm and Altered Beast.
- 1991: Street Fighter II.
- 1992: Mortal Kombat and Wolfenstein 3D.
- 1993: Doom.

Release years do not claim the exact date each cabinet reached the author’s local arcade. The figure states that boundary.

## Source boundary

The user supplied the Boing Boing URL and its headline. Direct retrieval encountered a CDN JavaScript challenge during this reporting pass. The link remains the origin artifact, but the historical claims do not depend on unread Boing Boing body text.

The modern-redemption paragraph remains first-person observation. Research on gambling-like systems is broader than the physical ticket arcades described here, so the article avoids using it to diagnose children, declare every redemption game gambling, or make a causal claim.

## Editorial classification

Technology owns the article because the argument depends on coin-operated economics, LaserDisc playback, control constraints, home hardware, and modifiable PC software. Animation and Don Bluth are central relationships, but removing the technical system would remove the article’s main claim. Strict series membership is empty.
