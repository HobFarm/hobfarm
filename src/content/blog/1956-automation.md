In January 1956, Playboy ran a short illustrated forecast package about the year ahead. One panel was labeled **Labor**. The caption read:

> "Automation will make further inroads into business and industry. However, certain highly specialized fields will remain relatively free from encroachment by I.B.M."

The joke is simple enough: IBM will automate business and industry, but some forms of work are too specialized, too human, or too tied to private desire to be swallowed by machines.

Then you look at the drawing.

There is already a mechanical woman on the bed.

That is what makes the cartoon useful seventy years later. It says one thing and draws another. The text says IBM will not reach certain human zones. The image says the machine is already in the room.

Replace **I.B.M.** with **AI** and the sentence still works:

> "Automation will make further inroads into business and industry. However, certain highly specialized fields will remain relatively free from encroachment by AI."

That is basically what people say now. AI will take the boring work, the paperwork, the spreadsheets, the emails, the low-level code, the customer service scripts, the factory tasks, the SEO trash, the generic content, the tedious stuff. But not the special stuff. Not taste. Not art. Not physical presence. Not charm. Not care. Not whatever I personally happen to do.

Every generation draws the safety line just past itself.

## The future did not disprove the cartoon. It made the cartoon reusable.

The cartoon is not interesting because Playboy "predicted" modern AI or humanoid robots. That would be too cheap. The stronger point is that the cartoon captures a recurring automation loop.

First, a machine enters a boring part of work.

Then the machine gets better.

Then the definition of "boring" expands.

Then the people who thought they were outside the machine's reach find out they were standing in the next category.

The 1956 caption treats IBM as the automation monster. That made sense. IBM was not just a company selling machines. IBM had become a symbol for a bigger shift: offices, governments, factories, banks, and businesses were becoming machine-readable.

That did not mean every worker vanished. That is the lazy version of the story. What actually happened was stranger.

IBM did not eliminate labor. IBM moved labor into the machine room.

## 1930s: bureaucracy becomes machine-readable

Before "computers" looked like computers, IBM was already part of the automation story. Punched cards, tabulators, sorters, collators, and keypunch machines turned records into something a machine could read, sort, and process.

This matters because the first big automation revolution was not robots walking around a factory. It was information becoming structured.

A name became a card. A payment became a field. A worker became a record. A government program became a data-processing problem.

The 1935 Social Security Act created a bookkeeping problem at national scale. Millions of people, employers, wages, accounts, and payments had to be tracked. IBM's punched-card systems became part of that administrative machine. That is a key part of the automation story: before computers "thought," they organized. They counted. They sorted. They made huge institutions legible to themselves.

That is not as flashy as a robot girl on a bed, but it is more important. Once the world becomes recordable, the next step is making it computable.

The old machine did not need to be intelligent. It just needed the world to be chopped into pieces small enough for the machine to handle.

That is still how a lot of automation works.

## 1950s: IBM becomes the office monster

<figure class="blog-wide">
  <img src="https://cdn.hob.farm/blog/1956-automation/1953-ibm-650-with-operator.jpg" alt="IBM 650 with an operator in a 1950s machine room." />
  <figcaption>IBM 650, announced in 1953, helped make commercial business computing feel real.</figcaption>
</figure>

By the 1950s, IBM was no longer just punched cards and electromechanical business machines. The company was moving into electronic data processing. The IBM 650, announced in 1953, became one of the most important commercial computers of the decade. It helped make the computer feel like a business machine, not just a scientific instrument or military device.

That is the atmosphere behind the 1956 cartoon.

When the cartoon says "I.B.M.," it is not doing a neutral product reference. It is using IBM as a cultural shorthand. IBM means the office machine. The company machine. The thing that arrives with cabinets, cards, operators, executives, consultants, and a promise that the future will be faster and more efficient.

The fear was not exactly wrong. Computers did change labor. They changed clerical work, accounting, payroll, inventory, logistics, banking, research, insurance, government administration, and management itself.

But the fear was also incomplete. The machines did not arrive alone. They brought people with them.

Operators. Programmers. Systems analysts. Repair technicians. Data-entry workers. Machine-room supervisors. Managers who had to redesign the process so the machine could do anything useful.

Automation did not remove work from the building. It reorganized the building around the machine.

That is the part people still miss with AI.

You cannot just have a bunch of AI agents doing things and call that a company. Someone has to define the work, give the agents context, check outputs, approve actions, manage permissions, catch mistakes, handle edge cases, refresh the data, and decide whether the loop is still producing anything worth keeping.

AI agents are not employees. They are weird interns with root access unless someone builds the workflow around them.

## 1959 to the 1980s: the machine becomes a platform

<figure class="blog-wide">
  <img src="https://cdn.hob.farm/blog/1956-automation/ibm1401.jpg" alt="IBM 1401 machine-room scene with business computing equipment and operators." />
  <figcaption>The IBM 1401 pushed business computing deeper into ordinary commercial workflows.</figcaption>
</figure>

The IBM 1401, introduced in 1959, helped push computing into more business environments. It was not just a single machine doing isolated calculations. It was part of a larger shift toward stored programs, business data processing, and repeatable workflows.

Then came System/360 in 1964.

<figure class="blog-wide">
  <img src="https://cdn.hob.farm/blog/1956-automation/1965-system360-model-65.jpg" alt="IBM System/360 Model 65 computer installation." />
  <figcaption>IBM System/360 turned business computing into a platform logic, not just a machine purchase.</figcaption>
</figure>

System/360 is important because it changed the shape of computing. IBM was no longer just selling separate machines for separate jobs. It was selling a family of compatible systems. A business could enter the IBM world at one scale and grow into another. That is platform logic.

This is where the modern world starts to become recognizable.

The machine is no longer just a machine. The machine becomes an environment.

Once a company builds its records, processes, staff, training, software, and budgets around a platform, the platform becomes part of the organization's nervous system. It changes what the organization can do, how it thinks, what it measures, what it ignores, and what kinds of workers it needs.

That is the real automation story.

The work does not simply disappear. It moves up the stack.

Manual recordkeeping becomes data processing. Data processing becomes IT. IT becomes enterprise software. Enterprise software becomes cloud platforms. Cloud platforms become AI infrastructure. AI infrastructure becomes agent management, model governance, data pipelines, automation design, and endless dashboard rituals.

The machine gets smarter, but the stack gets thicker.

By 1981, IBM also helped make the personal computer respectable to business buyers. The IBM PC did not stay under IBM's control forever, but that is part of the point. Sometimes the giant company opens a door and the weird ecosystem rushes through. Clones, add-ons, software companies, repair shops, consultants, magazines, user groups, games, spreadsheets, bedroom programmers, office workers, and entire new categories of labor appear around the thing.

That is the better version of the automation story.

Not "IBM took our jobs."

More like:

IBM helped create a machine world so large that everyone eventually needed a relationship with it.

## IBM also made strange futures possible

<figure class="blog-wide">
  <img src="https://cdn.hob.farm/blog/1956-automation/IBM-research-fractal-style-lab.png" alt="Corporate machine-room and fractal-style lab editorial image." />
  <figcaption>Corporate machine rooms did not only produce business automation. They also produced researchers, ideas, and visual systems that escaped the office.</figcaption>
</figure>

There is another part of IBM that matters for a HobFarm reading: the research culture.

Benoit Mandelbrot spent decades at IBM. His work on fractal geometry is a perfect example of how corporate research can accidentally leak into culture in ways that are much stranger than payroll automation.

Fractals are not just math trivia. They became a way to think about coastlines, clouds, branching structures, turbulence, markets, nature, computer graphics, psychedelic visual culture, simulation, texture, recursive detail, and the weird beauty of systems that repeat without becoming simple.

That is the part of IBM I like for this article. IBM was not only a job-taking machine. It was also a talent furnace. A place where engineers, mathematicians, operators, programmers, and researchers helped build concepts that later escaped into the rest of culture.

This is the more honest automation story.

Big systems can flatten work, centralize power, and make life worse. They can also produce tools, standards, people, methods, and accidents that become useful far outside the original business case.

That tension is the whole thing.

The problem is not that machines exist. The problem is who controls them, who can use them, who can modify them, who gets priced out, and who has to fight the tool just to make something interesting.

## 2026: IBM is still here, just quieter

<figure class="blog-wide">
  <img src="https://cdn.hob.farm/blog/1956-automation/modern-ibm.png" alt="Modern IBM, watsonx, AI, mainframe, and quantum computing editorial image." />
  <figcaption>IBM today is less visible in consumer AI culture, but still active in enterprise AI, hybrid cloud, mainframes, consulting, automation, and quantum computing.</figcaption>
</figure>

You do not hear about IBM the same way you hear about OpenAI, Google, Meta, NVIDIA, Anthropic, or whatever model provider is melting everyone's workflow this month.

That does not mean IBM disappeared.

IBM today is more enterprise than consumer spectacle. It is in hybrid cloud, consulting, automation, mainframes, enterprise AI, governance, and quantum computing. Its watsonx products are pitched around business AI, model management, and agents. Its z17 mainframe is marketed as a mainframe built for the AI age. Its quantum roadmap points toward fault-tolerant quantum computing later this decade.

That makes IBM weirdly relevant to the article. It started as the automation symbol in the 1956 cartoon. Seventy years later, it is still selling the serious version of automation: not the viral chatbot, not the influencer demo, but the governed enterprise system.

IBM is not the robot girl in the room anymore. It is the room.

The infrastructure. The control plane. The compliance layer. The machine that gets installed where nobody on Instagram is looking.

## China builds bodies. America builds compute.

The modern version of the 1956 cartoon has split into two big visual lanes.

China is pushing physical automation: industrial robots, humanoid robots, robot factories, warehouse systems, service robots, embodied AI, and machines that move through the world.

America is concentrated around compute: chips, data centers, cloud platforms, model companies, AI infrastructure, big tech, big government contracts, and institutional bottlenecks.

That is an oversimplification, but it is a useful visual split.

China says: build the robots, deploy the robots, train the robots, scale the robots.

America says: build the chips, train the models, rent the cloud, own the platform, govern the API.

Both matter. Both are powerful. Both are dangerous when too much control piles up in too few places.

Moya is the perfect modern punchline because it is not just a robot arm welding cars. It is a humanoid social robot. It is designed to be looked at as a presence. It brings the 1956 cartoon back into the room: the machine is no longer only doing arithmetic, sorting cards, or running payroll. It is learning posture, face, motion, warmth, service, attention, and the performance layer around human interaction.

That is where the old Playboy joke gets nasty.

In 1956, the joke was: IBM cannot automate this.

In 2026, that sounds like a product roadmap.

## Automation does not eliminate labor. It moves labor up the stack.

This is the part that matters most for the next seventy years.

The dumb automation argument is:

Robot does work. Human goes away.

The better version is:

Robot does repeatable work. Human manages the system that decides what the work is, how the work changes, what counts as good, what gets updated, and what happens when the loop drifts.

That is true for IBM machines. It is true for AI agents. It is true for humanoid robots. It is true for image generation, video tools, creative workflows, enterprise software, logistics, and probably whatever comes next.

A machine can run a process. It can optimize a local task. It can generate variations. It can monitor signals. It can suggest improvements. Eventually, it may improve parts of its own workflow.

But these systems still need fresh contact with reality.

Otherwise they become a copy of a copy of a copy.

Stale data. Bad incentives. Recursive outputs. Synthetic training sludge. Local optimization. Weird edge cases. Model drift. Tool updates that break the workflow. Safety systems that block legitimate work. Pricing changes. Platform rules. Hardware limits. Hidden variables. A machine that worked yesterday and now gives you a different answer because the provider changed something behind the wall.

That is not science fiction. That is already normal creative work with AI.

The promise is that the machine does the work.

The reality is that the worker has to learn the machine, manage the machine, route around the machine, pay for the machine, and keep adapting because the machine keeps changing shape.

## The word-and-logic brush

<figure class="blog-wide">
  <img src="https://cdn.hob.farm/blog/1956-automation/future-workshop.png" alt="Generated future creative workshop scene with people steering automated tools." />
  <figcaption>The better future is not workerless. It is smaller, more legible, and more distributed, with people steering automated tools instead of being trapped inside them.</figcaption>
</figure>

For creative work, AI is not just a productivity button. At its best, it is closer to painting with words and logic instead of a brush.

That sounds easy until you actually do it.

A normal brush does not rewrite its rules every week. It does not get nerfed by a policy update. It does not suddenly decide your idea is too weird. It does not charge a monthly subscription, hide the variables, change the model, break the style, or produce a different result because the provider's priorities changed.

AI tools can be powerful, but they are not neutral. They are built by companies with their own incentives, costs, rules, fears, investors, legal exposure, and idea of what "good" output looks like.

That is fine if you want generic output.

It is a problem if you are trying to make something specific.

For people using AI to make pictures, videos, websites, characters, voices, jokes, articles, fake ads, cartoons, research trails, and strange visual systems, the tool is not just doing the work. The tool is part of the material. It has grain. It has failure modes. It has bias toward certain shapes. It has censorship. It has cost. It has hidden knobs.

You can make great things with it, but you spend a lot of time negotiating with the machine.

That is why the future should not belong only to giant AI platforms.

## The smaller-builder path

The best future is not "no automation."

That is not happening, and it is not even desirable. Machines are useful. Automation is useful. AI is useful. Robots are useful. The question is whether the tool layer becomes more open, more diverse, more modifiable, and more useful to actual builders, or whether everything gets trapped inside giant institutional dashboards.

If big tech companies, research labs, governments, and hardware companies release more open tools, better small models, usable local workflows, affordable compute access, open standards, grants, documentation, and less hostile creative infrastructure, the result could be a much healthier AI culture.

Not one model trying to be everything to everyone.

More small systems. More specialized tools. More weird local workflows. More people using AI for the things they actually care about.

Someone building comics does not need the same system as someone doing legal discovery. Someone making avatar videos does not need the same system as a bank. Someone running robots in a warehouse does not need the same system as someone restoring old magazine scans or making fake 1970s ads.

A diverse tool ecosystem would produce more diverse culture.

That is the part big platforms keep flattening. The more centralized the tool layer becomes, the more the output starts to feel the same. Same safety tone. Same visual polish. Same prompt tricks. Same model defaults. Same approved weirdness. Same subscription prison.

The future should not be one giant machine making everything slightly acceptable.

It should be a field of smaller machines that people can actually steer.

## 2096: the job is loop management

So what does this look like seventy years from now?

Nobody knows, which is the point. A January 1956 cartoon could not literally predict Moya, AI agents, server farms, synthetic influencers, local models, humanoid training data, or a guy using an AI video tool to animate a fake 1950s robot editorial cartoon for social media.

But it did capture the loop.

In 1956, IBM was coming for the office.

In 2026, AI is coming for the workflow.

By 2096, the real job may be keeping the automated world from turning into a copy of a copy of a copy.

The future is probably not workerless. It is loop-managed.

Machines will do more visible work. Robots will handle more physical tasks. AI systems will write, sort, schedule, generate, simulate, monitor, summarize, compose, edit, fabricate, and operate across more of daily life.

But people will still be needed to keep those systems connected to reality, value, maintenance, taste, context, consequences, and whatever new weird thing the machine cannot understand because nobody told it the world changed.

The machine never arrives alone.

It brings a management structure.

The question is whether that structure belongs only to the biggest companies and governments, or whether smaller builders get enough access to make something better, stranger, more specific, and more alive.

## The cartoon still works

That is why the January 1956 Playboy cartoon still lands.

The joke was supposed to be that automation has limits.

The image accidentally says the opposite.

The machine is already in the room, smiling politely, waiting for the rest of the century to catch up.
