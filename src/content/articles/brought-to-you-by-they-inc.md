The lettuce was going to make me shit my pants.

This news arrived in a work lunchroom in Las Vegas. I was about to eat something with lettuce on it when a coworker warned me that people were getting sick. The president had screwed something up. Lettuce was dangerous now.

“That’s bullshit,” I said.

There had been an actual outbreak. In November and December 2006, people became sick after eating at Taco Bell restaurants. The [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/25166) identified shredded lettuce as the most likely source. Seventy-one illnesses were reported across New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, Delaware, and South Carolina. Fifty-three people were hospitalized. That was real.

I was not eating at Taco Bell. I was not in one of those five states. My coworker did not know which supplier, product, dates, restaurants, or states were involved. He could not tell me what the president had done to the lettuce.

He knew the conclusion.

That was the useful part of the story. A narrow outbreak had gone into the machine. The machine removed the scope, location, product, dates, and causal chain. It kept the sickness and attached the president. The practical warning disappeared. The political payload survived.

Then the finished product arrived in the lunchroom under the cover of personal concern.

I have spent most of my adult life meeting products like this. They come from television, newspapers, official statements, cable panels, websites, feeds, podcasts, coworkers, friends, and relatives. They are usually built from something real. That is why they travel so well.

The lie is often too weak to survive on its own.

The incomplete truth with the uncertainty removed can go anywhere.

## Welcome to They, Inc.

They, Inc. is the company people cite when they cannot name a source.

They say lettuce is dangerous.

They say the election was stolen.

They say the war is necessary.

They say the vaccine does something terrible.

They say the unvaccinated are doing something terrible.

They say a celebrity is part of a psychological operation.

They say artificial intelligence uses a bottle of water every time somebody asks it a question.

Sometimes “they” can be recovered. A person appeared on a program. A government agency issued a report. A reporter wrote an article. A study reached a qualified conclusion. An account clipped six seconds from a longer recording. A headline turned the clip into an accusation. A coworker remembered the accusation and forgot the headline.

By the time it reaches me, every link in that chain has collapsed into one anonymous authority.

That authority is They, Inc.

The company does not need a headquarters. It has suppliers, contractors, distributors, franchises, hostile competitors, and customers who perform unpaid fulfillment. Its divisions include Some People, LLC, the Caption Factory, Public Certainty, Audience Satisfaction, Flags and Identity Products, the Department of Unresolved Scandals, and Regional Lunchroom Distribution.

The corporate structure is a joke.

The product is not.

They, Inc. is the layered information market that converts events into audience-compatible certainty. It operates in the distance between an event and the account people carry around as settled truth. It turns agreement into belonging and refusal into suspected allegiance to the enemy.

### The limits of this map

They, Inc. is my name for a recurring process. It is not a literal company, a secret committee, or a complete theory of media, politics, psychology, and power.

This article is an overview drawn from thirty years of things I saw, heard, researched, produced, argued about, and eventually learned to reject at the loading dock. Most of its examples come from American public life and the places where that life crossed my work, family, friendships, travel, and use of technology. It is not a representative sample of every outlet, party, country, platform, or community. A complete account would need a library.

Omission is not endorsement. Inclusion does not make every example morally, legally, or historically equivalent.

The standard here is equal inspection, not equal blame. If one claim is documented and its rival is false, putting the answer halfway between them would be another distortion. If one faction has more institutional power, greater reach, or a worse factual record in a particular case, that difference belongs in the account. The shared machinery does not guarantee equal output.

I keep four kinds of sentence separate. Dates, counts, laws, poll results, and study findings should lead to recoverable sources. Historical explanations should be presented as interpretations. Private experiences are recollections, not survey data. They, Inc. and its departments are satire.

Those categories cannot quietly trade places just because one team likes the result.

This does not mean every report is false, every institution is corrupt, or every claim is equal. Reality has outcomes. Evidence supports or undermines explanations of those outcomes. A claim can be true even when a terrible person says it. A claim can be false even when it serves a good cause.

I am not neutral between every claim.

I am independent of your team.

That position should be simple. It has become difficult to process because They, Inc. sells two main membership packages, and every refusal is filed as an order for the other one.

## The Supply Chain

Imagine a televised press conference.

A public figure stands at a podium. Reporters sit in the room. Cameras record the statement. People at home can hear the same words as the reporters.

That looks like direct access.

Then the production lines split.

One reporter selects the first sentence. Another leads with the third. One editor writes a headline about reassurance. Another writes a headline about evasion. A pundit explains what the speaker was really trying to do. A rival outlet asks why the speaker refused to answer a question that came later. A foreign outlet places the statement inside a different history. An influencer clips the facial expression between two sentences. An account adds music. Users remember the emotional conclusion. The coworker next morning remembers what everybody knows.

The original recording can remain untouched through the whole process.

They, Inc. rarely needs to alter the footage. It changes which edit reaches you.

The stages are easy to list:

> event  
> → source statement  
> → report  
> → commentary  
> → headline or clip  
> → audience-approved interpretation  
> → remembered factoid  
> → group talking point  
> → “they say”

Every arrow is a decision.

The first account may be wrong. It may also be accurate and incomplete. The reporter has limited time. The editor has limited space. The headline has limited characters. The audience has limited attention. The business has customers. The platform has a ranking system. The political group has a preferred lesson.

There is no need for a secret meeting where the entire chain agrees to lie. Market pressure can produce coordination without conspiracy. Research by economists Matthew Gentzkow and Jesse Shapiro found [economically meaningful demand for ideologically congenial news](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12707) and strong publisher responses to audience preferences. Customers help shape the product they later describe as an independent authority.

The later stages have evidence too.

Repetition changes how a claim feels. A [2026 meta-analysis of 182 studies](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-70041-x), covering 31,184 participants and research published from 1977 through 2025, found a small but robust illusory truth effect. Repeated statements tended to be rated as more truthful than new ones. The effect appeared across true and false statements, headlines, opinions, rumors, and other material, although its size varied.

Identity changes the inspection process. Across four U.S. experiments, [accuracy incentives improved judgments and reduced partisan bias](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01540-w), while incentives to identify headlines political allies would like reduced accuracy. The study also found unequal baseline performance between liberals and conservatives in its samples. A shared vulnerability does not mean identical behavior, and the evidence should not be sanded down to make the parties look even.

People are not helpless receivers. A [2025 meta-analysis of 67 experimental articles](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02086-1), with nearly 195,000 participants across 40 countries, found that people generally distinguished true news from false news and tended toward skepticism. It also found that people were more skeptical of news that conflicted with their politics.

Reporting can inform. A [preregistered 2025 field experiment](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02205-6) in France and Germany found that following politically balanced news sources on Instagram and WhatsApp improved current-affairs knowledge and belief accuracy. Corrections can work too, although a [three-country set of experiments](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01884-x) found a tradeoff: fact-checking, media-literacy tips, and coverage of misinformation reduced belief in false information while also lowering the credibility of accurate information.

That is the boundary of the factory metaphor. The problem is not that everybody believes everything. The problem is that selection, repetition, incentive, identity, and memory can bend an otherwise usable act of judgment at several points in the same trip.

My mother gives me a harmless version of the same supply chain.

She watches *CBS Sunday Morning*. Somebody appears in a segment and says something interesting. A reporter or host interprets it. CBS packages it. My mother remembers it. Later she tells me what “they say.”

Her version is usually recoverable. There was a real guest. There was a real segment. She is not running a partisan influence operation from the couch. Memory compressed the path.

The mechanism stays visible:

guest → program → remembered conclusion → they.

Some People, LLC begins where that path becomes useful to the speaker.

“Some people say” allows a host to introduce an allegation while posing as a messenger. “Critics are asking” turns a question into a constituency. “Concerns are growing” can describe evidence, polling, three loud accounts, or the producer’s need to get to commercial.

The phrase does not tell you whether the crowd exists.

It brings the crowd into the room.

## Thirty Years of Product Development

This is not a complete history of American media. I already wrote the larger machinery as media history in [Vacation Into Nothing](/articles/vacation-into-nothing/). This is my field report.

I watched the company add capabilities for thirty years. Each period taught it something new about turning reality into compulsory team products.

### 1998: Private life becomes continuous programming

The Clinton and Lewinsky scandal ran through my senior-year government class.

A president had a sexual relationship with a White House intern. He denied it publicly. The investigation moved through depositions, recordings, legal definitions, a special prosecutor, a grand jury, the House, and the Senate. The House impeached Bill Clinton in December 1998. The Senate acquitted him in February 1999.

The conduct, the deception, the institutional questions, and the abuse-of-power arguments were real subjects.

They were also programming.

A private relationship became an endless national product with legal, sexual, moral, and partisan editions. Every new document produced panels. Every phrase became a symbol. The spectacle could be condemned as a constitutional crisis, defended as a private matter, or consumed as celebrity scandal.

[Pew’s retrospective](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/10/03/clintons-impeachment-barely-dented-his-public-support-and-it-turned-off-many-americans/) found that the process did not destroy Clinton’s public approval and that many Americans were tired of the story. That did not shut down the factory. Exhaustion was another response to measure and discuss.

The new capability was continuous moral theater. A private scandal could become a government series with no natural commercial break.

### 2000: Reality forks live

The 2000 presidential election showed television attempting to declare reality before reality had finished loading.

Florida was called for Al Gore, moved back into uncertainty, called for George W. Bush, and pulled back again. Gore conceded, withdrew his concession, and entered a recount and court fight. Each legal decision came with instant translation. Each ballot design became an argument about legitimacy. The Supreme Court ended the recount in *Bush v. Gore*. The certified result gave Bush 271 electoral votes and Gore 266, while Gore won more popular votes.

Those are facts.

The public meaning split before the count ended. One audience watched Democrats try to keep counting until they got the answer they wanted. Another watched Republicans stop the count before every vote could be heard. Television supplied both realities while the lawyers were still producing exhibits.

[Pew found](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2000/12/01/many-question-bush-or-gore-as-legitimate-winner/) that legitimacy judgments were strongly partisan during the dispute. Its [study of campaign coverage](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2001/03/15/campaign-2000/) documented negative and uneven framing before election day.

The new capability was a live reality fork. One unresolved event could support incompatible national histories before the government had a result.

### 2001–2003: Permanent emergency mode

September 11 created a different information climate.

The attacks were real. The deaths were real. The fear was real. The government had genuine responsibilities. It also had access, intelligence, secrecy, patriotic authority, and a public trying to understand a threat none of us could see.

Official claims entered the distribution system with the force of emergency. Terror alerts, unnamed officials, suspected connections, threat assessments, military graphics, expert panels, and presidential speeches formed a permanent atmosphere.

Then came Iraq.

In 2002 and early 2003, the administration made confident public claims about Saddam Hussein’s weapons programs and ties to terrorism. Many of those claims drew on intelligence assessments. Television and newspapers amplified them. The distinction between established evidence, inference, disputed intelligence, and worst-case possibility was difficult to maintain under repetition.

A later federal commission concluded that the intelligence community had been [“dead wrong in almost all of its pre-war judgments”](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-WMD/pdf/GPO-WMD.pdf) about Iraqi weapons of mass destruction. A [Pew retrospective](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/03/14/a-look-back-at-how-fear-and-false-beliefs-bolstered-u-s-public-support-for-war-in-iraq/) documented how fear and false beliefs about Iraq, terrorism, and weapons supported the war.

That conclusion does not prove every official lied or every reporter acted in bad faith. It proves that official confidence and television repetition can turn uncertain claims into public knowledge before the evidence is settled.

The new capability was emergency certification.

The package came stamped with national security. Doubt could be interpreted as disloyalty. By the time the stamp failed, the war existed.

### Some People, LLC

The 2004 documentary *Outfoxed* focused on Fox News and included a rapid sequence of hosts using the phrase “some people say.” The criticism was fair and useful. The film also belonged to a political media fight and was packaged for an audience ready to see Fox as the machine.

Fox was a visible factory line. It did not own the patent.

“Some people say” now lives everywhere: cable panels, talk radio, podcasts, political YouTube, newsletters, social posts, workplace conversations, and group chats. The sentence creates an invisible constituency behind whatever comes next.

Some people say the policy is unconstitutional.

How many? Which people? What part? Based on what?

The phrase arrives with the authority of public concern and none of the inconvenience of a source.

The new capability was synthetic consensus. Opinion could be introduced as a report about the public.

### 2005: A person becomes a symbol

Terri Schiavo had been in a severely brain-damaged state for years while her husband and parents fought over her care and the removal of her feeding tube.

The case involved difficult questions about medical evidence, consciousness, prior wishes, family authority, disability, religion, end-of-life care, state law, and the courts.

Then the national machinery took possession.

Congress passed special legislation giving a federal court jurisdiction to review claims by Schiavo’s parents. President George W. Bush returned to Washington to sign it. Political leaders issued statements. Religious groups mobilized. Cable television ran images and commentary around the clock.

I was working inside news production while this happened. I had already read *Manufacturing Consent*. Schiavo was not my discovery that institutions shape a story. She was confirmation from the control room.

A later study reviewed 1,141 newspaper articles and found that medical explanation of persistent vegetative state was rare, charged language was common, and coverage contained inaccuracies. The [study’s numbers](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18685135/) do not decide the moral and legal dispute. They show what happened when a medical condition became a national symbol faster than it became publicly understood.

Schiavo stopped being allowed to exist only as a person. She became life, death, family, government, religion, disability, cruelty, compassion, and judicial tyranny, depending on the package.

The new capability was symbolic extraction.

A human being could be stripped down into an identity object owned by political groups, broadcasters, and audiences.

### 2008–2011: The unsupported identity claim

Claims that Barack Obama had been born outside the United States circulated in anonymous emails during the 2008 campaign. Hawaii officials verified his birth record. His campaign posted a standard certificate. The claim continued.

Donald Trump gave birtherism a celebrity promoter in 2011. Obama released the long-form certificate. The claim continued.

[FactCheck.org’s chronology](https://www.factcheck.org/2016/09/trump-on-birtherism-wrong-and-wrong/) matters because it separates evidence from later mythology about who started the rumor and what the documents established.

Birtherism survived because its job was larger than establishing a birthplace. Repeating the question marked Obama as foreign, fraudulent, and outside the legitimate nation. Asking became a club signal.

Disproof could not kill a claim whose social function did not depend on proof.

The new capability was the self-sealing identity allegation. The claim remained useful after being disproven because disbelief in the disproof was part of the membership.

### 2015–2016: The politician becomes the program

Donald Trump did not enter politics as a normal politician who happened to be famous.

He had decades of television appearances, tabloid coverage, interviews, cameos, professional-wrestling performances, and *The Apprentice*. The television version of Trump was a decisive billionaire boss even where the underlying business history was more complicated. [Research summarized by the Annenberg School](https://www.asc.upenn.edu/news-events/news/politics-entertainment-media-how-apprentice-helped-trump-2016) found that the program’s audience relationship contributed to his 2016 primary support.

Trump understood attention as a medium.

He could generate an event that supportive media celebrated and hostile media felt compelled to cover. He could force opponents into permanent reaction. Every condemnation returned him to the center. Every panel about whether he had gone too far extended the episode.

Not every controversy needed to be planned. A person trained by television can produce television by instinct.

The new capability was total programming. The politician became host, show, antagonist, protagonist, and schedule.

### 2016: I return from Vietnam

I lived in Vietnam from 2014 to 2016. When I flew back to California shortly before the election, the country felt like an alternate dimension.

America had not been normal when I left. I had watched the machinery for nearly twenty years. The threshold had changed while I was gone.

Everybody seemed to have joined a club.

Politics had escaped the news department. It lived in entertainment, food, clothing, dating, friendship, work, comedy, and the phrases people used to introduce themselves. Ordinary preferences arrived with inferred positions attached.

The older system asked which program I watched.

The new system wanted to know what kind of person I was.

### 2020: The pandemic merger

COVID merged government, medicine, media, platforms, employers, schools, families, bodies, and political identity into one distribution system.

The virus was real. Uncertainty was real. Evidence changed as researchers learned. Policies had costs. Institutions made mistakes. People needed usable guidance before perfect knowledge was available.

The factory converted that mess into two complete people.

Responsible people trusted the package.

Independent people rejected the package.

Masks, vaccines, closures, proximity, risk tolerance, employment, school attendance, and trust became political signals. [Pew documented growing partisan gaps](https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2020/06/03/partisan-differences-over-the-pandemic-response-are-growing/) in threat perception, precautions, and trust in public-health officials. Other studies connected the widening differences to partisan cues.

No single sentence resolves every COVID argument. That is precisely why the identity packages were so destructive. A person could support vaccination and oppose a mandate. A person could think closures were initially reasonable and later excessive. A person could wear a mask around an elderly relative without treating bare faces as moral confession.

The system had no shelf for combinations.

The new capability was physical subscription. Political identity could be worn on the face, enforced at the door, and argued through the body.

### Summer 2020 and January 6: Vocabulary becomes team property

The protests and unrest after George Floyd’s murder were not the same event as the January 6 attack on the Capitol.

Their causes, targets, scale, participants, organization, geography, and legal consequences differed.

The useful comparison is vocabulary.

Protest. Riot. Unrest. Uprising. Insurrection. Terrorism. Mostly peaceful. Isolated bad actors. Law and order. Systemic danger. Existential threat.

Each team selected the words that preserved its preferred meaning. Property destruction could become the defining truth or a distraction from police violence. Violence at the Capitol could become an attack on constitutional government or a disorderly event inflated to criminalize a movement.

Evidence can justify different words in different cases. The problem begins when the approved label arrives before the inquiry and controls which evidence may count.

[Pew found large partisan differences](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/01/04/a-look-back-at-americans-reactions-to-the-jan-6-riot-at-the-u-s-capitol/) in reactions to January 6. Research on 2020 coverage found different emphasis across partisan outlets on police brutality, peaceful demonstration, property destruction, and law and order.

The new capability was vocabulary ownership.

The word became a jersey before it became a description.

### Iran versus Djibouti

Those are not trivia. They are part of the infrastructure behind military operations, shipping security, regional surveillance, and American power.

The country appears less often because infrastructure is harder to sell than an enemy.

## The Latest Shipment: Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence is a technology.

They, Inc. sells it as a personality test.

The underlying labor questions are real. The International Labour Organization estimated in 2025 that [one in four workers worldwide](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) had some occupational exposure to generative AI. It placed 3.3 percent of global employment in the highest exposure category. The report emphasized transformation of work more often than simple wholesale replacement.

Those numbers are not permission to ignore displacement. Some people will lose tasks, leverage, income, or jobs. Some employers will use software to deskill work, increase monitoring, or keep the benefit of productivity gains. Some companies will sell inflated claims and unreliable systems.

The technology also has real value. It can assist research, programming, accessibility, translation, creative work, production, and the tedious parts of verification. I use it.

The factual labor question is:

> Which tasks change, who gains, who loses, who owns the system, and whether workers share the benefit?

The factoid versions are:

> AI destroys all work.

or:

> AI liberates everyone from work.

Both packages remove the workers, firms, tasks, contracts, industries, timelines, and distribution of gains.

The infrastructure questions are equally real.

Berkeley Lab estimated that data centers used about [4.4 percent of U.S. electricity in 2023](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2025/01/15/berkeley-lab-report-evaluates-increase-in-electricity-demand-from-data-centers/) and projected 6.7 to 12 percent by 2028. Data centers can strain grids, require transmission, consume land, and compete for water.

There is no single water number for “an AI prompt.” A 2025 Berkeley Lab study found [workload-level water use varied by more than 10,000-fold](https://seta.lbl.gov/publications/water-use-data-center-workloads). Server efficiency, utilization, cooling design, grid water intensity, climate, and facility operation all mattered.

The real environmental question is which facility, using which cooling system, drawing from which water source, on which grid, under which permit.

The factoid version is that every chatbot response drains a lake.

The National Parks Conservation Association published a 2026 report warning about possible impacts from rapid data-center expansion near Mid-Atlantic parks. That is a specific advocacy argument about identified projects, parks, land, water, energy, transmission, and air quality. It deserves project-level evidence and regulatory review.

They, Inc. removes the project.

One team receives:

> AI steals jobs, destroys the environment, belongs to predatory billionaires, and helps bad politicians fire workers. Using it marks you as one of them.

The rival team receives:

> AI eliminates waste, exposes useless bureaucrats, and makes institutions efficient. Opposing it marks you as a frightened incumbent protecting obsolete work.

Both contain pieces that can be true.

Neither requires the customer to understand a model, a job, a cooling system, a permit, or the tool in front of them.

Elon Musk makes the identity transfer easy to see. Electric cars once fit neatly inside a progressive climate-and-innovation package. Musk’s public rightward turn and political association with Trump helped turn Tesla, X, SpaceX, and AI into partisan objects. In early 2025, [Pew found](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/02/19/how-americans-view-elon-musk-and-mark-zuckerberg/) that 73 percent of Republicans viewed Musk favorably, compared with 12 percent of Democrats.

The executive became a club flag. The products inherited the signal.

I can use a tool without joining the chief executive’s political party, defending the company that made it, or accepting the rival group’s complete explanation of what the tool means.

I use the thing.

I criticize the thing.

I refuse both membership packages.

## How I Research Without Moving Into the Factory

When a subject catches my attention, I look it up.

I do not subscribe to a permanent feed and call the resulting mood knowledge.

I start with the claim. What, exactly, is supposed to have happened? I search the language in the claim. I sort by date. I look for the earliest recoverable account. I find the official record, recording, study, court document, press conference, photograph, or data when one exists.

Then I scan different outlets for facts that remain stable across the packages.

I count origins, not copies.

Twenty sites repeating the same wire report are one origin. Five accounts reposting one clipped video are one clip. A hundred headlines based on one anonymous statement do not become a hundred confirmations.

I separate the visible evidence from the attached story.

The video shows a person doing something.

The caption identifies the person, place, date, motive, and consequence.

Those are separate claims.

I look at what each source can know. A government knows what it decided and may misrepresent why. A company knows its product and may exaggerate its value. A witness knows what was visible from one position. A study knows what its design can measure. A commentator may know the history and still be selling a conclusion.

The goal is not perfect certainty. The goal is to preserve uncertainty where uncertainty exists.

That habit sounds exhausting because the feed sends fifty emergencies a day.

I do not investigate fifty emergencies a day.

Most of them receive the other boundary.

## “I Don’t Care” Is a Complete Sentence

People hear “I don’t care” as a claim about the importance of the subject.

That is usually not what I mean.

I mean I am not donating my attention to this delivery.

I may not care which politician produced today’s stupid remark. I may not care what a celebrity’s facial expression proves. I may not care which audience won the morning’s interpretation contest. I may care about the underlying war, law, person, or technology and still refuse the package in front of me.

Attention is finite.

I have work, friends, articles, images, projects, and ordinary life. A stranger’s emergency does not automatically outrank them because an algorithm or coworker brought it into the room.

“I don’t care” is a boundary around my attention.

“That’s bullshit” is a boundary around the claim.

I use the second when:

- the source has disappeared into anonymous authority;
- the claim is broader than the evidence;
- the conclusion performs more work than the facts;
- the practical details are missing;
- the causal chain has been replaced by a villain;
- the speaker is delivering a team product as useful information.

The phrase does not mean I already know the complete truth.

It means the package has failed inspection.

## Stop Working for Free

They, Inc. relies on unpaid labor.

People carry a claim from a screen into a lunchroom, family dinner, group chat, job site, date, or friendship. Then they ask everyone else to unpack it, verify it, absorb its urgency, and declare a team.

The way out is boring and practical:

- Name the source instead of saying “they.”
- State what the source actually observed, measured, decided, or alleged.
- Keep the date, place, population, product, and scale attached.
- Separate the record from your explanation of the record.
- Say what remains uncertain and what evidence would change your mind.
- Correct the claim for the same people who received it.
- Do not turn disagreement into a diagnosis of somebody’s hidden allegiance.
- Let people decline a political delivery without converting that boundary into a confession.

None of this requires political neutrality. It requires responsibility for the package you hand to another person.

If the claim survives those steps, we can talk about it. If it does not, I do not owe the distributor a half-hour reconstruction of the factory that made it.
