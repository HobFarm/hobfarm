People give me ideas for articles. They rarely give me ideas for getting those articles in front of people.

“You should write about sharks” supplies a noun. It does not supply a thesis, structure, visual system, source list, audience route, or reason another shark article needs to exist. Discovery already has Shark Week. The shark is occupied.

The suggestion became useful when the animal revealed a factory.

The finished article, [*Sharksploitation: The Shark Never Had to Come Ashore*](/articles/sharksploitation/), follows one biological animal through a century of human machinery. Newspapers turn it into panic. Exploitation films turn it into spectacle. *Jaws* turns it into an event. Franchises turn the event into inventory. Tracking software turns a real shark into a named character with a profile and an audience waiting for its next ping.

That connection is the article. Sharks were only the entrance.

The audience route grows from the same decision. A reader who opens for sharks can leave with a way to think about movies, media systems, and independent production. A reader who arrives for film history can still follow a real animal back into the water. Distribution is not a slogan pasted onto the finished piece. It begins with giving several kinds of curious reader a reason to enter the same door.

## The article starts as a mess

The first version of an article is not a clean prompt. It is a source field.

Mine can contain a memory of a movie scene, a photograph I forgot I had, three links, a bad joke, a claim that turns out to be false, a useful sentence, an observation from Las Vegas, and an association that makes no sense until it does. Some items are evidence. Some are questions. Some are bait for a route that never opens.

I type a lot of shit into a box.

The box is useful because it can hold the mess long enough for me to look across it. It can group notes, compare possible structures, find missing receipts, draft a transition, and point out that two things I placed beside each other do not actually connect. It can also invent the receipt, polish the weak connection, and make the wrong route sound finished.

So the first job is preservation, not publication. I want the field intact before I decide which part deserves a road through it.

## Follow the shark

The shark route began to show itself in stages.

The 1916 attacks along the New Jersey coast and in Matawan Creek supplied the panic. *Mondo Cane* supplied documentary authority turning visible fragments into a moral story. American International Pictures and Roger Corman supplied a low-budget industrial logic: strong title, legible concept, controlled exposure. Universal scaled related logic around a bestselling novel, a young director, a difficult ocean shoot, television advertising, and wide release. *Star Wars* extended the event into merchandise and durable franchise assets. Contemporary independent filmmakers brought the argument back to cheap audience proof. Commercial fishing reversed the monster relationship. White-shark telemetry brought the article back to an actual animal near Atlantic Canada.

The shark mostly remained a shark. Humans kept changing the narration, technology, commerce, and delivery system around it.

## A good detour comes back

I think about this structure through the Grateful Dead’s “Dark Star.” A recognizable theme appears, leaves for an extended improvisational journey, discovers material that was not visible at the beginning, and eventually returns. The return makes the wandering legible.

An article can leave its apparent subject for a long time. The detour earns its space when it comes back carrying evidence, context, or a stronger interpretation.

The *Sharksploitation* route leaves the animal for roadshow films, Italian shockumentaries, studio economics, merchandise, and independent financing. It returns to a tagged shark with a name and a public tracker. “Follow the shark” has become literal again, but the reader now understands the tracker as another distribution system.

A detour that cannot return is not automatically bad. It may be another article trying to form.

## Route, branch, or cut

The *Mondo Cane* research made the sorting problem obvious.

The shark sequence stayed because it performs a specific job. The visible fragments—bones underwater, beach labor, injured men, drying fins, a captured animal—do not independently establish the account delivered by the English narration. Montage, reaction shots, music, and voice-over create continuity, motive, duration, and judgment. That is useful inside an article about the systems that turn sharks into spectacle.

The research also opened a much larger field: Gualtiero Jacopetti, Paolo Cavara, Franco Prosperi, *Africa Addio*, staged and reconstructed reality, colonial framing, political controversy, later shockumentaries, *Faces of Death*, VHS gore culture, and online feeds that deliver the same mixture of evidence and performance without a theater.

That field has its own center. Forcing it all into the shark article would not make the article richer. It would make the shark disappear.

## The article becomes a finished object

The original suggestion eventually became a 2,561-word illustrated feature with sourced history, archival material, custom graphics, factual checks, citations, revisions, page construction, and publication review.

That list is deliberately broad. Readers do not need a wiring diagram of the workshop to understand that an article is more than prose. A newspaper image needs provenance and a caption. A changing box-office figure needs a date. An uncertain shark location needs uncertainty. A generated hero needs art direction. A diagram needs to work on a phone. A related link needs a real reason to be there.

The article becomes publishable when those parts agree about what the object is doing.

## Then I remembered Sharknado

After writing 2,561 words about sharks being converted into entertainment products, I somehow remembered that I had forgotten *Sharknado*.

This is funny because *Sharknado* is not hiding. The series released six films on Syfy between 2013 and 2018. It moved from Los Angeles to New York, the East Coast, space, a global circuit, and finally time travel. Each sequel learned that the premise could absorb more locations, effects, cameos, self-reference, and franchise memory. By the second film, the audience understood that the production understood the joke too.[^sharknado-series]

The original did not begin as a guaranteed phenomenon. Contemporary reporting put its audience around 1.4 million for the first airing, while conversation on Twitter became the real event. The sequel drew 3.9 million viewers, and Syfy’s marketing operation treated viewers as participants who could circulate clips, use apps, host watch parties, and even help name the film.[^sharknado-social]

The Asylum already had a system built for high-concept, market-driven films made and delivered quickly. *Sharknado* supplied the proof that one of those concepts could become a live social object, then a functioning franchise.

As of August 4, 2026, six films have been released. A seventh project, *Sharknado Origins*, has been announced as a prequel about teenage versions of Fin and April. It was planned for summer 2026, but I could not verify a completed release or firm date. The branch is active. It is not finished.[^sharknado-origins]

That discovery does not require reopening the entire shark article. *Sharknado* could become a short addition, a separate article about The Asylum and social television, or both.

## The ocean becomes optional

<SharknadoPoster />

The poster explains why the branch belongs near *Sharksploitation*. It contains the complete sale before the film has to move: sharks, tornado, disaster, title. The shark is now a modular media object. It can be placed inside weather, geography, space, time travel, or whatever the next poster can communicate immediately.

*Sharknado* is sharksploitation after the shark no longer requires an ocean, plausible behavior, or gravity.

The shark never had to come ashore. By *Sharknado*, the ocean had become an unnecessary production expense.

## The box is only the terminal

AI participates in this process. It is neither a passive hammer nor an autonomous author hiding behind my byline.

It can preserve a large source field, organize notes, compare structures, locate missing information, challenge assumptions, analyze an image, draft prose, and translate an editorial decision into a page or graphic. Its proposals affect which routes become visible. Sometimes it finds the sentence that joins two fields. Sometimes its confidence makes a false bridge look sturdier than it is.

It also forgets context, flattens peculiar ideas into common ones, overexplains, invents facts, and turns uncertainty into confident filler. I remain responsible for recognizing the subject, choosing the route, supplying experience and source material, checking the receipts, cutting weak associations, selecting images, revising the language, and deciding what becomes public.

The box is the interface. The process is everything connected to it.

The related Workshop Note [*The Card Catalog Started Talking Back*](/articles/the-card-catalog-started-talking-back/) deals more directly with that division of labor. [*How to Fix Slop*](/articles/how-to-fix-slop/) explains why memory, roles, and structure matter when the work needs continuity. This article is the visible example: one noun enters, one route survives, several branches remain available.

## I keep building the same machine

A node is a bounded piece of work with a recognizable purpose and usable output. An article can be a node. So can a source field, character, visual experiment, software component, published page, or unresolved article seed.

The mesh is made from the useful routes between them.

[Other Alice](/presents/other-alice-adventures/) preserves a world that can support stories, characters, places, images, and play. [The Grimoire](/grimoire/) preserves reviewed meaning and supplies bounded material to Wonder Machine and StyleFusion. [Workshop Notes](/workshop/workshop-notes/) preserve public explanations of methods that produced something worth showing. The objects differ. The instinct repeats: preserve the source, select the relevant context, divide responsibility, make an artifact, retain the result, and leave useful routes open.

<NodeMesh />

Publication adds another node. It does not seal the object.

A finished article can receive a correction, image, new source, reciprocal link, supporting video, or later interpretation. [*Hey, It’s That Guy*](/articles/hey-its-that-guy/) and [*I May Have Inspired Stephen King’s It*](/articles/i-may-have-inspired-it/) connect through Bangor, screen history, memory, and local geography without needing to behave like consecutive episodes.

A series asks what happens next. A mesh asks which connection becomes useful next.

## The next article has no message

This article has now performed the method it describes. It began with article-making, followed *Sharksploitation* into *Mondo Cane*, remembered *Sharknado*, opened The Asylum and social television, moved through the HobFarm mesh, and returned to article-making.

The next source field begins with Salvador Dalí on *The Dick Cavett Show*, saying his paintings contain no message. Around that statement are the public character, hypnagogic images, geometry in nature, early paintings, magazines, advertising, and pictures that refuse to reduce themselves to neat verbal explanations.

The Sahara and Chad tried to follow. They have been routed elsewhere.

The shark article began when someone supplied a subject. It became an article when the subject found a route through everything around it. It became a node when *Sharknado* arrived after the build and opened another path.

I already know the Dalí article is going to wander.
