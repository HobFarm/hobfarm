In December 1985, Playboy ran a future-tech spread called **"Hold On, It's Comin',"** framed as a sneak peek at the "electronic drawing board." The piece joked about a fictional Sony "Crystal Ballman," then did what old magazine futurism does best: it mixed plausible engineering, consumer fantasy, trade-show wishcasting, and horny gadget logic into one glossy prediction pile.

The predictions are fantastic because they are not simply wrong. They are wrong in revealing ways.

Playboy imagined a domestic robot with plastic skin and ROM commands from a compact disc. It imagined television goggles with stereo sound, 3-D viewing, and a giant private screen. It imagined a wearable satellite dish hat, because apparently the future was going to make every man look like a cable installer receiving orders from Saturn. It imagined dashboard navigation, infrared night driving, and something close to automatic pilot.

Looking back from 2026, the pattern is obvious: Playboy mostly got the **interfaces** right, but guessed the wrong **containers**.

The future did not arrive as a CD-ROM robot girlfriend, TV mask, satellite hat, and arcade dashboard cockpit. It arrived as phones, cloud maps, robot vacuums, FPV drones, smart glasses, cheap sensors, software subscriptions, and cars slowly absorbing the driver.

## Company trail: who survived, who shifted, who became a logo

**Sony** survives, but not as the imaginary "Crystal Ballman" company Playboy joked about. Its actual path went through Walkman-era portable electronics, camcorders, TVs, PlayStation, image sensors, music, movies, and VR. Sony later made real head-mounted displays like Glasstron, then VR returned through PlayStation VR and PS VR2. The 1985 TV-goggles instinct was not crazy. It was just waiting for smaller displays, better graphics, cheaper sensors, and a reason people would tolerate wearing the thing.

**Philips** is still around, but the old consumer-electronics giant changed shape. The company that once sat near car navigation prototypes later became much more focused on health technology. Its lighting business became Signify, and Philips sold its domestic-appliances business. The name still floats around consumer life, but the corporate center of gravity moved away from the everything-electronics department-store model.

**Blaupunkt** is the cleanest corporate ghost story in the article. In the 1980s, Blaupunkt was still a serious car-audio and car-navigation name connected to Bosch. The EVA navigation work was real. Later, Bosch sold the Blaupunkt brand to Aurelius. The original company withered, the brand became a licensing object, and eventually the Blaupunkt name kept traveling as a badge on products rather than as the same engineering force behind the dashboard future.

**G.M.** also survived, obviously, but the "automatic pilot" thread became much uglier than a dashboard button. GM built Super Cruise, invested heavily in Cruise, then decided not to keep funding Cruise's robotaxi development and refocused on advanced driver assistance and autonomous personal vehicles. That is the modern lesson: automatic driving was not a feature. It was a legal, operational, software, mapping, sensor, insurance, and public-trust swamp.

## 1. The robot doll: the fantasy came back, but the useful robot was a trash can

The Playboy copy says most domestic robots of the time looked like "high-tech trash receptacles." This is funnier now because the home robot that actually won looked almost exactly like that.

Roomba launched in 2002 as a round automatic floor cleaner. It did not have skin, personality, flirtation, or a compact disc full of commands. It bumped into furniture, avoided stairs, followed walls, and ate dust under the couch. It was not the robot from the centerfold future. It was a small, stubborn appliance with just enough intelligence to do one boring job.

That became the real domestic robot lineage: vacuum, mop, lawnmower, pool cleaner. Narrow task. Cheap enough. Dumb enough to work.

The humanoid companion fantasy took a different road. Realistic synthetic dolls became an industry later, and companion robotics kept trying to add animatronics, voice, AI, facial expression, warmth, and presence. Playboy's "ROM commands from a compact disk" sounds primitive, but the basic idea is recognizable: body plus programmed behavior plus the illusion of personality.

That is where Moya becomes interesting. Moya, from Shanghai startup DroidUp/Zhuoyide, is a 2026 lifelike humanoid robot described in reports as having expressive facial movement, eye contact, warm synthetic skin around human body temperature, and a company-claimed 92 percent humanlike walking metric. It has also been reported around the $173,000 range, which puts it nowhere near the kitchen of a normal household.

That price and the walking metric should stay framed as reported launch/demo claims, not as mature independent benchmarks. Moya looks like the magazine painting escaped the page, but with a launch-demo price tag and a cloud-age press kit.

The verdict: Playboy was aesthetically prophetic and practically wrong. The robot that entered the home was not "android companion follows you anywhere." It was "round plastic idiot eats dust while you forget it exists."

## 2. TV goggles: Sony built the awkward bridge, FPV drones found the clean use case

Playboy's "TVG" prediction was television goggles with stereo sound, 3-D viewing, and a huge virtual screen. That is not a bad read. It points straight toward head-mounted displays.

Sony later sold products like the Glasstron. In 1998, Sony announced the PC Glasstron PLM-S700 as a personal LCD monitor with tiny displays, stereo headphones, and the virtual experience of watching a 30-inch screen from about 1.2 meters away. It was real, expensive, and extremely "future of 1998."

The category struggled for decades because the idea was not enough. Displays were too crude. Batteries were too weak. Cameras were awkward. Networks were slow. The field of view was limited. The software was thin. The social penalty was enormous. Google Glass later proved that wearing a face camera in public could turn you into a walking privacy argument before the utility was strong enough to defend itself.

That is why FPV drones are a better modern comparison for this specific 1985 image than generic VR. FPV gear gives the goggles a job. You are not just wearing a private TV. You are seeing from a machine.

The goggles become a cockpit. The controller becomes the dashboard. The drone becomes the remote body. The camera becomes your eye. It is the 1985 fantasy finally wired to a use case that makes sense.

Sony's own branch continued too. PlayStation VR and PS VR2 kept the head-mounted display alive in gaming, with better screens, tracking, controllers, and graphics. But the mass-culture breakthrough still remains slippery. VR keeps surviving. AR keeps trying again. FPV drones are one of the places where goggles stopped looking like a gimmick and started looking like an instrument.

The verdict: Playboy imagined TV on your face. The cleaner descendant is not just VR entertainment. It is remote presence: goggles as machine vision.

## 3. Dishmon: the dumbest-looking prediction got the network logic right

The "Dishmon" is the funniest object in the spread: a portable satellite dish worn around the head, paired with a small flat-screen TV. In theory, you could make video calls on the run or tilt your head to catch the latest episode of *Dynasty*. In practice, nobody wanted to look like a cyberpunk DirecTV installer.

As a product, dead on arrival.

As an infrastructure prediction, not bad.

Small satellite dishes became normal. GPS became invisible. Mobile video calls became ordinary. Streaming video became ordinary. Navigation became ordinary. Satellite broadband became part of the conversation again. The signal logic was right. The body-worn object was ridiculous.

This is one of the great rules of bad futurism: when people do not know where the infrastructure will go, they draw it on the user's body.

Dish on head. Camera on face. Computer on belt. Phone on wrist. Screen in goggles. Keyboard in jacket. The more primitive the future looks, the more equipment the human has to visibly carry.

The real future hides the antenna. It hides the map. It hides the routing. It hides the compression. It hides the satellite. Then it hands you a rectangle and tells you the magic was always obvious.

The modern smart-glasses version is much less humiliating. Meta's Ray-Ban Display line puts a small private display, camera, microphones, speakers, AI, live captions, translation, messages, calls, and wristband control into a glasses format. Newer cheaper AI glasses push parts of that experience lower in price, often without the display. The point is not that the exact device has won yet. The point is that the Dishmon's job description survived after the dish disappeared.

The verdict: the Dishmon is a fossil version of the smartphone plus smart glasses plus satellite/cellular network logic. The object is stupid. The network instinct is right.

## 4. Dashboard navigation: this was the clean hit

Playboy's dashboard navigation prediction was the most accurate part of the spread because it was already close to real engineering. The article mentions Philips and Blaupunkt prototypes, dashboard digitization, rooftop antennas, navigational satellites, coordinate displays, CD-based maps, road-condition alerts, rest stops, and infrared night-driving screens.

That is not fantasy. That is a preview of the modern navigation stack.

Philips CARIN and Bosch-Blaupunkt EVA were part of the real road to in-car navigation: map databases, route-search software, vehicle-position estimation, voice guidance, arrows, diagrams, and visual displays. The basic idea was already there. Store the map. Locate the car. Compute the route. Tell the driver where to go.

Then the stack evolved.

CD-ROM maps became DVD maps. Built-in GPS became mainstream. Voice directions became normal. Live traffic arrived. Phones invaded the dashboard. Cloud maps ate the standalone navigation unit. The car screen fought the phone screen and often lost.

The only thing Playboy missed is that the winning dashboard screen would spend years being a phone suction-cupped to the windshield like a parasite.

From there, navigation turned into driver assistance. The question changed from "Where am I?" to "What lane should I be in?" Then "Can the car keep the lane?" Then "Can the car change lanes?" Then "Can the car drive me across town?" Then "Who is responsible when the car does something stupid?"

The verdict: Playboy spotted the birth of the modern navigation stack. This one aged less like a joke and more like a trade-show preview.

## 5. Infrared night driving: real product, weak destiny

The infrared night-driving screen also came true. Cadillac offered Night Vision on the 2000 DeVille, using infrared imaging to help spot people, animals, and vehicles beyond normal headlight range. It was expensive, futuristic, and exactly the kind of feature that made sense in a luxury-car brochure.

It was also not destiny.

This is a classic future-tech failure mode: the product exists, works, and still does not become culturally important.

Sometimes the future does not fail because it is fake. It fails because the use case is narrow, the price is high, the interface is weird, and the benefit is not strong enough to force adoption. Infrared night driving sounds amazing. Most people still just drove with headlights and hoped the deer made better choices.

Today, IR and thermal sensing make more sense as machine perception than driver spectacle. Robots, cars, industrial systems, security systems, and drones can use non-visible sensing as part of a larger perception stack. The driver does not need a sci-fi screen if the machine is already interpreting the world.

The verdict: real product, weak destiny. It was not wrong. It just did not become the main character.

## 6. Automatic pilot: GM saw it, Tesla sold it louder, Waymo made it boring

The Playboy article ends with the rumor that GM was working on something close to automatic pilot.

That line aged into the ugliest and most interesting branch of the whole spread.

GM did build serious driver-assistance technology. Super Cruise became a hands-free driver assistance system for mapped roads. GM also backed Cruise in the robotaxi race, then pulled back. In late 2024, GM said it would stop funding Cruise robotaxi development and refocus its autonomous work on advanced driver assistance and personal autonomous vehicles.

That is a huge tell. The dream did not disappear. The business model cracked.

Robotaxis are not just self-driving software. They are fleet operations, service areas, maintenance, cleaning, remote assistance, local regulation, insurance, rider support, emergency behavior, weird construction zones, weather, public trust, and every edge case a city can throw at a sensor stack.

Tesla is the loud version of the dream. Its robotaxi service began in Austin in 2025 as a limited rollout, and by 2026 Tesla was pushing unsupervised service in Austin while still operating at a much smaller scale than Waymo in that market. Waymo, meanwhile, has taken the more methodical, sensor-heavy, heavily mapped path and built a larger real-world robotaxi footprint, while still dealing with recalls, operational limits, and the usual machine-driving weirdness.

The 1985 fantasy made it sound like a feature. Push button. Car drives.

The real thing is a city-scale argument.

Can the car see enough? Can it predict enough? Can it handle construction? Can it understand cops, cones, emergency scenes, flooded roads, cyclists, pedestrians, debris, and people being strange in ways no training set fully captures? Can it fail safely? Can it explain itself? Who gets sued? Who updates the software? Who watches the watchers?

The verdict: Playboy's "automatic pilot" happened, but not as a clean invention. It became one of the most expensive software problems in public life.

## The real then-vs-now take

This spread is not funny because it was wrong. It is funny because it drew the future as **objects**.

A robot woman.

A television mask.

A satellite hat.

A glowing car cockpit.

The real future came as **systems**.

Phones ate portable TV, video calls, cameras, music, maps, address books, and boredom.

Cloud maps ate CD-ROM navigation.

Flat screens won, 3-D TV mostly fizzled, VR survived as a niche, and AR keeps trying to sneak into normal glasses.

FPV drones gave goggles a purpose that living-room video never quite did.

Robots succeeded first by being appliances, not people.

Self-driving moved from "automatic pilot" fantasy into an engineering, liability, regulatory, and public-trust swamp.

The sharpest lesson is that old futurism often imagined the right desire and the wrong object.

People did want private screens. They did want portable video. They did want video calls. They did want navigation. They did want machines that did chores. They did want cars that reduced the burden of driving. They did want computers that felt less like desks and more like companions.

But the future rarely arrives as one heroic gadget. It arrives as a stack.

Sensors get cheaper. Displays get smaller. Batteries improve. Networks spread. Software eats categories. Interfaces shrink. Infrastructure disappears. The object gets less theatrical, then becomes normal.

That is why the Dishmon looks ridiculous and the smartphone does not, even though the smartphone is the more magical device. The hat shows the machinery. The phone hides it.

And the most Playboy thing about the whole spread is that the humanoid robot fantasy is coming back now, not because it is the most useful robot shape, but because AI has revived the old dream that the machine should look at you, talk to you, smile warmly, and make the future feel like it has a face.

Forty years later, the Crystal Ballman still does not exist.

But the future did show up.

It just had better product design.
