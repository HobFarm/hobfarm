A few days after my [Wavy Gravy article](/articles/before-wavy-gravy-was-ice-cream/) went live, I watched Netflix's new documentary series *Conversations with a Killer: The Charles Manson Tapes*. The murders were not the part that stuck with me. The family was.

Former Manson Family member Dianne Lake described the life she had before Manson. Her parents had already dismantled their conventional suburban life, traded their house for a trailer, and moved the family deeper into the counterculture. By the time Lake was fourteen, her parents were living at the Hog Farm. She left them and entered another group that called itself a family.

My reaction was not another revelation about Charles Manson. Netflix has that covered.

It was: **regular families actually did this?**

A family could look at ordinary American life in the late 1960s, sell everything, load the children into a truck, and head toward a communal experiment. The decision was extreme, but it occurred inside a real movement. Thousands of new communities were appearing. Young people were moving between cities, farms, crash pads, buses, concerts, political groups, spiritual centers, and rural settlements. “Drop out” could describe an actual destination.

I wanted to know where that idea came from, why it became so popular, why so many communes dissolved within a decade, and what happened to the desire underneath them.

The desire is still here. We just stopped asking it to run the whole household.

## The old American habit of starting over

The hippies did not invent the commune. Americans had already spent centuries periodically deciding that society was organized badly and that the solution was to acquire some land and prototype another one.

The Shakers established communal settlements in the eighteenth century. Brook Farm tried to combine shared labor with intellectual and educational life in the 1840s. Oneida reorganized property, marriage, meals, work, and child-rearing around a single religious community. Some experiments were Christian. Others were socialist, abolitionist, pacifist, anarchist, artistic, educational, or built around a particular theory of health and domestic life. Brook Farm ended after financial trouble and a catastrophic fire. Oneida eventually converted into a joint-stock company. Even utopia has a business-development phase.

The recurring proposition was simple: the surrounding society has produced the wrong way to live. We know enough to try another one ourselves.

That proposition never disappeared between the nineteenth-century utopians and the 1960s. Religious communities, Catholic Worker farms, Hutterite colonies, socialist settlements, artist communities, and other collective experiments continued operating. Historian Timothy Miller argues that the new communalism began before “hippie” had fully hardened into a public identity. In that reading, the early communes helped build hippie culture rather than merely receiving it.

The 1960s supplied the scale.

## When dropping out became a mass option

There was no single cause for the commune boom. Several conditions arrived at once.

Postwar prosperity gave many young Americans enough material security to question the system that produced it. Colleges concentrated large numbers of young adults away from their families. The civil-rights movement exposed the distance between American ideals and American life. Vietnam made institutional authority harder to trust. Beat culture had already made rejection of middle-class respectability look possible. Psychedelics, rock music, sexual change, environmental concern, Eastern religions, and an expanding youth press supplied other models of identity and social life.

The commune let people test all of those questions at once.

What should work be for? Who should own the house? Why should every couple reproduce the same private kitchen, lawn mower, television, and mortgage? Who raises the children? What counts as a family? Can art, food, politics, sex, religion, and labor all be reorganized together?

Miller calls the late-1960s and early-1970s surge the greatest wave of communal living in American history. Hundreds of thousands of people participated, and thousands of communities appeared in an enormous range of forms. Some were disciplined and durable. Others were a few people in a farmhouse with a garden, no money, and a rapidly developing argument about the dishes.

The movement also developed its own network effect. Once enough communes existed, joining one stopped sounding like an isolated eccentricity. There were places to go, people who had already gone, underground publications explaining the route, buses moving between scenes, and photographs proving that another life was being attempted somewhere.

That helps explain Dianne Lake's parents. Their decision still seems reckless, especially in what it asked of their children. It was not an incomprehensible act performed outside history. They were responding to a visible social movement that told ordinary people the entire structure of ordinary life could be replaced.

## A few years after reinventing society, people wanted a bedroom

The commune scene in *Easy Rider* is useful because the movie does not stop to explain what a commune is. By 1969, the audience could recognize it as one possible destination on the road out of conventional America.

The scene was inspired by New Buffalo, a real commune founded near Taos in 1967. Dennis Hopper wanted to film there, but the residents declined, so the production recreated the settlement elsewhere. New Buffalo lasted into the late 1970s, long enough for its internal history to reveal what happened across much of the movement.

When former resident Iris Keltz returned after about a year away, most of the people she had known were gone. Some had formed stable relationships and wanted nuclear-family lives. Some wanted privacy. Some found a profession or craft that pulled them elsewhere. Some got lost in drugs. None of those explanations requires a grand ideological defeat. People changed. Couples wanted their own space. Children altered the calculation. A social arrangement that felt liberating at twenty-three could feel exhausting at twenty-nine.

The classic commune bundled nearly everything:

shared property, shared housing, shared labor, shared meals, shared money, shared childcare, shared social life, shared ideology, and sometimes shared authority over intimate relationships.

That is a lot of dependencies inside one system.

<CommuneUnbundled />

The Farm in Tennessee survived by confronting the financial version of the same problem. Around its 1983 “Changeover,” the community was bringing in roughly $6,000 a week while spending about $10,000. It had debt, weak financial oversight, expensive shared obligations, and businesses that were not covering the system built around them. The response included budgets, a board, individual household contributions, outside employment, and businesses expected to function as businesses. The debt was eliminated within several years.

The commune that survives eventually discovers accounting.

Twin Oaks, founded in Virginia in 1967, represents another durable answer. It still shares income and substantial resources, but it operates through explicit governance, labor expectations, membership procedures, managers, planners, businesses, and private rooms. Members work a defined weekly quota, while the community provides housing, food, healthcare, and other needs. There is no single central leader. The arrangement preserves collective life by making its rules legible.

The permanent experiments that lasted generally became less casual, not more. They learned that goodwill does not schedule the kitchen, allocate healthcare, settle land ownership, or remove a dangerous member. Community requires infrastructure.

It also requires boundaries.

I know the opposite failure mode. Before the house I live in now, I spent eight years in another house and never knew my neighbors. I do not think they knew one another either. Modern private life can solve the problem of everybody being up each other's ass so thoroughly that people live fifty feet apart for years without learning each other's names.

The interesting design problem sits between those extremes: community without compulsory intimacy.

## The culture escaped the commune

By the mid-1970s, the largest wave had receded. The political moment changed, people aged, families formed, money ran out, and the practical load of permanent communal life became harder to ignore. Yet many of the underlying practices escaped into the broader culture.

The Vermont Historical Society traces former commune activity into food co-ops, farmers markets, community gardens, alternative schools, daycare networks, renewable-energy work, art collectives, and rural lives that retained parts of the original counterculture without preserving the total communal structure.

Taos makes this evolution unusually visible. New Buffalo belonged to the original commune wave. Lama Foundation survives as an intentional spiritual community. The Greater World Earthship Community preserves another branch of the experiment: privately owned off-grid homes, a shared greenbelt, common design commitments, and neighbors who deliberately chose the same strange patch of high desert. The residents share a premise. They do not have to share a kitchen.

The Foundation for Intentional Community's current directory contains more than a thousand listings across categories such as ecovillages, cohousing, housing co-ops, communes, community land trusts, and shared spiritual communities. The word *commune* became less common, but intentional community did not vanish. It diversified.

I encountered another surviving branch in Topanga Canyon in 2006. I used to shoot video for a man who held “organic lifestyle” sessions in a fancy house in the hills. A small group attended in person, other people called in, and I recorded the talks. I no longer remember his name. I remember the format.

The commune had become a seminar, a call-in service, a media product, and a canyon lifestyle. The social stream of the 1960s was still running. It had acquired property values and video production.

My earlier article, [*How Psychedelia Went Beige*](/articles/how-psychedelia-went-beige/), follows another part of this evolution through festival graphics, retreat culture, yoga spaces, live painters, visionary art, workshops, vendors, and the visual language that grew around them. This article is about the social architecture underneath that look.

## Build your house for the weekend

My first experience of anything resembling a temporary commune was the Great Went, Phish's 1997 festival at Loring Air Force Base in northern Maine.

I was seventeen. I paid about $75, drove into a decommissioned military base with tens of thousands of other people, camped there, and discovered that a concert could become a settlement. The official ticket price was $70 with camping included, and attendance estimates ran around 65,000 to 70,000.

People built little homes around their cars and tents. The campsite became a neighborhood. You met whoever landed beside you. The music organized the calendar, but the place between the shows carried much of the experience.

By 2009, Phish's Festival 8 in Indio, California, had formalized more of that temporary-city logic. Tickets were $199 plus a $1 charitable donation, with a separate $15 vehicle pass. The site included camping, art, markets, installations, and other attractions around the performances.

Lightning in a Bottle, Gem & Jam, and Serenity Gathering carried the model farther during the early 2010s. Music remained the main draw, but the festival was designed as a place to inhabit and explore.

You arrived and built your house. Your camp became your address. The people next to you became your neighbors for several days. A temporary market sold clothes, art, jewelry, food, and objects that were difficult to find anywhere else. Artists painted in public. Musicians played scheduled sets and impromptu ones. Someone brought a lounge. Someone built a sculpture. Someone juggled because juggling was their thing. People wandered around cleaning the grounds because a cared-for place tends to remain cared for.

Most of the interesting material was not hidden backstage. It was distributed through the town.

Lightning in a Bottle's 2013 program combined music with installations, live painting, yoga, meditation, tea, workshops, and the Temple of Consciousness. A weekend pass was advertised at $240.

This was the festival formula at its most complete: the show supplied the agenda, while the temporary community supplied the memory.

My firsthand knowledge of this festival circuit ends around 2014. From here, the current version has to be checked rather than remembered.

## The commune vacation

The modern versions openly understand themselves as little cities. Lightning in a Bottle describes itself as a community and a “mini city” that attendees help create. Its current 2027 materials preserve significant parts of the model I saw in 2013: camping, participatory art, a marketplace of artisan goods and food, meeting neighbors, contributing to the place, packing out waste, and helping keep the grounds clean. The same event offers ordinary camping, pre-set tents, RV options, boutique accommodations, and hotel packages.

The 2027 guide still uses the Lightning in a Paintcan name, now for more than fifty live painters and a Sunday art walk and silent auction. The 2013 program was then associated with Do Art Foundation; the current guide does not make that same organizational claim.

You can drop out, but there is a hotel package.

I have never attended Burning Man. Friends' accounts gave me the comparison, but the shape of Black Rock City in this article comes from Burning Man Project's current materials.

Those materials describe Black Rock City as a temporary metropolis, not a literal commune. Tens of thousands of people build a city where they live and work for a week, then make it disappear. Camps become temporary homes. Placed theme camps are expected to contribute a service, engagement, art, or another interactive experience, and volunteers help build, operate, and dismantle the city. The Regional Network carries parts of the culture into year-round local events, civic projects, and other community activity. Black Rock City itself remains deliberately temporary.

The people I know who go to Burning Man have regular jobs and regular homes the rest of the year. One friend organizes a large camp, built an art car, plans for most of a year, and spends more than $10,000 making his part of the city happen. Then he returns to ordinary life. That is one person's way of doing Burning Man, not a representative cost.

That sounds less like dropping out than balancing two modes of living.

A few days or a week of constant social contact, shared labor, improvised infrastructure, art, dust, noise, and people doing their thing can feel extraordinary. It can also become old. Eventually your own shower, bed, kitchen, and closed door regain their appeal.

The temporary structure preserves intensity by giving it an ending.

## Temporary civilization is expensive

I stopped doing the festival circuit around 2014 because it had become too expensive for me. I did not have the parking-lot hustle that sustained some touring people. At the Great Went, I realized there was an entire population traveling with Phish. Sometimes they had tickets. Sometimes they did not. They sold food, shirts, jewelry, art, or whatever else could finance the next stretch of road.

The Grateful Dead scene developed that mobile economy, and Phish inherited much of it. In the 1990s, a person could at least imagine paying for part of a tour with grilled-cheese sandwiches, vending, odd jobs, and sleeping in a vehicle.

That road economy is harder for me to imagine now. Touring still means stacking ticket, gas, food, lodging, and parking costs, while vending operates under different financial, legal, and safety conditions.

The festivals themselves face the same problem as the communes: infrastructure costs money.

Burning Man says $775 is the 2026 ticket price that reflects the actual cost of producing one participant's share of the event. A vehicle pass adds $165. Its sample order for two $775 tickets, a vehicle pass, delivery, taxes, and fees totals $1,973.92 before travel, food, shelter, camp dues, costumes, art, or an art car enters the calculation.

The community is real, and the experience is commercialized. People genuinely meet neighbors, build art, clean the grounds, help strangers, form friendships, and make work they would not have made elsewhere. The temporary city also requires permits, insurance, toilets, medical staff, roads, stages, power, labor, security, waste removal, artists, and an organization capable of paying for them.

One descendant of the commune impulse is temporary community. That temporary community now operates inside an experience economy.

I now watch another part of the same ecosystem from the production side at Brooklyn Bowl Las Vegas. Jam bands, Dead-adjacent bands, and artists who circulate through these festivals come through the venue. Some of the culture is still there. The infrastructure around it is more professional, more expensive, and often more corporate.

The scene did not disappear when I stopped attending. My position inside it changed.

## Everybody gets to go home

The old commune tried to answer too many needs with one institution. It offered belonging, housing, work, food, family, identity, ideology, purpose, and social life in the same place. That totality could make people feel necessary. Somebody had to cook, grow food, repair the vehicle, watch the children, make the sign, clean the grounds, or build the structure. A person's usefulness was visible.

It could also consume them.

The modern descendants are modular. A church can provide recurring community. A co-op can share economics. Cohousing can create neighbors without abolishing private homes. An intentional community can formalize shared labor and resources. A festival can provide several days of intense collective life. A retreat can provide spiritual practice. An art space can provide creative belonging.

People can take part without asking the same group to own every part of them.

That may be what the commune experiments actually taught us. The need for community was real. The first architecture often overcorrected. Private suburban life offers independence with almost no common life. The total commune offers common life with too little independence. A durable community needs doors that close and reasons to open them again.

Dianne Lake's parents bought the whole package. Sixty years later, people spend serious money to enter one of its descendant environments for a week or a weekend. They build a temporary home, meet the neighbors, contribute something, wander through the market, watch the artists, listen to the music, clean up the town, and eventually decide they are ready to leave.

The commune did not disappear.

It learned to pack up on Monday.
