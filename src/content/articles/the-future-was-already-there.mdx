On July 29, 2004, I was walking through Portland on my way to see Toots and the Maytals play a free show in Monument Square when I met Mike Rich.[^toots]

That is the firm date in this story. The rest returns in clusters: the back of the Asylum, the wall, video gear, people turning up at the same apartments and clubs, the feeling that every useful project in Portland was separated from every other useful project by one introduction.

Mike was the first introduction. He was already deeply established in Portland graffiti, the person who had proposed the Asylum's sanctioned wall in 1997 and had become its unofficial curator.[^wall-history] When I met him, I saw a filmmaker's way into a living subject. Graffiti was public but guarded, visible everywhere but difficult to document honestly. It contained art, property, permission, risk, friendship, rivalry, and the argument over whether the work belonged in the street or on a canvas.

I proposed a film built around three people.

Mike would represent street practice. Tim Clorius, who painted as SUBONE, would represent the move into studios, galleries, murals, and fine art. Eli Cayer would represent organizing: the work required to make legal walls, events, and durable community space possible.

That division was too neat. This was why it worked.

## Three paths through one scene

Mike gave me access. I could meet people through him, understand the wall, and get close to a culture that had no reason to trust somebody arriving with a camera. Access did not erase the basic problem. Some of the activity around graffiti was illegal. The closer the film came to the actual street practice, the less openly its central figure could participate.

Tim could participate. He could talk on camera, work in spaces where a camera was possible, and explain what happened after a writer's vocabulary expanded beyond a name. He had come to Portland from Heidelberg. By 2006 he was discussing aerosol as one painting tool among others, alongside oil, and describing his movement away from letters toward personal figures and dreamlike environments.[^bollard]

So the film moved toward Tim.

From Mike's side, that could look like I had used the access and changed the subject. From my side, the subject had revealed which parts could survive contact with a camera. Both readings are fair. The disagreement was not a plot turn with a villain. It was the film's central contradiction becoming personal.

<ThreeBranches />

The three-part structure never became the documentary I first imagined. It became something more useful: a map I could check later.

The public record now makes the branches look almost embarrassingly accurate. Mike continued building a long practice in graffiti and large murals. Tim kept moving between public walls, workshops, canvas, and commissioned work. Eli kept making systems in which other people could work, gather, screen films, paint, grow things, ferment things, and start something of their own.

They were never trapped inside the roles I assigned them. They crossed constantly. Tim had street roots. Mike made commissioned public art. Eli was part of the Faze collaboration with Tim and Bob Smith, all three coming out of graffiti.[^faze] The point was not that they represented separate species. It was that one small scene already contained three possible futures for creative work.

## Portland behaved like one room

The size of Portland mattered. It did not mean the work was small. It meant the distance between kinds of work was short.

An apartment on Park Avenue could put Rick Porensky, Stream Reggae, John Kelly, the people around a hemp store, visiting musicians, visual artists, and whoever had a camera into the same orbit. A concert could lead to a wall. A wall could lead to a studio. A studio could lead to a canoe in Casco Bay, a trip to Cow Island, a rooftop screening, a club projection, or the next person who knew how to solve one part of a project.

Eli's organization MENSK made that connective work explicit. Its later archive describes fiscal sponsorship, event logistics, Portland Rooftop Films, and projects designed to help creative or sustainable communities get something done without first becoming large institutions.[^mensk] That was the practical intelligence I was trying to assign to the third branch of the film. Culture was not only the person holding the can or the brush. It was also the person obtaining permission, finding a wall, getting a projector onto a roof, and making a temporary idea real long enough for people to use it.

Music traveled through the same network. Kut Kaper was a friend and collaborator. His instrumentals gave the finished SUBONE film much of its motion. The soundtrack did not arrive from a licensing catalog after the edit. It came out of the same life as the pictures.

That overlap is easy to romanticize twenty years later. I do not remember it as a golden age. Small scenes can be insular. Money and time are scarce. Personal friction carries farther because everybody knows where it came from. The advantage was more mechanical: when the scene was working, social proximity shortened the route between an idea and the people who could help make it.

## The wall moved the date

For years, I connected my first meeting with Mike to the large George W. Bush and Armageddon mural at the Asylum. The images belong to the same section of memory. The dates do not.

A 2006 interview with Tim says that anti-war mural had already been largely intact for more than three years.[^bollard] A later history of the wall also places it before the Stephen King mural that followed.[^wall-history] I met Mike in July 2004. I did not witness the first creation of the Bush mural that day.

This is a small correction, but it matters. The wall is not supporting scenery. It is evidence with its own chronology. A first-person account gets to preserve the way events felt connected. It does not get to promote that feeling into a false date.

The corrected version is better anyway. I did not arrive at the beginning of Portland graffiti. I arrived at a wall with a history, maintained by people who had already spent years negotiating what could appear there and how long it might survive.

## The camera was changing jobs

While the film was changing shape, the machinery around it was changing faster.

I was working around WCSH during the long transition from analog broadcasting to digital production and distribution. The station's digital signal had begun operating in 2002, years before the nationwide full-power analog cutoff in 2009.[^digital] The transition was not one switch. It was an overlap: old transmission and new transmission, established crews and smaller digital kits, television stories and web stories, familiar job descriptions and a growing suspicion that one person might soon be expected to do several of them.

I remember conversations about a job that would combine reporting, shooting, and editing. That memory is mine, not a claim preserved in a station memo. The wider role became easy to document. By 2009, the *Bangor Daily News* could describe a WCSH/WLBZ “backpack journalist” who worked as photographer, reporter, editor, and web reporter.[^backpack]

At the time, the opportunity sounded like television becoming smaller. In practice, it was also video becoming more portable. A person could carry the reporting tools, make the pictures, shape the piece, and publish for more than one screen.

Then, in the middle of my Portland project, YouTube appeared.

<PortlandTimeline />

YouTube's first upload went online April 23, 2005.[^youtube-first] I had already conceived the graffiti film. By the time I uploaded the finished SUBONE piece on May 28, 2006, the platform was thirteen months old. Two months later, Comscore measured an average of 100 million YouTube streams a day. In October, Google announced that it would buy the company for $1.65 billion in stock.[^comscore][^google]

I did not make my first YouTube video for YouTube. I was already making it when YouTube was invented.

That distinction explains why the film looks the way it does. It was not optimized around a thumbnail, a creator persona, a posting rhythm, audience retention, or a recommendation system. It came from documentary television, local art, club visuals, and the practical question of how to finish something with the access I had.

YouTube gave it a durable address after the fact.

## The film that survived

The finished video is called *SUBONE Graffiti Art*. It runs 9 minutes and 47 seconds. Tim talks about spray paint as a medium, the physical memory that goes into a line, and the move from letters toward a visual language that could hold personal and political meaning. The Asylum wall appears as one place where that language could become public.

What survives is not the neutral middle of the original three-part proposal. It is the branch that could be photographed and finished.

That makes the video incomplete as an account of Portland graffiti. It also makes it honest about its own production. Mike's absence is part of the form, even when a viewer cannot see the negotiations behind it. Eli's organizing work is mostly outside the frame, even though that kind of work helped create the conditions in which public walls and art events could exist.

The video is strongest when Tim talks from inside his own practice. He does not present graffiti as an adolescent stage redeemed by a canvas. Spray paint remains a tool with particular speed, scale, and bodily demands. The studio expands the work; it does not erase the wall.

That is also why the later record between Tim and Mike matters. In 2021, after someone painted anti-Black and anti-gay slurs over downtown murals behind the Lancaster Building and Aura, the two artists were photographed working together to cover the vandalism.[^repair] Whatever conflict I carried from the film was not a permanent description of their relationship.

## The project kept traveling

The Portland film did not stop at the Portland edit.

Kut Kaper had connections to Higher Minds in Southern California. A trip west carried the collaboration into Carson and Los Angeles, where the small-scene logic repeated at a different scale: music, video, live visuals, and friends turning one introduction into several kinds of work.

Tim's route also led back to Germany. The short video below, uploaded in January 2007, follows SUBONE and FRESCO painting a train-yard mural. It works as a compact afterimage to the Portland piece: another wall, another collaborator, another city, and the same practice moving without asking whether it belongs to “street” or “fine art.”

The extra clips are not a hidden second documentary. They are working fragments. One records several pieces taking shape over four days. Another preserves a piece of a Mix Master Mike performance. Together they show what online video was good at before anybody had settled on a respectable form for it: keeping the connective material that television would usually cut away.

## What the branches became

Twenty years of public history do not prove that my original film treatment was correct. They do show why I could see those paths in 2004.

Mike's current artist biography describes more than thirty years in graffiti and large-scale murals.[^mike] Tim, who was described as thirty in 2006, turns fifty in 2026. His work continued through canvases, commissions, community walls, and teaching. There is no need to invent an exact birthday to recognize the span.

Eli's path is the clearest example of organizing as a creative medium. He began keeping bees and making mead in 2002, opened Maine Mead Works in 2007, and founded Urban Farm Fermentory in 2010.[^eli] The fermentory ended its Portland run in 2024, but the underlying project did not harden into one permanent venue. In 2026, reporting placed Eli and Jason Dionne in Madawaska, building the community-funded NorthStar Brewpub in a former border station.[^northstar]

Wall, studio, institution. The words were never strict categories. They were ways of asking where the energy went next.

The film itself followed a fourth path: archive. It stayed online long enough to become evidence of its own moment. The video now carries more than a hundred thousand views, a number that would have sounded absurd when I started shooting and YouTube did not exist. That audience accumulated without converting the project into a career plan or a content schedule. The piece simply remained findable.

## Leaving Portland

I left for Los Angeles after the upload.

At the time, moving west felt like moving toward scale. Los Angeles had larger industries, denser professional networks, more production, and more possible versions of the work I wanted to do. Portland could feel like a place where the same small group kept circulating through every project.

The mistake was not leaving. I do not wish I had frozen my life in 2006. The mistake was confusing the size of a scene with the size of its potential.

The Portland network was small enough to see. Its functions were exposed. You could watch access turn into a film, a wall turn into an argument, a friendship turn into a soundtrack, and an organizer turn an unused surface or roof into public space. In a larger city, the same exchanges happened across more people and more distance. They were harder to recognize as one system.

Los Angeles did not replace the Portland lesson. It hid it inside a much larger machine.

## The unwritten second season

The television version of this story returned in Nevada fifteen years later.

In August 2021, HBO premiered *Small Town News: KPVM Pahrump*, a six-part series about an independent television station trying to grow into a larger market.[^hbo] By the end of that year, KPVM had opened a second operation in Henderson, about five miles from where I lived. I started there around Christmas.

The new studio was small. The job was not. I reported, shot, edited, wrote for the web, handled graphics, helped with production, attended mixers, talked with potential advertisers, and did the miscellaneous work required when a local station is trying to establish a new city bureau. The “backpack journalist” idea I had heard around WCSH was no longer a speculative digital role. It was the practical unit of production.

The distribution plan was the other half of the convergence. When KPVM announced the Henderson studio, the station described short hourly stories streamed to YouTube and then assembled into half-hour television broadcasts.[^henderson] The web clip and the newscast were no longer rival formats. They were two outputs from the same reporting day.

I covered the opening period around the Dollar Loan Center, the Henderson arena now called Lee's Family Forum. I shot interviews and community events. The archive records the first Studio B interview with Trish Nash, who was running for Henderson City Council Ward III—not mayor, as memory or casual retelling can easily compress it.[^nash]

This was not a triumphant return to the opportunity I missed in Maine. KPVM was improvised, demanding, and fragile in the way small media operations often are. The point is not that history rewarded patience. The point is that the production pattern had become ordinary.

One person could carry a camera, report, edit, publish to YouTube, and assemble material for broadcast. The hybrid role had moved from a transitional experiment inside local television to a baseline expectation. The platform invented while I was making the graffiti film had become part of the station's daily plumbing.

The unwritten second season of the HBO show was happening in a storefront in Henderson. I had somehow walked into it without recognizing the structure at first.

## The future in the frame

I began the Portland film with three people because I thought their differences would explain graffiti. The unfinished version explained something larger.

Creative work needs practice, translation, and organization. It needs the person willing to protect the form, the person willing to carry it into another medium, and the person willing to build a place where other people can participate. Those jobs may belong to three people or one. In Portland, they were visible because the scene was compact.

The camera had its own three-way split. Television, online video, and one-person production looked like separate futures in 2004. By Henderson, they were one job.

The original documentary did not fail because only one branch reached the screen. It survived in the exact form the moment allowed: a short film, made inside a changing local scene, completed just as a new public archive arrived to hold it.

I thought I was making a record of graffiti before I left Portland. I was also recording the production system I would keep encountering for the next twenty years.

The future was already there. It just did not have a play button yet.
