In June 2026, Dario Amodei, CEO of the most powerful AI lab on earth, published a policy essay. Most of it is what you'd expect: testing regimes for frontier models, export controls, wage insurance, the machinery of governments trying to keep pace with an exponential. But buried in the economics section is the most honest sentence in the document. He writes that any real response to AI-driven job displacement has to solve two problems, not one: providing for people economically, and the harder one, the need for people to find meaning, purpose, and agency.

And then, in the very next breath, he punts. The economic half gets a policy menu. The meaning half, the half he himself calls more important, gets handed off: that one is for society to work out collectively, not something policy can directly address.

I want to take that hand-off seriously, because I think he means it, and because of what it implies. There is no committee coming. The most resourced institution in this story just looked at the question of how people stay people in an AI world and said, correctly, that it's above policy's pay grade. Which means the question doesn't trickle down to society in the abstract. It lands on you. I'm society. You're society. The working-out happens at the scale of one person deciding how they're going to live with these machines, or it doesn't happen at all.

And I should be clear about where I'm standing when I say that. Amodei writes from the tower, and as far as I can tell he writes accurately: testing regimes, coalitions, the shape of the next decade. I'm not a policymaker. I have no skin in that game. My entire relationship to his world is that I pay for the tools his industry makes and I use them every single day. The view from the tower is the future of AI. The view from down here is a monthly bill and a product that argues with me. Both views are real. Only one of them is mine to write about.

So here's my working-out. It starts in a newsroom.

## The wave, judged by where it broke last season

I began working in television news in 2004. I was in the building when the station flipped from analog to digital, and a few months into the job a video site launched that most of the industry dismissed as a toy. I made a bet that seemed eccentric at the time: that the internet, not broadcast, was where video was going. I spent the next couple of years making videos and websites for bands and artists in Portland, Maine, then moved to Los Angeles in 2006 and kept doing it. That year, on a shoot, a guy pulled a video iPod out of his pocket and played footage on it, and there was the whole arc in one object: video had gone from the tower, to the browser, to a deck of cards in someone's hand, in about two years. Each of those transitions took years to arrive and longer to be believed. I built a career on noticing them slightly before the people around me did.

AI is the same movie at a different frame rate. The distance between "this is a toy" and "I can't tell it from real" used to be measured in years. Now it's measured in months. Video the models generated looked like garbage not long ago; today I genuinely can't always tell. And this is the part that matters: the people who evaluated AI a year ago, decided it was junk, and filed the judgment away permanently are wrong in a specific, expensive way. They're judging a wave by where it broke last season. They are exactly the people who will never benefit from putting it in their workflow, not because the tool excluded them but because their snapshot expired and they kept it framed on the wall.

## The billion-dollar prediction, and who it's actually for

The essay does offer one optimistic image for individuals: the prediction that AI will let single individuals build billion-dollar companies. I believe him. I also think it's a strange thing to hold up as the human-scale good news, because right now it describes maybe a thousand people on the planet, a handful of small groups already well connected in the AI and VC world, and I am not one of them. Twenty-five years of creative projects have taught me a few things, and one of them is that you never get anywhere without a network. My network hates AI. The mere mention of it makes them reject everything attached to it, so there's no point in even bringing it up. AI doesn't do the work for me, and it's cost me plenty while returning nothing in the currency Dario's counting. Business is not my business, unfortunately. I'm a graphic designer, a web developer, a photographer, a videographer, an AV tech, and lately a person who spends too much time trying to make AI not act like shit. So maybe the billion-dollar founder is my future, and maybe it's yours, but getting there takes as much work as the traditional route, probably more. He's right about the leverage and wrong about who it's obviously for.

The difference now is what one person can carry: things that would have taken a team, I can do alone. I don't have a venture capital network or a well-connected group in Palo Alto, but I have better ideas than they do, and I'm not locked into their closed society of tech bros and billionaires. That frees me up to be creative, and to figure out how to bend their lame, generic tools into something that actually suits my needs. None of it is cheap, either. The subscriptions, the compute, the hardware: it stacks up, and unless you're attached to a funded network, you're paying for all of it out of your own income while everything else gets more expensive. The one-person studio is real, but nobody mentions that it runs on your grocery money. And yet that's exactly what makes the billion-dollar prediction more plausible than it sounds, just not for the people it's usually pointed at. The creators worth backing are findable, because they're the ones already spending their spare cash and spare time building something interesting with no support at all. If the well-funded networks ever get tired of recycling the same connected circles, the proof-of-work is sitting out in the open.

And the carrying capacity is measurable. Something that took me a month ten years ago takes me a day now. I can do half a year's work in a week. It doesn't cash out as "faster, then done." It cashes out as faster, better, and more. The scope of what one person can attempt has exploded. The labs read that curve as revenue. From down here it reads as something else entirely: range. For the first time in my working life, the size of my ambitions and the size of my output are in the same neighborhood.

## What displacement feels like at the scale of one person

Which brings me to what displacement actually feels like at individual scale, because it is not an unemployment statistic. The thing people fear losing first isn't the paycheck. It's the seat. The specific dignity of being the person who directs the work: who decides what gets made, to what standard, and why. When a model can do the whole thing end to end, what gets automated isn't your task list. It's your position at the head of it. That's why the dread arrives years before any job does or doesn't disappear, and why no wage insurance program touches it.

Here's the reframe, and it's the whole reason I'm writing this. Agency is not a feeling you wait to recover. It's infrastructure you build.

There are two kinds of agency hiding inside that one word, and the essay uses the existential kind: purpose, dignity, a reason to get up. But there's also the practical kind: actual, mechanical command of the instruments. The relationship between them is not optional. You do not get the existential kind without the practical kind. You cannot feel like the director of your life in an AI world if you have no instrument with which to direct the AI.

## Slop is the photograph of an empty chair

And I can tell you exactly what the absence of that instrument looks like, because it's flooding every image search on the internet right now. AI doesn't replace a workflow; it's another piece of the puzzle, and the piece demands as much from you as it gives. I spend as much time tuning these systems for my projects as I spend on the projects themselves. Probably more. That's not a defect in the tool or in me. That tuning is the chair. Skip it, expect the machine to do the directing for you, and you get what everyone who skips it gets: the defaults. And the defaults are someone else's taste, averaged. Prompt "goth" and you get boots, piercings, and tattoos. Prompt "psychedelic" and you get mushrooms and eyeballs. Type that, post it everywhere, multiply it by a few million people doing the same, and you get slop: search for graffiti art today and you'll wade through generic machine output before you find a single actual wall. I hate it as much as the painters do.

But slop is not an argument against the machine. Slop is the photograph of an empty chair. It's what AI output looks like when nobody is directing it, and the answer to it isn't exiling everything machine-touched to a ghetto while hand-made work keeps the gallery. The systems need to get better at letting tuned work stand next to drawn work, because here's the part nobody who types a one-line prompt understands: making AI output that isn't generic takes more work than learning to draw. I mean that literally. A themed avatar, a script, b-roll, visuals, the site they live on, the posts that carry them, all of it coherent, all of it recognizably yours and not the average of the training data: getting a generic corporate model to produce that means first figuring out what your own taste even is, precisely enough to specify it, and then forcing tools that were built to please everyone into serving one person. And those tools push back. Anyone making real work with these systems knows the experience: your tone gets toned down, your style gets flagged, whole categories of legitimate art sit behind a refusal because somewhere a moderation layer decided that edge equals risk. The blandness isn't only an averaging problem. It's enforced. Half of tuning is specification; the other half is negotiation with a product designed to say no to exactly the things that would make the work yours. Anyone can type some shit into a prompt. Almost nobody does the second part.

## The second part has to live somewhere

The second part has to live somewhere. Mine lives in a system I call the Grimoire, and the pattern under it is simple enough to fit in a sentence: structured data, plus scoped agents that serve it, plus a human who stays the director. The data is curated by hand, never auto-ingested, because what I choose to keep is the taste, and the taste is the part that's mine. Gaps get logged, not auto-filled, because an honest map of what I don't know is worth more than a confident hallucination of what I might. The agents are deliberately narrow: a swarm of small workers, each one individually incapable of replacing me, collectively capable of multiplying me. No single genius in the system. Just a filing system with geniuses bolted to it, and me holding the index.

![A hand resting on a card catalog index while narrow, single-purpose workers hum around it: no central genius, just a filing system and the person who holds the index.](https://cdn.hob.farm/blog/genius-system/genius-system3.jpg)

That's the secret the title is pointing at. The country of geniuses is coming whether we like it or not, and geniuses don't file. They generate. Endlessly, brilliantly, indiscriminately. Whoever owns the filing system owns the direction, and whoever owns the direction keeps the seat. The genius was never the scarce thing. The index was.

One honest caveat. This is a worked example, not a doctrine. The Grimoire answers the agency question for one domain, creative and aesthetic production, because that's the domain I live in. A nurse, a machinist, and a tax attorney will need different instruments. The essay left the question open on purpose, and I'm filling in one answer, not the answer. But one worked example is exactly what the hand-off asked for, and it's one more than policy is ever going to produce.

## The ladder starts lower than you think

If you want the ladder, it starts lower than you think. Structure what you know before you automate anything: the files, the references, the decisions, the taste. Then put small, scoped tools in service of that structure, one job each. Then stay in the chair. Foundations first, then instruments, then direction. You climb from user of geniuses to architect they report to, and the climb is made of filing.

I'd still trade a chapter of that essay for a model that just follows direction reliably. But the direction part, the part that decides whether these machines amplify a person or dissolve one, was never going to come from a lab or a law. It comes from the boring, sovereign, unglamorous work of building your own index. I've watched video move from the tower to the browser to a pocket, and now to a prompt. The wave isn't slowing down to be believed this time either. The geniuses are arriving. Bring a cabinet.
