## The old photograph is full of things that disappeared

The Hôtel de Sens is easy to identify in the 1920 photograph. Two round turrets hold the street entrance between them. A steep roof rises behind the pointed arch. The silhouette is close enough to the building standing at 1 rue du Figuier today that the comparison seems obvious.

The photograph becomes more interesting when the eye leaves the monument.

A dark market wall runs along the left side of the frame. A low commercial building presses against the right. Advertisements climb the wall at the corner. A car waits beneath the arch while pedestrians cross a cobbled street around a freestanding lamp. The medieval house is not presented as a museum object. It is trapped inside the working city.

That is the real before-and-after story. Paris did not merely clean a façade. The city rescued one building, cleared much of the fabric crowding it, carried out a three-decade restoration, and assigned it a public use. The turrets endured. The conditions that made them look ordinary did not.

## Built for an archbishop, not for posterity

The Ville de Paris dates construction of the present Hôtel de Sens from 1475 to 1519. Tristan de Salazar, archbishop of Sens, ordered the work at a time when the bishopric of Paris fell within his jurisdiction.[^forney] It was a Paris residence for an ecclesiastical authority whose principal seat lay elsewhere.

That origin accounts for the building's mixture of ceremony and defense. The pointed entrance, steep roofs, turrets, stone window frames, and compact courtyard belonged to a late-medieval urban house built to project position. They were not designed as picturesque props for a future historic district.

The institutional logic did not last. Salazar's successors used the house little. Marguerite de Valois, known as Queen Margot, stayed there for a few months. The archbishops left in 1622, and the property was rented to private occupants until the Revolution.[^forney]

The building survived, but survival is not the same as remaining unchanged.

## A monument used as a workplace

After the Revolution, the former residence was sold as national property. Through the nineteenth century it accommodated uses that sound improbable only if the modern library is mistaken for the building's natural state: a transport business, a laundry, a food-canning operation, a hare-hair cutter, the Confiturerie de Saint James, and a glass depot.[^forney]

The 1869 photograph makes one of those occupations visible. **CONFITURERIE DE SAINT JAMES** is written over the main entrance. A man stands in front of the gateway, but the doorway reads like business premises rather than a ceremonial portal.

Later views show the same pressure from the city around it. Eugène Atget's photograph from about 1898 turns away from the symmetrical front and down the side street. Neighboring buildings nearly touch the turret. The 1911 Agence Rol photograph gives the entrance only a narrow interval of open space. A market structure occupies the left edge.

These images do not supply an element-by-element building survey. They do show a consistent condition: the Hôtel de Sens remained embedded in a dense commercial block. The pale, freestanding monument familiar from modern photographs had not appeared yet.

## The 1920 frame has a name and a date

The supplied photograph can be identified more precisely than a generic label such as "old Paris."

The Bibliothèque nationale de France catalogues it as an Agence Meurisse press photograph made on May 26, 1920. Its agency number is 80722. The BnF record identifies the glass-negative image as a view of the Hôtel des archevêques de Sens on rue des Figuiers and marks it public domain.[^meurisse]

That record matters. It fixes the photograph after the City of Paris bought the building in 1911 but before the restoration began in 1929.[^forney] It also keeps the image from being used as evidence for events outside its frame. The blackened masonry and damaged openings show condition. They do not reveal, by themselves, who altered a wall or when a particular repair occurred.

The image is evidence with edges.

## Saving the building changed the block

The City of Paris acquired the Hôtel de Sens in 1911, when its condition was described as extremely dilapidated.[^forney] The current French Ministry of Culture record lists it as a protected historic monument and records a classification order dated March 20, 1912.[^pop]

Municipal rescue did not begin with a clean slate. The city had bought a repeatedly adapted building inside a district undergoing its own demolition and redevelopment. A 1926 drawing by Gustave Ferdinand Boberg is explicitly titled *Demolitions of old houses opposite the Hôtel de Sens*. The hotel appears at the far left while broken timbers and exposed party walls take over the scene.

That drawing is useful because it shows clearance as an urban act, not a magical fade between two photographs. It is also a drawing, not a construction photograph. The distinction stays in the caption.

The formal restoration began in 1929 and was not completed until 1961.[^forney] A stereoscopic façade view marked 1928–29 sits at the threshold of that work. It records the building near the opening of the campaign, but it does not prove that any specific stone, roof, window, or turret was repaired in that exact moment.

The published sources used here establish the dates of the long campaign. They do not provide a reliable component-by-component account of original fabric, replacement stone, or reconstructed details. The modern façade should therefore be described as restored, not treated as a perfectly untouched survival from 1519.

## The after is a library

The future use was decided before the work was finished. Paris approved moving the Bibliothèque Forney into the Hôtel de Sens in 1929. The collections began moving 32 years later, in 1961.[^forney]

Forney had opened in 1886 from a bequest intended for the education of artisans. The present library continues that practical visual mission through collections devoted to fine arts, decorative arts, graphic arts, crafts, fashion, advertising, and design.[^forney]

This use changes how the restoration reads. The city did not preserve a sealed aristocratic interior. It adapted a former residence, factory, depot, and commercial address into a public research library. The building remained useful, but the definition of useful changed.

## What persisted, and what the pair cannot prove

The before-and-after crops hold the main street composition at roughly the same scale. The paired turrets, pointed entrance, steep central roof, and asymmetrical window arrangement remain legible across 96 years. The comparison also exposes major visual changes: the masonry is lighter; windows and roofs are repaired; surrounding structures no longer press into the same frame; signs, market walls, and curbside commerce have disappeared.

The photographs were not made from the same tripod point. The 2016 camera is closer and slightly lower, and its lens gives the façade a different shape. Cropping can improve the comparison, but it cannot create a survey-quality match. No perspective warp was used in the feature.

The pair also cannot assign a date to every visible difference. The chronological gallery helps separate periods, while the institutional history supplies the documented milestones. Where neither source answers the question, the answer remains open.

That restraint makes the transformation more convincing. The Hôtel de Sens did not travel untouched from medieval Paris into the present. It persisted through new owners, ordinary work, damage, clearance, restoration, and another public purpose. What survived was not a frozen scene. It was a building capable of entering another century.
