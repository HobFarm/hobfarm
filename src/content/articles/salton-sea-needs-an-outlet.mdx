## North Shore, connected and disconnected

In 2010, I stood at North Shore and photographed water inside the old marina. Pilings rose from the harbor. The Albert Frey yacht club faced a basin that still read as one piece of the Salton Sea. The building had just reopened after rehabilitation, more than fifty years after its ship-like concrete form arrived on the shore.[^north-shore-register]

The camera was not recording a golden-age resort. The jetty had been damaged by flooding decades earlier. The Sea was already saline and unstable. Yet the local fact was plain: the harbor and the main water body still met.

In 2010, the restored yacht club also housed the Salton Sea History Museum. I volunteered inside the same building I was photographing, among display cases and wall panels about the resorts, races, families, and changing shoreline. The historical materials in this article came from that work. They show the earlier promise in physical detail: advertisements and renderings, but also built roads, working docks, launch ramps, boats, and the yacht club beside an open harbor. The captions identify only what the scans show or what their retained archive labels support. Item-level creators are not known.

[Deserts Remember Water](/articles/deserts-remember-water/) established the basin's repeated connection to the Colorado River. This article starts after that history and asks what an operating system for the present Sea would require.

## First too much water, then too little

The museum files show that the Salton Sea was an actual racing venue, not just a development rendering. Hydroplanes crossed open water in front of shorelines packed with cars, trailers, crews, cranes, boats, and spectators. Posters promoted the Salton City 500 with serious prize money. The waterfront had an operating culture because there was water at the waterfront.

The system underneath it was still uncontrolled. Storms and rising water damaged shoreline property in the 1970s and early 1980s. Later, falling inflow and continued evaporation pulled the Sea away from the same marinas. The Salton Sea that flooded people out of waterfront property eventually receded so far that their harbors were left in the desert.[^salton-bay]

## The Sea has no control surface

The Salton Sea is a terminal basin. Water arrives from agricultural drainage, the New and Alamo rivers, Mexico, the Coachella Valley, local runoff, and groundwater. Water leaves mainly through evaporation. Salt does not evaporate. Neither do selenium, nutrients, pesticides, or every industrial contaminant carried into the basin.

The Pacific Institute's 2025 accounting lists about 890,200 acre-feet of gross inflow: 759,000 from Imperial Valley, 43,000 from Mexico, 71,000 from Coachella Valley, 4,900 from the local watershed, and 12,300 from groundwater. After 85,600 acre-feet of project and wetland use in that accounting, about 804,600 acre-feet reached the Sea.[^current-sea]

At the current footprint, this article's screening model estimates annual net evaporation near 1.05 million acre-feet. The number comes from the Bureau of Reclamation's 1995 area-capacity table and a 5.6-foot annual net-evaporation assumption used to reproduce the supplied engineering checkpoint.[^hydro-survey] It is not a forecast. Seasonal weather, salinity, heat, inflow, and future bathymetry change it. It is enough to show the operating fault: recent inflow does not hold the current shoreline, and no outlet controls the accumulating salt.

The common answer is to add water. That treats level and salt as if they were the same variable. They are not. Ocean water could slow retreat while adding more salt. Fresh water could reduce salinity while consuming a scarce regional supply. A smaller Sea could reduce evaporation while exposing more playa. Any serious design has to control level, salt, contaminants, habitat, power, and flood risk at once.

The proposal is therefore simple to state and difficult to build: give the Salton Sea an inlet specification and an outlet.

<SaltonSeaGraphic kind="two-plants" />

Upstream treatment cleans water before it enters the Sea. That includes separate treatment trains for Gulf water, the New River, the Alamo River, and agricultural drainage. The plants remove pathogens, nutrients, pesticides, industrial contaminants, and enough salt to meet an inlet target.

Lake-side desalination does a different job. It continuously withdraws lake water, produces finished water for local users, and sends concentrate into a salt-handling system. The freshwater product replaces some imported Colorado River water. The withdrawal removes the salt that would otherwise remain behind.

Deep geothermal brine is a third fluid. The Department of Energy's lithium estimate concerns brine in a deep bedrock geothermal reservoir, not water scooped from the surface Sea.[^doe] That loop makes power, may recover lithium and other minerals, and reinjects its brine. It can exchange electricity and usable heat with the water industry. It does not get mixed into the lake-water or polluted-river loops for the sake of a tidy illustration.

<SaltonSeaGraphic kind="regional-machine" />

Every box in that graphic exists somewhere as commercial engineering. The integrated project does not. Screening coherence is not feasibility.

## Give the Sea an operating specification

A managed lake needs a level band and a salinity band. It also needs rules for wet years, dry years, outages, earthquakes, habitat needs, and upstream water changes.

The model tests -240 feet as a lower design-study shoreline, -235 feet as an upper sensitivity, and -230 feet as a flood-history comparison. It does not select a final target. At the August 2026 observation, the interpolated volume is about 4.01 million acre-feet. Reaching -240 feet would require roughly 754,000 acre-feet of one-time refill before losses. Reaching -235 would require about 1.81 million. Reaching -230 would require about 2.93 million.[^hydro-survey]

The higher number is not automatically better. In the 1970s and early 1980s, the opposite problem damaged homes, businesses, farms, and infrastructure. In *Salton Bay Marina v. Imperial Irrigation District*, shoreline owners pursued claims after rising water flooded their properties. The appellate opinion records a $6,959,336 jury award and a landscape full of flooding easements and agreements.[^salton-bay]

That history rules out casual promises to put the old shoreline back. Before an elevation becomes policy, current parcel, tribal, road, rail, utility, levee, habitat, and structure GIS must be intersected with updated bathymetry and flood modeling.

<SaltonSeaGraphic kind="shorelines" />

The same caution applies to salinity. The 2024 long-range planning record carries measured and modeled salinity histories, but I did not find a same-week 2026 field sample that could support a precise claim about today's whole-Sea salinity.[^long-range-plan] The model therefore labels 90 parts per thousand as a transition sensitivity, not an observation, and drops lake-side product recovery to 25 percent for that case. The steady cases use 35.5 and 40 ppt with 45 percent recovery. Actual membrane selection requires current chemistry, temperature, scaling, organic matter, pretreatment tests, and pilot data.

The annual ideal balance is compact. Let product water be P, lake withdrawal L, recovery r, net evaporation E, treated inlet I, lake salinity Cₛ, and inlet salinity Cᵢ. Then P = rL, I = E + L, and ICᵢ = LCₛ.

<SaltonSeaGraphic kind="stable-balance" />

At the -240-foot study shoreline, a Bahrain-nameplate product output of 286,535 acre-feet per year requires 636,745 acre-feet of lake withdrawal at 45 percent recovery. Add modeled net evaporation of 1,132,925 acre-feet, and treated inlet rises to 1,769,670 acre-feet per year. To hold a 35.5 ppt Sea in the ideal balance, that admitted water averages 12.77 ppt.

That is the central scale correction. Producing 286,535 acre-feet does not mean importing 286,535 acre-feet. The lake has to replace evaporation and the water removed with the outlet stream.

## Move Bahrain to the Salton Sea, as a comparison

Bahrain's Electricity and Water Authority reports six desalination plants using reverse osmosis and thermal technologies with combined capacity of 213 million imperial gallons per day.[^ewa-capacity] Converted with a 365-day operating year, that is 968,317 cubic meters per day or 286,535 acre-feet per year.

That is nameplate capacity, not verified current production.

The official open-data series reports 61,821.243 million gallons of distilled potable water in 2024. EWA's statistical booklet identifies the same annual series in million imperial gallons, which makes the 2024 result about 169.37 million imperial gallons per day, or 227,847 acre-feet per year.[^bahrain-data]

Coachella Valley Water District says about 260,000 acre-feet of Colorado River water is delivered to local farms in a year.[^cvwd] Bahrain's actual 2024 desalinated production equals 87.6 percent of that comparison. Its nameplate is 26,535 acre-feet larger.

<SaltonSeaGraphic kind="bahrain-coachella" />

This is not a claim that one plant can supply both valleys. Bahrain operates a fleet. It is not a claim that the Coachella delivery is available for an automatic trade. The nameplate margin disappears quickly under 90 percent plant availability and 95 percent product conveyance efficiency. To deliver 286,535 acre-feet after those two assumptions, the local fleet needs about 335,129 acre-feet of installed annual product capacity.

The comparison does prove something useful. The product volume is industrial, but it is not beyond the scale of water infrastructure already operating in an arid place.

## The power field is there, but it is not free

Modern seawater reverse osmosis commonly uses about 3 to 4 kilowatt-hours of electricity per cubic meter of product water, with intake, pretreatment, pressure recovery, and post-treatment affecting the total.[^ro-energy] At Bahrain-nameplate output, the model gives 121 average megawatts at 3.0 kWh/m³, 137 MW at 3.4, and 161 MW at 4.0. Annual electricity is 1.06 to 1.41 terawatt-hours.

A peer-reviewed Bahrain study estimated that desalination plants used 1,260 GWh of electricity in 2017, equivalent to about 144 average MW. That is a historical cross-check, not current fleet consumption. It also excludes thermal energy used by thermal desalination.[^bahrain-energy]

<SaltonSeaGraphic kind="water-power" />

The Salton Sea geothermal field offers a similarly useful scale comparison. BHE Renewables reports ten existing plants at about 345 MW net combined. DOE describes about 400 MW operating region-wide in 2023 and a 2,950 MW technical potential.[^bher][^doe] The 345 and 400 figures overlap. The 2,950 is potential, not a plant fleet waiting for a cable.

Existing output has contracts. New wells have drilling, seismic, transmission, brine, reinjection, cooling, and permitting constraints. Solar and wind add energy but also add storage and grid requirements. Residual geothermal heat may support multi-effect distillation, vapor compression, or later brine concentration, but only after a temperature and opportunity-cost audit. Multi-stage flash belongs in the design only if that heat audit beats modern RO and concentration alternatives.

The full water machine will consume more than the RO bars show. Gulf pumping, river treatment, lake intake, finished-water delivery, salt concentration, storm capacity, and emergency operations all need their own load profiles.

## Substitute for Colorado water; do not pretend to pump it backward

On April 17, 2026, Reclamation reported total Colorado River system storage near 36 percent of capacity, alongside the lowest snowpack on record and record-breaking March heat.[^colorado-current] The post-2026 operating process is still unsettled. Reclamation released a Draft Environmental Impact Statement in January 2026, closed comments on March 2, and did not identify a preferred alternative in the draft.[^post-2026]

That record matters because the supplied outline anticipated a July 2026 final EIS. The official project page did not support that claim when this article was checked on August 14. The article uses the public draft status instead.

The water benefit proposed here is substitution. Salton Basin product water serves an approved customer in Imperial or Coachella Valley. That customer's equivalent Colorado River diversion is reduced under a conservation, exchange, storage, delivery, or forbearance agreement. The conserved volume remains in the river system under whatever accounting the United States, Mexico, states, tribes, districts, and federal government actually approve.

No water is physically pumped upstream. Nevada and Arizona do not automatically receive a new allocation. A year at Bahrain-nameplate output represents 286,535 acre-feet of production; five years represent 1.43 million and ten represent 2.87 million. Those are production-equivalent totals, not guaranteed reservoir storage.

The model also refuses to count today's drainage twice. If 286,535 acre-feet of imported supply is replaced, agricultural return flow to the Sea will likely fall. The base case applies a transparent 20 percent return-flow response, reducing future raw drainage by 57,307 acre-feet. That fraction is a sensitivity, not a measured forecast. District-specific crop, soil, canal, drainage, and conservation studies must replace it.

## Clean the rivers before they become lake water

The New River crosses the border carrying urban runoff, treated and untreated municipal and industrial waste, agricultural runoff, bacteria, nutrients, pesticides, pathogens, trash, and sediment. Binational projects have sharply improved some measures. The State Water Board reports that border bacteria fell by roughly an order of magnitude, volatile organic compounds dropped below detection in its comparison, dissolved oxygen improved, and nutrient loading declined. It also lists continuing trash, pesticide, nutrient, pathogen, industrial-discharge, and infrastructure problems.[^new-river]

The New River Improvement Project added a trash screen, bypass, erosion control, and a pump-back system around Calexico. The current state tracker marks construction completed.[^new-river-project] That work reduces exposure and improves a specific reach. It does not transform all New River water into a finished controlled inflow.

The Alamo River is dominated by Imperial Valley agricultural return flow. Both rivers need measured source profiles and modular treatment trains. The output target is not necessarily drinking water. It is water clean enough, and low enough in salt, to enter the managed Sea without importing the next pollution problem. Potable and agricultural product comes from the separate lake-side plant after the Sea functions as regulated storage.

## Salt is the project, not the byproduct

At the -240-foot base case, the lake-side outlet moves about 27.88 million metric tons of dissolved salts each year. Its ideal 45 percent-recovery brine is 64.5 ppt and occupies 350,209 acre-feet before further concentration.

<SaltonSeaGraphic kind="salt-path" />

At 5.6 feet of net annual evaporation, simply spreading that brine volume into shallow ponds produces a hydraulic minimum of about 62,537 acres. Real ponds need residence time, operational storage, storm freeboard, liners, seepage monitoring, wildlife protection, dust control, access, and closure space. The minimum is not a site plan.

The dry-solids equivalent is more than 1.1 million 25-ton truckloads per year. That does not mean the proposal is to truck all the salt. It shows why a casual promise to sell it is not a disposal strategy.

Magnesium, gypsum, bromine, potash, and industrial salt may have markets. Recovery requires current assays, consistent purity, buyers, processing energy, price forecasts, and a residual fraction with a legal destination. Lithium from deep geothermal brine is a separate opportunity. Selenium-, pesticide-, nutrient-, and industrial-bearing river residuals stay separate from cleaner marine concentrate unless a documented treatment stage justifies combining them.

## The route exists on a map, not on the ground

A 2016 Salton Sea Authority study described a Gulf of California corridor of roughly 120 miles, with a maximum elevation near 300 feet above mean sea level through the Laguna Salada area. Its 150,000-acre-foot-per-year example used an 86-inch pipeline.[^route-study]

<SaltonSeaGraphic kind="gulf-route" />

The profile is favorable compared with a route across a mountain range. It is not construction readiness. A present design needs a surveyed intake location, seasonal Gulf ecology, screens or subsurface intake analysis, marine construction, Mexican environmental review, land and Indigenous rights, seismic design, pumping stations, power, emergency shutdown, border crossings, ownership, finance, and operating authority.

Mexico is not a transit strip between an American intake and an American lake. It has to be a full partner in source protection, ownership, jobs, construction, pricing, habitat, emergency operations, residual responsibility, and benefit sharing.

There is institutional precedent for doing the work jointly. Minute 323 created a binational framework that included study of a Sea of Cortez desalination plant and water exchanges, while stating that implementation requires a specific agreement by both governments through the International Boundary and Water Commission.[^minute-323] The 2020 binational desalination study examined sources, costs, distribution, exchange, and benefits. It is not permission for this project. It proves the subject is already inside a treaty institution.

## The review panel said no to the import case it studied

California's Independent Review Panel evaluated 18 import submissions. Three Sea of Cortez approaches passed its fatal-flaw screen and were combined into a concept for feasibility analysis. The panel did not recommend refilling the Sea with a large direct ocean import. It emphasized cost, long schedules, energy and carbon, ecological risk, Mexican benefits, salt management, exposed-playa alternatives, and the possibility that a lower-volume Sea could meet regional goals.[^panel]

The panel's most relevant insight was not that imported water is physically impossible. It was that water without an outlet and a benefit structure is not enough. Its own alternatives paired a smaller Sea with lake desalination and used imported or transferred water to replace brine losses.

Proposal advocate Jenny E. Ross later argued that the panel had not completed the promised proposal-by-proposal feasibility work, combined unlike designs into an unfavorable composite, inflated costs, chose grid power over proposed renewables, and gave the public too little time to respond.[^ross] Those are detailed participant objections. They are not an independent reversal of the panel's findings.

This proposal changes the benefit case. It adds finished-water production, local Colorado River substitution, separate upstream treatment, an explicit salt outlet, and a lower shoreline study band. Those changes may answer some objections. They do not erase intake ecology, cost, land, energy, waste, schedule, binational law, or the possibility that targeted habitat and dust projects outperform a whole-Sea system per dollar.

The honest conclusion is narrower: the revised architecture deserves a new screen. It has not earned the word feasible.

## Ecology does not end at a salinity target

Lower salinity can preserve more biological options, but salinity alone does not eliminate nutrient loading, algal production, hypoxia, disease, contaminants, or food-web changes. California wildlife officials describe a cycle in which winds bring old deoxygenated bottom water upward, fish die as oxygen collapses, and avian botulism can then move through birds, carcasses, and maggots.[^birds]

The 2010 beach photographs record both life and damage. They cannot assign a cause to one carcass or one fish kill. That is why the most graphic supplied image is not published here.

A managed system still needs shallow habitat, depth variation, mixing, refuge areas, seasonal operating rules, contaminant monitoring, bird-disease response, and ecological thresholds. Intake and outlet operations cannot be tuned only for gallons and power.

## Build evidence before building the fleet

The first phase is not a 120-mile pipe. It is an independent model audit and a binational design table.

California, Mexico, tribes, local communities, water districts, habitat managers, public-health agencies, engineers, and potential customers need to agree on the questions before anyone agrees on a project. The deterministic model published with this article is a screening instrument. It makes assumptions inspectable and forces water, salt, power, drainage response, plant availability, and conveyance loss into the same arithmetic. It does not price land, solve law, or predict ecology.

The next gate is laboratory and field work using actual Gulf, Salton, New River, Alamo River, agricultural-drainage, and geothermal fluids. The third is a 5 to 10 million-US-gallon-per-day pilot with a real customer, a real concentrate plan, a real habitat interface, and public monitoring. The model's 10 MGD pilot produces about 11,200 acre-feet per year, enough to expose pretreatment, membrane, scaling, energy, residual, and reliability problems without pretending to operate the Sea.

After that comes a regional demonstration attached to one drainage basin and one lake intake. A larger binational conveyance demonstration follows only after environmental and treaty gates. Modular expansion toward the 260,000-acre-foot Coachella comparison happens only if water, energy, salt, ecology, customer, cost, and governance results survive independent review.

Bahrain-equivalent capacity is the last step in the argument, not the first construction contract.

## An outlet changes the question

North Shore does not need a promise that the 1960s are coming back. It needs a shoreline whose future can be stated in operating terms.

The lower -240-foot screen is close enough to today's Sea to limit refill compared with -235 or -230, yet even that level cannot be called safe until the parcel and infrastructure work is complete. The old marina might need a maintained channel, a separate harbor cell, or a different relationship to the main Sea. A restored photograph is not a hydraulic design.

What the outlet supplies is control. When evaporation rises, inlet can respond. When wet years arrive, outlet and storage rules can respond. When salt exceeds the band, withdrawal can increase. When a plant goes offline, the shoreline target can yield inside a defined range instead of drifting as an accident.

The Salton Sea has spent a century being whatever remained after other water decisions were made. A managed regional reservoir would reverse that order. The Sea would have a level, a salt budget, an inlet standard, an outlet, a waste plan, a power plan, customers, and a binational operating agreement.

The machinery is real. The arithmetic closes under declared assumptions. The salt floor is enormous. The law is unfinished. The ecology is not a single number. Mexico's consent is foundational. Flood history rules out nostalgia as a target.

That is not a reason to stop at another attractive pipeline drawing. It is a reason to build the pilot that can fail honestly.
