I found [Future Carriage](/workshop/future-carriage/) by accident.

I was wandering through [PICRYL](https://picryl.com/media/design-for-4-seat-phaeton-no-top-no-4033-8d1316) and landed in a large collection of carriage drawings from Brewster & Company. They were presentation drawings, technical studies, and custom designs from a period when a vehicle could be almost any shape as long as a horse could pull it.

One of them was [No. 4033](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/378903), a four-seat phaeton drawn in 1889 by Herman Stahmer. The bottom of the sheet carries a handwritten note: “Special for Mr. A. C. Harrison of Philadelphia,” followed by “May ’89.” The carriage has four enormous wheels, two elevated seating areas, a thin open body, a central lantern, exposed suspension, and almost none of the visual mass associated with a modern car. The Metropolitan Museum of Art identifies the drawing as pen and ink with watercolor and gouache. William Brewster donated it with the company archive in 1923.

The drawing already looks strange enough to be futuristic. It simply belongs to a future imagined before the automobile settled transportation into a handful of familiar shapes.

## Remove the horse and keep the vehicle

I started studying the drawings as design systems.

The wheel count mattered. The seating arrangement mattered. The lamps, suspension, shafts, body curves, and relationship between the passenger compartment and the pulling mechanism all mattered. I gave myself a basic rule: preserve the recognizable operating logic of the source, then replace horse power with electric, robotic, or autonomous propulsion.

The historical number becomes the future model name.

Four-wheel carriages can carry motors, batteries, sensors, steering, and autonomous control inside the vehicle. Two-wheel forms retain their pulling geometry and use a robotic draft unit in place of the horse. The complete source-to-concept method is documented on the [Future Carriage Workshop page](/workshop/future-carriage/).

Model 4033 became an empty autonomous carriage on a scenic road. The new version keeps the two raised seating areas, the tall wheel scale, the lantern, and the peculiar open body. Small sensor housings sit above the seats. Electric components occupy the understructure. The result looks like an expensive vehicle from a company with an extremely specific idea of luxury.

That was the first stage of the project: take the drawing seriously enough that the future version still belongs to it.

## Put somebody next to it

After the carriage worked, I applied Ami, my avatar and commercial presenter.

A person changes the image immediately. The vehicle acquires scale. The empty product study becomes an advertisement, a fictional service, or a frame from a story in which these vehicles already exist.

Current image-generation systems can place Ami beside a carriage fairly well. Sitting inside one remains a mess. The model has to understand the seat depth, side panels, hips, legs, dress, footwell, and all the places where the body should disappear behind the vehicle. It usually solves five of those things and quietly loses her feet.

Passengers can wait until next year.

For now, Ami can stand beside the vehicle, approach it at a lodge, wait at a scenic stop, or present it in a fake luxury campaign. The carriage can travel empty through the landscape. Since it is autonomous, an empty moving carriage is one of the few continuity errors that becomes part of the concept.

## The sleigh found its route

Then I found the sleighs.

[Brewster & Company’s No. 3415](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/379297) is a four-seat sleigh dated 1878. The original drawing uses two passenger compartments arranged around a deep curved body, with runners underneath and ornamental curls at the ends. The Met lists it as pen and ink with watercolor, gouache, and gum arabic.

The Future Carriage version replaces the runners and horse with a low tracked undercarriage. The batteries and motors sit close to the ground. The body stays open, upholstered, and slightly ridiculous. Lanterns remain part of the visual identity even after becoming lights and sensor housings.

That image gave the concept a route, a season, and a reason to exist.

A tracked electric sleigh makes sense on a groomed resort loop, a snow-covered park road, a private winter trail, or the route between a lodge and a scenic overlook. Low speed is expected. A fixed route is normal. The ride itself is the attraction. Heated seats and blankets would contribute more to the experience than another hundred miles of battery range.

I started this as a visual experiment. The sleigh made me wonder whether the underlying idea was actually good.

## The machinery already exists

Zoox provided the useful receipt.

In September 2025, [Zoox launched its purpose-built robotaxi service on and around the Las Vegas Strip](https://zoox.com/journal/las-vegas). Its vehicle has no conventional steering wheel or pedals. It uses carriage-style seating, drives in either direction, steers with all four wheels, and [combines lidar, radar, cameras, and long-wave infrared sensing](https://zoox.com/journal/three-ways-zoox-reinventing-road-safety) around the vehicle.

A Zoox vehicle looks nothing like Model 4033. The engineering permission is the same.

Removing the human driver removes the driver’s station. The dashboard, forward-facing control position, pedals, steering column, and fixed front end stop defining the entire object. The vehicle can be organized around passengers, doors, visibility, maneuverability, and the operating environment.

Future Carriage uses that freedom to recover an old vehicle form.

A real version would still be a serious machine. It would need electric propulsion, steer-by-wire control, redundant braking, 360-degree perception, onboard computing, detailed route maps, remote fleet supervision, accessible boarding, weather protection, charging, maintenance, and a safe response when any part of that stack fails.

The beautiful body is the easy part.

A limited route makes the rest more manageable. A scenic carriage could run a few mapped roads at low speed, stop at known pullouts, return to the same depot, and charge between service periods. Its operating domain would be far smaller than a city robotaxi network.

The road would still contain pedestrians, cyclists, wildlife, rain, fog, snow, fallen branches, emergency vehicles, and tourists who step into traffic because they found a better angle for a picture. Autonomy does not remove the park from the park.

## A park makes the ride the point

Traffic congestion is already a [system-wide National Park Service management problem](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/transportation/congestion-management.htm). NPS identifies effects on safety, visitor experience, natural resources, emergency response, park operations, and nearby gateway communities. Its congestion program includes reservations, changes to road and parking management, new transportation services, and other site-specific tools.

Acadia is the first place I picture because I know it.

Cadillac Summit Road winds roughly three miles to the 1,530-foot summit. Acadia now receives more than four million visits a year, with visitation up almost 60 percent over a decade. During the 2026 season, [vehicles require reservations from May 20 through October 25](https://www.nps.gov/acad/planyourvisit/vehicle_reservations.htm). The Island Explorer shuttle does not serve the summit road.

That is an unusually easy scene to imagine.

Leave the ordinary car farther down. Board a small open electric carriage. Ride slowly up the mountain. Stop at a pullout. Look at the scenery instead of the rear bumper ahead. Reach the summit without adding another rental SUV to the parking lot.

I would travel for that.

I would also take one through giant sequoias, across a Joshua Tree desert road, under the redwoods near Mendocino, or along the Kancamagus Highway during fall. A tracked Model 3415 could run from a mountain lodge into a winter landscape.

These places function here as imaginary routes. There is no procurement plan, startup deck, or claim that a particular park should order a fleet of Victorian robotaxis.

The exercise asks a simpler question: what kind of vehicle would make the road itself worth riding?

A normal shuttle treats the route as the space between destinations. A Future Carriage treats movement through the landscape as the destination.

## The Park Service already tried the plain version

The National Park Service has already tested electric automated shuttles in park settings.

In 2021, pilots at Yellowstone National Park and Wright Brothers National Memorial carried more than 13,400 riders across more than 3,300 trips. Survey results showed high overall satisfaction. Most riders used the shuttles because the experience sounded fun, rather than because they needed transportation to a particular destination.

That may be the most relevant result in the entire report.

The ride itself attracted people.

[The pilots also exposed the real machinery](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/76562/dot_76562_DS1.pdf). Yellowstone expected 100 operating days and received 74 days with some level of service. Weather, battery problems, vegetation, pedestrians, and other vehicles caused suspensions and disengagements. At Wright Brothers, the shuttle averaged about 5.2 miles per hour. Roadside weeds interfered with lidar. Heavy rain and ponding interrupted service. Battery use increased with air conditioning, forcing midday charging and reducing operating time.

The vehicles worked often enough to carry thousands of people and failed often enough to reveal the maintenance, supervision, weather, and operating-domain problems.

NPS continued the experiment with [an autonomous bus pilot at Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore in 2024](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/transportation/innovation.htm). Its current transportation program describes automated shuttles and other emerging mobility systems as technologies worth continuing to test rather than finished answers ready for every park.

Future Carriage inherits that reality. A real fleet would need attendants, remote operators, fallback procedures, conventional recovery vehicles, route maintenance, and a clear rule for shutting down when conditions exceed the system’s capabilities.

The carriage body does not solve any of that.

It does give people a better reason to get on.

## The old drawings still have somewhere to go

Future Carriage remains design fiction with receipts.

The Workshop page records the project method, model families, historical sources, image studies, Ami’s presenter role, and the rules that keep each new design tied to its original drawing. This article follows the idea outside the Workshop and into a possible world where one of the vehicles actually moves.

<CarriageWorkshopCallout />

The old Brewster drawings are useful because they preserve a larger vocabulary of transportation forms. They were created before engines, dashboards, crash structures, highway aerodynamics, and human driving controls organized nearly every passenger vehicle around the same basic geometry.

Electric propulsion removes the horse. Autonomous control removes the driver’s position. A narrow operating domain removes the requirement to behave like a universal road vehicle.

The archive becomes a design library again.

Maybe no one will ever build Model 4033. The tracked Model 3415 may remain a very elaborate picture of a sleigh that does not exist. That is fine. I found a forgotten drawing, moved it into another century, and ended up with a ride I would genuinely like to take.

The Met preserved the drawings. PICRYL put them in front of me. Image generation gave them another life. Zoox and the National Park Service made the machinery plausible.

I still want the ride.
