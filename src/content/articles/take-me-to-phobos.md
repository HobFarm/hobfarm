I was finishing an article about the electronic comic book in *Big* when one of the citations failed inspection.

ChatGPT had given me a Computer History Museum page that supposedly documented Infocom’s InfoComics line. It looked legitimate. The museum was real, Infocom was real, and the page contained the word “Infocomics.” This is usually how a bad citation survives. Everything surrounding it feels correct.

Codex opened the actual record and found something else.

The page catalogued a 1988 package of *Leather Goddesses of Phobos*. The box contained a floppy disk, instruction manual, map, hint booklet, scratch-and-sniff card, warranty form, advertising, and one InfoComics advertisement. The ad proved that somebody had placed an InfoComics flyer inside the package. It did not prove the larger claim attached to it. ([Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/collections/catalog/102720176))

The citation had to come out.

Unfortunately, I had now seen the words *Leather Goddesses of Phobos*.

The correct source turned out to be Infocom’s own Summer 1988 newsletter, *The Status Line*. That issue introduced new InfoComics, explained the company’s uneasy move toward graphics, sold *Leather Goddesses* as a budget “Solid Gold” release, printed reader arguments about nerds and computer games, and included a mail-order catalog full of fantasy, murder, politics, horror, romance, espionage, sex comedy, wordplay, and Douglas Adams. ([The Status Line, Summer 1988](https://ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/NZT%2BTSL/TSL72.pdf))

I had been researching one imaginary electronic comic from a 1988 movie. The bad source dropped me into a much larger history that I knew in pieces but had never assembled.

Behind Phobos was Infocom.

Behind Infocom was *Zork*.

Behind *Zork* was *Colossal Cave Adventure*.

Underneath *Adventure* was a real cave in Kentucky, a tabletop game called *Dungeons & Dragons*, a PDP-10 mainframe, a primitive network, and two programmers who are still around to receive some overdue credit.

Then the trail moved forward again, through personal computers, modems, bulletin boards, shareware, and eventually *Doom*.

This is the computer-game history that happened before most people think the Internet began.

## The Internet Was Small Enough to Draw

The March 1977 ARPANET map looks less like the modern Internet than a wiring diagram for a secret government railway.

It shows universities, research laboratories, military sites, and the computers attached to them. There are no websites because the Web does not exist. There is no browser, search engine, app store, Wi-Fi router, or domain-name system in its modern form.

There is already a network.

Users can log into remote machines, transfer files, and send electronic mail between host computers. RFC 733, published in 1977, formally describes text messages passed between users through “electronic mail” on ARPANET. Its addressing system worked through combinations of usernames and host names, the recognizable ancestor of `person@place`. ([RFC Editor](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc733/))

The machines on the map are not personal computers. Many are PDP-10s and related systems serving multiple people at once. A user might sit in another room, another building, or another institution and connect through a terminal.

The terminal is the doorway.

The mainframe is the computer.

The network connects the computers.

That distinction matters because saying somebody “played a computer game in 1977” creates the wrong mental picture. They were not necessarily sitting in front of a self-contained desktop machine. They might have been typing into a video terminal connected to a refrigerator-sized installation shared with researchers, programmers, administrators, and whoever else had an account.

The PDP-10 was built for interactive timesharing. Many users could run programs through terminals while the machine divided its attention among them. That made it a natural home for software that behaved conversationally, including early games. *Colossal Cave Adventure* was written in FORTRAN for a PDP-10 timesharing system. ([Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/computer-games/16/190))

Some terminals had screens. Others were teleprinters, essentially networked electric typewriters that printed the machine’s replies onto a roll of paper.

<figure class="article-wide">
  <img src="https://cdn.hob.farm/cdn-cgi/image/format=auto,quality=82,width=1280/articles/take-me-to-phobos/teleprinter.jpg" alt="A teleprinter terminal with a keyboard and a roll of printed paper." width="1280" height="960" loading="lazy" decoding="async" data-lightbox="https://cdn.hob.farm/articles/take-me-to-phobos/teleprinter.jpg" data-lightbox-caption="On a teleprinter, the game transcript became a physical object. Every command and response accumulated on paper." />

The first game screen could be a roll of paper piling up beside the player.

The computer printed a description.

The player typed a command.

The computer printed the result.

That was enough to build a world.

## A Real Cave Becomes Data

William Crowther was a programmer at Bolt, Beranek and Newman, one of the companies deeply involved in ARPANET’s development. He was also a serious caver who had helped survey parts of the Mammoth Cave system in Kentucky.

During the mid-1970s, Crowther combined his experience underground with ideas from the recently published *Dungeons & Dragons*. He created a program that would be accessible to nontechnical players, including his daughters. The game described a cave in text and accepted short commands such as directions, actions, and object names. ([Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/computer-games/16/190))

This was *Adventure*, later known as *Colossal Cave Adventure*.

<figure class="article-wide">
  <img src="https://cdn.hob.farm/cdn-cgi/image/format=auto,quality=82,width=1280/articles/take-me-to-phobos/colossal-cave-adventure.png" alt="Opening text from Colossal Cave Adventure explaining that the program knows about places, actions, and things." width="668" height="501" loading="lazy" decoding="async" data-lightbox="https://cdn.hob.farm/articles/take-me-to-phobos/colossal-cave-adventure.png" data-lightbox-caption="The Crowther and Woods version introduces itself as a program that knows about places, actions, and things." />
  <figcaption>The Crowther and Woods version introduces itself as a program that knows about places, actions, and things.</figcaption>
</figure>

People often describe these games as early “choose your own adventure” programs, which is close enough to explain the general idea but wrong about the mechanism.

A printed branching book gives the reader a visible menu:

> Enter the cave, turn to page 31.
> Return home, turn to page 12.

*Adventure* asks the player to invent the command:

```text
EAST
GET LAMP
OPEN GRATE
INVENTORY
THROW AXE
```

The program tracks the player’s location, the locations of objects, inventory, puzzle conditions, events, and the state of the world. It interprets the command, changes its data, and prints a response.

Crowther’s recovered original exists as two FORTRAN files, one containing code and another containing game data. The data file is organized into tables for room descriptions, short labels, map connections, vocabulary, static states, hints, and events. The cave was geography translated into a primitive world database with a language interface. ([Digital Humanities Quarterly](https://0j2zj3i75g.unbox.ifarchive.org/0j2zj3i75g/Article.html))

The computer could not understand anything a human dungeon master might understand. Its vocabulary was limited. Its responses were authored. Its parser could be stubborn, literal, and easily confused.

That stubbornness became part of the game.

You told the machine what you wanted.

The machine misunderstood.

You changed the wording.

Eventually the world moved.

This basic loop still feels familiar.

  <figcaption>The cave rendered as lines of text on a DEC VT100 terminal.</figcaption>
</figure>

Crowther’s work supplied one of the major root systems of the modern computer RPG and adventure game. It did not single-handedly invent the entire form. Other programmers were already translating D&D statistics, combat, characters, and dungeons into computers. PLATO users were building graphical and multiplayer fantasy games. Wargames contributed their own rules and simulations.

*Adventure* supplied something especially durable: a navigable imaginary place containing objects that could be examined, carried, used, and changed.

The player was inside the data.

## Don Woods Searches the Whole Network

One of the people who encountered *Adventure* was Don Woods, a Stanford graduate student.

Woods and another student reportedly stayed up through the night playing the game, mapping it, and scanning its compiled data for clues. Woods wanted the source code, but he did not know where Crowther had gone or which computer held his account. ([Digital Humanities Quarterly](https://0j2zj3i75g.unbox.ifarchive.org/0j2zj3i75g/Article.html))

He used the network.

The common version of the story says Woods sent electronic mail to `crowther` at every domain then in existence. “Domain” is slightly misleading to a modern reader because today’s Domain Name System did not exist yet. The practical action was still astonishingly direct: try the username on the available ARPANET hosts until one of the messages reaches the correct person. ([Digital Humanities Quarterly](https://0j2zj3i75g.unbox.ifarchive.org/0j2zj3i75g/Article.html))

The Internet was small enough to search by emailing every computer on it.

Crowther responded and gave Woods access to the code.

Woods expanded the game. He added more fantasy, puzzles, treasures, object states, a scoring system, a dragon, a pirate who steals from the player, and many additional locations. His 1977 version became the widely distributed form most people remember as *Colossal Cave Adventure*. ([Colossal Cave Adventure history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossal_Cave_Adventure))

Crowther had been interested in turning the feeling and geography of caving into a computer environment.

Woods was interested in making it a larger game.

That tension, place versus play, still sits underneath world design. A convincing environment needs rules. A good game needs reasons to interfere with them.

The code spread through the network. Programmers copied it, ported it, modified it, and built variants. A personal project for Crowther’s daughters became shared cultural material before the software industry had settled on modern language for open source, modding, remakes, or community preservation.

## Zork Makes the Cave Smarter

When *Adventure* reached MIT in early 1977, it damaged productivity exactly as a good computer game should.

An Infocom history published in *The New Zork Times* says the game “swept the ARPAnet.” People solved it, examined it, and started arguing about how to build something better. They disliked its limited parser, the difficulty of changing its FORTRAN code, and some of its puzzle design. ([Interactive Fiction Archive](https://ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/articles/NZT-Zorkhistory.txt))

Tim Anderson, Marc Blank, Bruce Daniels, and Dave Lebling began constructing a new underground world in a language called MUDDLE, later known as MDL. They drew maps, designed puzzles, wrote a more capable command parser, and built what became *Zork*. ([Interactive Fiction Archive](https://ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/articles/NZT-Zorkhistory.txt))

*Adventure* turned a cave into data.

*Zork* tried to make the data understand better sentences.

The map makes the achievement easier to understand. A terminal displayed one room at a time, but the game maintained a connected world of rivers, reservoirs, mines, banks, temples, passages, puzzles, creatures, treasures, and changing conditions.

The player saw prose.

The program contained architecture.

The mainframe version became too large for the first generation of home computers. That problem helped produce Infocom’s most important technical invention.

## The Computer That Never Existed

The home-computer market was a mess of incompatible machines.

Apple II, TRS-80, Commodore, Atari, IBM, Amiga, Macintosh, and other platforms used different processors, operating systems, memory layouts, graphics hardware, and disk formats. Rewriting every story separately for every machine would have consumed the company.

Infocom built an imaginary computer instead.

The Z-machine was a virtual machine designed to run compiled story files. Infocom could create a game for the Z-machine, then build a smaller interpreter for each real computer. The story remained largely the same. The interpreter translated it for the local hardware.

```text
One story file
      ↓
Apple II Z-machine interpreter

The same story file
      ↓
Commodore Z-machine interpreter

The same story file
      ↓
IBM PC Z-machine interpreter
```

The solution separated the fictional world from the physical machine displaying it.

Infocom closed its original operation in 1989, but the imaginary computer survived. The interactive-fiction community documented the architecture, standardized it, preserved interpreters, and continued writing new stories for it. The Interactive Fiction Technology Foundation now holds the community Z-machine specification. Modern Inform projects can still compile to Z-code and package the result with Parchment, a JavaScript interpreter that runs the game inside a contemporary browser. ([Interactive Fiction Technology Foundation](https://blog.iftechfoundation.org/2018-11-20-on-the-treaty-of-babel.html))

A current Z-machine game can therefore follow this path:

```text
Write an interactive story
        ↓
Compile it to Z-code
        ↓
Load it into a JavaScript interpreter
        ↓
Play it on a website
```

The PDP-10 is gone from normal life.

Infocom is gone as a functioning studio.

The fictional computer between the story and the hardware is still accepting commands.

## These Games Were Made for Nerds

The public memory of early games tends to center on arcades.

Arcade history has cabinets, quarters, high scores, recognizable graphics, public competitions, and players standing where a camera can film them. The computer branch developed inside labs, universities, offices, hobby clubs, and homes. Its important events often looked like people typing into terminals after midnight.

The audience was different too.

Early computer games were not automatically children’s entertainment. Access alone selected for adults, university students, engineers, programmers, hobbyists, science-fiction readers, tabletop players, and anyone obsessive enough to learn how an expensive machine wanted to be addressed.

These were nerd games in the most productive sense.

They expected players to draw maps, take notes, understand rules, experiment with syntax, remember obscure clues, inspect packaging, and spend days solving a problem that could have been explained in one sentence by the author.

The arcade often asked how quickly you could react.

The computer game asked whether you were sufficiently fixated to understand its system.

Infocom embraced that audience. Its 1988 catalog included fantasy epics, locked-room mysteries, Cold War espionage, political speculative fiction, horror, pirate romance, science fiction, wordplay, Douglas Adams comedy, software about hacking, and games whose marketing openly bragged about difficult puzzles.

Steve Meretzky’s work alone covered an absurd range. *Planetfall* was comic science fiction. *The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy* adapted Douglas Adams into a hostile interactive system. *A Mind Forever Voyaging* placed the player inside future simulations to test a government plan. *Leather Goddesses of Phobos* was a pulp sex farce offering tame, suggestive, and lewd settings.

Early computer games had not learned that they were supposed to behave like toys. They acted like novels, political arguments, engineering projects, tabletop campaigns, dirty jokes, and private obsessions running on shared machinery.

## The Box Goes to Phobos

*Leather Goddesses of Phobos* was released in 1986, written by Meretzky and powered by the same virtual-machine lineage developed to bring *Zork* onto home computers.

Its premise begins in Upper Sandusky, Ohio, in 1936. The player is abducted into a pulp-space plot involving alien women who intend to convert Earth into their private pleasure world. The player could select a gender and choose among three levels of “naughtiness.” Infocom’s own 1988 newsletter insisted that the game was not X-rated, placing it somewhere around PG-13 or R despite the title and advertising. ([The Status Line, Summer 1988](https://ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/NZT%2BTSL/TSL72.pdf))

The package extended the game beyond the screen.

It included a 3D Lane Mastodon comic, red-and-blue glasses, a double-sided map, and a numbered scratch-and-sniff card. At certain points the program instructed the player to smell a particular section. The computer could not produce an odor, so Infocom printed one and placed it in the box. ([Leather Goddesses of Phobos history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leather_Goddesses_of_Phobos))

The game had no graphical world, so the box supplied a comic.

It had no smell engine, so the box supplied smells.

It could not place a physical map on the desk, so the package carried one outside the machine.

Infocom called such objects “feelies.” They performed several jobs at once: puzzle delivery, copy protection, comedy, worldbuilding, marketing, and tactile participation.

Before games could render everything, Infocom mailed the missing senses to the player.

The 1988 Computer History Museum package contained something else: an InfoComics advertisement.

That insignificant flyer was the object ChatGPT mistook for evidence.

## “Change Is Inevitable. Don’t Panic!”

The correct InfoComics receipt was Infocom’s Summer 1988 newsletter.

The front page announced, “Change is inevitable. Don’t panic!” Infocom told its readers that customer surveys showed growing demand for maps, character charts, color, animation, and graphics. The company promised to retain the prose, puzzles, and storytelling associated with its text games while moving into graphical adventures, role-playing games, and other interactive forms. ([The Status Line, Summer 1988](https://ifarchive.org/if-archive/infocom/NZT%2BTSL/TSL72.pdf))

The newsletter captures a company standing between eras.

In 1980, limited memory and crude graphics had made text a strength. By 1988, Macintosh, Amiga, Atari ST, Apple IIGS, IBM-compatible, and other systems could display richer graphics and hold more data. Infocom’s catalog listed different memory requirements for each platform, demonstrating exactly why portable software systems such as the Z-machine had mattered.

InfoComics tried another approach to electronic storytelling. The programs contained illustrated stories that could be paused, rewound, fast-forwarded, and explored from different character viewpoints. Readers could move into memories, thoughts, dreams, and subplots, but the routes eventually reached the same ending.

That distinction is what I had been trying to explain in the *Big* article.

Josh Baskin’s electronic comic imagines consequential branching. Press one button and a different story appears.

InfoComics offered branching perspective. The story remained fixed, but the reader changed routes through it.

*Adventure* and *Zork* offered another model. The player acted inside a simulated environment and changed its state through commands.

These systems were all trying to answer the same larger question:

What happens when a story begins listening to the reader?

## The Computer Comes Home

While *Adventure* and the first *Zork* were spreading through institutional computers, hobbyists were trying to put comparable power into machines a person could own.

Steve Wozniak built the Apple I around an inexpensive 6502 processor and designed it to work with a keyboard and video display. It was an assembled circuit board rather than the complete consumer appliance now suggested by the word “computer.” The owner still needed supporting hardware, including a display, keyboard, power supply, and usually some kind of enclosure. ([Computer History Museum](https://computerhistory.org/blog/steve-jobs/))

Wozniak built the machine to demonstrate at the Homebrew Computer Club. Steve Jobs recognized that people might pay for assembled boards.

Jobs showed the Apple I to Paul Terrell, owner of the Byte Shop, who ordered 50 assembled units. Apple did not have enough cash to buy all the required components. Wozniak sold his HP-65 calculator, Jobs sold his Volkswagen van, and Jobs used the Byte Shop order to negotiate 30 days of credit from parts suppliers. They could receive the components, build the boards, deliver the order, collect payment, and pay the supplier before the bill came due. ([Computer History Museum](https://computerhistory.org/blog/steve-jobs/))

The garage mattered.

The purchase order mattered more.

The Apple I did not immediately replace institutional computing, and Apple was only one part of a much larger microcomputer movement. It does illustrate the central transition. Computing was escaping the computer center. The terminal, processor, storage, and display were slowly collapsing into something that could sit in a house.

The next step was reconnecting those houses.

## Many Little Internets

The early online world was never one clean line from ARPANET to the Web.

PLATO developed as a centralized educational system at the University of Illinois, but its users created forums, messaging, chat, multiplayer games, and persistent communities. Games such as *dnd*, *Empire*, and *Avatar* built social and immersive digital environments during the 1970s. At its peak, gaming consumed a substantial portion of system use. ([Illinois Distributed Museum](https://distributedmuseum.illinois.edu/exhibit/plato_impacts/))

MUD1, created by Roy Trubshaw and Richard Bartle at the University of Essex, brought multiple people into the same textual adventure world. The Computer History Museum dates its online emergence to 1979 and reports that more than 100 MUDs and variants were active by 1984. ([Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/1979/))

Then there were bulletin board systems.

Ward Christensen and Randy Suess launched CBBS in Chicago in 1978. A caller used a modem and ordinary telephone line to connect directly to another computer. Early systems commonly handled one caller at a time. Users left messages, traded software, downloaded files, adopted handles, and gradually constructed local online cultures. ([Wired](https://www.wired.com/2010/02/0216cbbs-first-bbs-bulletin-board/))

A BBS was usually not part of the Internet. It was a small independent world with a phone number.

Thousands of those worlds appeared.

Some specialized in programming. Others carried games, art, politics, piracy, local gossip, role-playing, adult material, or whatever the operator wanted to host. Door games let users leave the board’s central menu and enter multiplayer or persistent games. FidoNet and other systems later allowed separate boards to exchange messages.

BBS culture recreated many network behaviors using personal computers and telephone lines:

* usernames and reputations
* public posts and private mail
* communities organized around interests
* downloadable software
* amateur publishing
* moderation by a system operator
* local celebrities and arguments
* games that continued while the player was away

Infocom’s *Status Line* resembles a paper version of the same culture. It contains reader mail, inside jokes, lore disputes, puzzles, contest entries, technical requirements, product listings, and community members talking back to the company and one another. The conversation traveled through postal mail instead of a modem, but the social mechanism is recognizable.

By the late 1980s and early 1990s, BBSs, commercial online services, Usenet, FTP servers, university networks, and local computer clubs formed an overlapping distribution system for software.

Then shareware turned copying into a business plan.

## Doom at the Mouth of the Cave

Shareware encouraged users to copy and distribute part of a program freely. Developers sold additional episodes or the full registered version directly to players.

The distribution network was already waiting: BBS file libraries, FTP sites, Usenet groups, copied disks, and friends with computers.

On December 10, 1993, the shareware version of *Doom* was uploaded to an FTP server at the University of Wisconsin. It spread through the same decentralized culture that had been trading programs and game files for years. Players ran it on increasingly powerful home PCs, connected machines for multiplayer games, and began altering its levels and appearance through mods. ([DoomWiki](https://doomwiki.org/wiki/Doom))

*Doom* was not an RPG. It did carry forward parts of the dungeon tradition.

The player moved through connected rooms and passages, searched for keys, managed resources, opened sealed areas, learned the map, and survived encounters with monsters. The text descriptions were replaced by a fast rendered viewpoint. The typed parser was replaced by immediate physical control.

The cave had become visible.

The same year, NCSA Mosaic helped move the World Wide Web from a research system toward mass use by making graphical pages easier to access on Unix, Macintosh, and PC systems. ([Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/1993/))

That coincidence makes 1993 a useful stopping point.

The Web begins becoming the public image of the Internet.

*Doom* becomes the public image of PC gaming.

Neither appeared from nowhere.

Behind them were decades of remote terminals, shared mainframes, network mail, computer clubs, D&D campaigns, cave maps, parsers, MUDs, BBSs, downloadable software, virtual machines, and obsessive people making expensive equipment do things it had not been purchased to do.

My next article picks up on the other side of *Doom*, when this underground computer culture becomes the Internet and gaming world more people remember.

This one ends at the entrance.

## The Wrong Passage Was Still a Passage

I started with a bad citation in an article about a fictional electronic comic.

The citation led to a package of *Leather Goddesses of Phobos*. The package led to Infocom’s newsletter. The newsletter led to the Z-machine. The Z-machine led to *Zork*. *Zork* led to *Adventure*. *Adventure* led to Dungeons & Dragons, a real Kentucky cave, a PDP-10, ARPANET, and Don Woods searching the entire network for a programmer named Crowther.

Then the path climbed back out through the Apple I, modems, BBSs, shareware, and *Doom*.

The AI failure was ordinary. A system found a respectable page containing the correct word and attached it to the wrong claim.

The correction was more interesting. Codex inspected the receipt, noticed that it proved something else, and opened a trapdoor into the history beneath it.

That history also explains why I keep thinking about old interactive fiction while building a new game. Graphics can carry the surface. The world still needs geography, objects, language, rules, memory, and consequences. The player needs to be able to interfere and receive an answer.

Crowther began with a place he understood.

Woods made it behave more like a game.

Infocom built a machine that could carry those worlds between incompatible computers.

The network carried everything else.

The bad citation came out.

Phobos stayed.

And underneath Phobos, the cave was still running.
