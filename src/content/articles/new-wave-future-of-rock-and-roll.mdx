## You’re the guy from the hamburger train, right?

Paul Reubens is sitting at a restaurant table in *Cheech & Chong’s Nice Dreams*, looking considerably more invested in the future of rock and roll than anybody around him.

Howie has been talking. Tommy Chong wants the check. The conversation slides past a little cocaine under the table, but that is not what Howie has decided is historically urgent. He leans in and tries again.

<div className="new-wave-transcript" aria-label="Short author-supplied transcript from the Nice Dreams restaurant scene">
  <p><strong>HOWIE</strong><span>Bruce Springsteen’s fucking the whole thing up.</span></p>
  <p><strong>CHONG</strong><span>Bruce Springsteen?</span></p>
  <p><strong>HOWIE</strong><span>New Wave.</span></p>
  <p><strong>CHONG</strong><span>New Wave?</span></p>
  <p><strong>HOWIE</strong><span>New Wave!</span></p>
</div>

Chong answers the manifesto by asking whether Howie has any of that for sale. Howie says he sells seashells. The waiter finally arrives. The movie leaves the argument on the table.

The scene appears roughly nine minutes into the 1981 film, and AFI credits Reubens as Howie Hamburger Dude. The available production record says the screenplay was only five pages long and was intended to guide improvisation, which tells us something about how loose the production could be. It does not establish who wrote or improvised this particular line.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

Then there is the receipt sitting on Primus’s own record. “Hamburger Train,” from *Pork Soda* in 1993, begins with Reubens’s Howie asking, “You’re the guy from the hamburger train, right?” The audio comes from this restaurant scene. Primus returned to this story twice: through covers and through the scene itself. The band sampled the doorway that starts the article.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-36" aria-label="Source 36">36</a></sup>

Forty-five years later, the question is worth taking seriously. Why did New Wave sound like the future in 1981? Why was Springsteen the obstacle? And why does the person delivering the speech keep turning up near Devo, Oingo Boingo, film scores, constructed characters, and the comedy institutions that moved all of those things onto American screens?

## Paul Reubens did not arrive from nowhere

Reubens was already building Pee-wee Herman through the Groundlings before *Nice Dreams*. The troupe’s own history places *The Pee-Wee Herman Show* in 1981 as its first alternative-format production, assembled from characters developed in workshops and revues. The cast included Phil Hartman, John Paragon, Lynne Stewart, Edi McClurg, and John Moody.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

He had also carried Pee-wee into *Cheech & Chong’s Next Movie* in 1980. AFI’s production history describes director Tommy Chong casting Groundlings performers after seeing the troupe. That is a specific institutional route, not a claim that every performer at the Groundlings shared the same taste in records.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

Hartman makes the route denser. The Groundlings record his membership from 1975 to 1986, his Captain Carl role in the original Pee-wee show and *Pee-wee’s Playhouse*, and his co-writing credit on *Pee-wee’s Big Adventure* with Reubens. If you followed Hartman through the preceding HobFarm article, [the comedy network around *Ghostbusters*](/articles/print-is-dead/) already showed what these shared rooms could do. Here I only need one part of that map: Reubens and Hartman were developing characters together before the movie and television branches spread.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

Then the music links become unusually literal.

Mark Mothersbaugh recalled meeting Reubens while both worked on the 1980 film *Pray TV*. Reubens later approached him about scoring *Pee-wee’s Big Adventure*. Mothersbaugh could not do it because Devo was touring, so Danny Elfman got the job. Mothersbaugh did take on music for *Pee-wee’s Playhouse*, work he said changed the direction of his career.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

Elfman’s version of the other half is just as direct. Reubens knew the Oingo Boingo film *Forbidden Zone*, heard Elfman’s work there, and insisted that he score *Pee-wee’s Big Adventure* even though Elfman did not yet consider himself a film composer.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-7" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

Howie is a character. The scene does not prove that Reubens was sneaking his personal record collection into the screenplay. The interpretation is narrower and more useful: the line fits the performance culture Reubens occupied, where New Wave music, comedy, theater, film, television, and a deliberately constructed public identity kept sharing rooms.

Pee-wee is useful here because he was built rather than confessed. The too-small suit, the voice, the gestures, the immaculate cheer, and the moments of menace made identity into a complete production. Devo and Oingo Boingo were not doing the same act, but they also rejected the idea that a band should appear as an unprocessed set of authentic men. Uniforms, characters, film, graphics, slogans, and staging could be part of the work rather than packaging placed around it. Reubens belonged to a Los Angeles performance world in which that proposition already made sense.

That is why Howie’s line can be more than an accidental period reference without becoming a confession from the actor. It catches a live argument about what counted as rock, what counted as performance, and whether visible construction was fraud or freedom. The people around Reubens would spend the next several years demonstrating that construction could travel very well.

## What future was Howie seeing?

In 1981, New Wave could cover Talking Heads, Devo, XTC, Blondie, Japan, Oingo Boingo, synth-pop, post-punk neighbors, and bands that shared clothing or venues while sounding almost nothing alike. The phrase had historical force because punk and art rock had broken open the specification of a rock band. It becomes less useful the moment it is treated like a laboratory definition.

Talking Heads could turn a song into an interlocking rhythmic system. Devo could make industrial Ohio into conceptual theater. XTC could put mechanical nerves inside a three-minute pop song. Japan could move from guitar-heavy glam into arrangements built around synthesizers, negative space, Mick Karn’s fretless bass, and Steve Jansen’s constructed rhythms. Oingo Boingo could convert Los Angeles theater energy into fast, crowded songs that seemed ready to become movie music.

Springsteen works as Howie’s villain because his line pointed backward as powerfully as theirs pointed forward. R&B and soul, Dylan, Phil Spector, bar bands, saxophone, live-band physicality, American road mythology: Springsteen could gather those histories into a room and make them feel current. *The River* was released on October 17, 1980 and became his first number-one album in the United States. Howie was not complaining about a relic. He was complaining that the rival future was winning.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-28" aria-label="Source 28">28</a></sup>

The joke is even tighter than the movie knew. An archival newspaper index at the Bruce Springsteen Special Collection records a June 6, 1973 notice announcing that Cheech & Chong would headline a Convention Hall bill with Bruce Springsteen. Eight years before Howie declared Springsteen the problem, the two lines had already shared a room.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-30" aria-label="Source 30">30</a></sup>

## The routers

If I have to choose one person as the main router through this particular mesh, I choose Brian Eno. That is my organizing judgment, not a claim that Eno invented New Wave or stands at the root of every electronic record.

The documented routes do enough work. Eno entered public view in Roxy Music. He made records with Robert Fripp. He worked with David Bowie while Bowie and Tony Visconti made *Low* and *Heroes*, and Fripp played the famous sustained guitar parts on *Heroes*. Eno produced Talking Heads and Devo, then continued his collaboration with David Byrne. His official biography gives the network in compressed form; the record credits keep the roles specific.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-8" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup>

The method matters more than the aura. Eno’s background in experimental composition made loops, systems, repetition, chance, tape, and studio construction available as pop procedures. He has repeatedly named the experimental music around John Cage and Cornelius Cardew as part of that education. What crossed into rock was not one recognizable Eno sound. It was permission to treat the studio as an instrument, artificiality as an aesthetic choice, and arrangement as a process that could discover the song instead of merely documenting it.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-9" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

The album sequence shows how portable that permission was. Eno and Fripp could make sustained guitar and tape systems together. Bowie, Visconti, Eno, and their players could apply related procedures to the fractured song forms and instrumentals around *Low* and *Heroes*. Eno could then produce Talking Heads across *More Songs About Buildings and Food*, *Fear of Music*, and *Remain in Light*, while a different set of constraints met Devo on *Q: Are We Not Men? A: We Are Devo!*. The edges are real, but the results do not collapse into one house style.

That distinction matters because “produced by Brian Eno” can become a lazy substitute for listening. Talking Heads brought David Byrne, Chris Frantz, Tina Weymouth, and Jerry Harrison, then built increasingly dense rhythmic structures out of their own band. Devo arrived with its own Akron history, Gerald Casale and Mark Mothersbaugh’s theory, films, uniforms, and songs. Bowie was not an empty screen either, and Tony Visconti’s production work cannot be handed to Eno for convenience. The router moved practices and provocations. The artists decided what survived contact.

Giorgio Moroder is the other great router in this story. “I Feel Love,” made with Donna Summer and Pete Bellotte, put an electronic pulse under almost the entire record and made the machine carry time. The voice floated over a repeating system rather than a conventional rhythm section. Moroder’s later work reaches Japan’s “Life in Tokyo,” Blondie’s “Call Me,” Bowie’s “Cat People,” film scores, and the international soundtrack branch of *The NeverEnding Story*.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

Moroder’s route is less about bringing an art-school exercise into a rock session than about reorganizing the floor underneath popular music. The click, repeating synthesizer figures, and deliberately mechanized pulse of “I Feel Love” made precision feel bodily. That method could support Donna Summer’s suspended vocal, Sparks on *No. 1 in Heaven*, the sleek turn of Japan’s “Life in Tokyo,” or the blunt dramatic function of a movie theme. “Call Me” could be Blondie and a Moroder production at once. “Cat People” could connect him to Bowie without turning him into part of the Bowie-Eno partnership.

The difference between Eno and Moroder is useful, but it should not become another border wall. One route foregrounded systems, treatments, studio process, and collaborative disruption. The other foregrounded sequence, pulse, electronic texture, and the force of an engineered dance floor. Records crossed those tendencies freely. Musicians listened to both. The future sounded persuasive because several technical and aesthetic arguments were arriving at the same time.

Kraftwerk remain a separate major lineage. Their records affected synth-pop, post-punk, dance music, industrial music, hip-hop, and electronic pop without needing to pass through either Eno or Moroder. The point of a mesh is that several routes can remain independent and still change the same future.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-26" aria-label="Source 26">26</a></sup>

## The Atlantic keeps sending the signal back

This music did not travel from a single European laboratory to grateful Americans. The exchange kept reversing direction.

London supplied art school, glam, progressive rock, punk aftermath, producers, musicians, and studios. New York supplied CBGB, visual art, downtown performance, disco, funk, punk, and the band that became Talking Heads. Akron supplied Devo’s industrial satire and theory of de-evolution. Los Angeles supplied the Groundlings, Oingo Boingo, film, television, theatrical performance, and the place where Mothersbaugh and Elfman could become screen composers. Wolperath, near Cologne, supplied Conny Plank’s recording environment. Munich supplied Moroder’s electronic-disco system.

David Byrne makes the exchange personal. He was born in Scotland and raised in the United States before Talking Heads formed in New York. Eno brought a British experimental-production route into the band, while American funk and African rhythmic models moved through *Remain in Light*. The official Talking Heads description itself refuses the clean category, listing rock, funk, punk, world beats, minimalism, and pop in the same record.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-17" aria-label="Source 17">17</a></sup>

Devo make a literal loop. David Bowie expressed interest in producing them. Eno took the production role. The band recorded in the German environment around Conny Plank, with Bowie appearing during the sessions. The participant accounts do not turn Bowie into the album’s producer, and the graphic does not either.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

The record industry liked to sell invasions: Britain conquers America, America answers, Germany supplies a machine. The credits describe something messier. American funk and R&B gave British musicians rhythmic and sonic material. European studios and electronic instruments changed the way American bands could organize that material. Jamaican dub demonstrated that a mix could dismantle and reconstruct a performance. New York clubs put art students, dancers, punks, disco, film, and fashion in physical contact. A map of the era needs arrows pointing both ways because the records already contain return traffic.

The same is true inside cities. “Akron” does not explain Devo, and “New York” does not explain Talking Heads. Place is a set of pressures and available rooms, not a genre. The map marks where a transfer happened or where a scene made it possible. It does not claim that everyone in the city joined the same movement.

## The mesh becomes audible

Talking Heads and Devo show what Eno could carry between very different bands. Talking Heads became more rhythmically layered across the three Eno-produced albums. *Remain in Light* made the construction audible: repeating parts, displaced accents, edited and accumulated performances, songs that seem to operate like systems. Devo’s debut keeps the band’s own clipped attack and conceptual comedy while allowing the German studio and Eno’s methods to intensify it.

On “The Great Curve,” the Talking Heads band does not behave like a singer standing in front of accompaniment. Guitar, bass, drums, percussion, voices, and studio additions keep locking into and slipping across one another. The part is less important than the relationship among parts. Byrne and Eno’s *My Life in the Bush of Ghosts*, released in 1981, pushes further into construction from loops, found and recorded voices, rhythm, and editing. It belongs beside the mesh as another route, not as the secret origin of everything around it.

Devo’s attack is different. “Jocko Homo” makes repetition into an interrogation and a slogan. The stiff changes, group responses, film imagery, and deliberately un-rock presentation belong to a concept the band had developed before Eno recorded them. Production could sharpen the object, but the object had authors. That is exactly why typed edges matter: “produced” says something precise and stops before it becomes “created.”

Neither band became Eno. A useful producer edge records work done on a specific project. It does not erase the band on the other end of the line.

### XTC to Peter Gabriel

The most concrete transfer in the article begins with a production team rather than a genre label.

Steve Lillywhite produced XTC’s *Drums and Wires* in 1979. Hugh Padgham engineered it. Peter Gabriel’s official history says Lillywhite and Padgham arrived for his third album after working with XTC, where Padgham had been experimenting with the SSL console’s gates. Gabriel asked Phil Collins to play a simple pattern on “Intruder,” recorded the room around the drums, and built the song around the huge sound and the abrupt cutoff.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-18" aria-label="Source 18">18</a></sup>

A gate is easier to hear than to mythologize. A room microphone captures the impact and the space around the kit. Compression can make that room surge forward. A noise gate closes the signal when it falls below a threshold, cutting the natural decay. Instead of a drum hit fading into the room, it becomes enormous and then stops unnaturally. That treatment later spread all over 1980s production, but the chain here does not need a universal invention claim. It only needs the documented people and the audible result.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-19" aria-label="Source 19">19</a></sup>

“Intruder” makes the mechanism dramatically useful. The drum does not simply announce expensive production. Its size and amputated tail help create the room in which the narrator moves. Gabriel’s rule against cymbals leaves more air around the hits and makes the kit feel less like a conventional rock performance. The sound is not pasted onto the song after composition; the recording decision becomes part of the composition.

That is the transfer worth preserving. Lillywhite and Padgham do not carry an “XTC sound” into Gabriel’s session like a preset. They carry recent experience, a console, an appetite for a particular effect, and the confidence to build around it. Collins supplies the physical performance. Gabriel supplies the instruction and the song. The room and equipment participate. Once separated into those edges, the story becomes more interesting than a lone-genius invention tale.

### King Crimson rebuilds itself in 1981

Early-1980s King Crimson belongs here through personnel and method, not because *Discipline* sounds like a Talking Heads record. Robert Fripp formed a new quartet with Adrian Belew, Tony Levin, and Bill Bruford. DGM’s official archive notes that this became the first King Crimson lineup to record successive studio albums without changing personnel: *Discipline* in 1981, *Beat* in 1982, and *Three of a Perfect Pair* in 1984.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-20" aria-label="Source 20">20</a></sup>

Belew had worked with Bowie and played on *Remain in Light* and with Talking Heads. Levin had been working with Peter Gabriel since 1977 and brought the Chapman Stick into the new Crimson. Put Fripp’s systems and precision beside Belew’s manipulated guitar, Levin’s Stick, and Bruford’s rhythmic intelligence, and the band becomes another answer to the same question: what else can four people make a rock group do?<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-21" aria-label="Source 21">21</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-22" aria-label="Source 22">22</a></sup>

To my ear, *Discipline* belongs in this route because it distributes attention. Interlocking guitar figures behave like gears whose teeth do not always meet where a rock listener expects. The Stick can carry bass, counterline, or both. Bruford’s part can puncture the grid instead of merely holding it down. Belew can speak, bark, or bend a guitar into an animal. This is not proof of genre membership. It is a description of a band rebuilding the available machinery with personnel already connected to Bowie, Eno, Talking Heads, and Gabriel.

### Japan changes inside the same band

Japan provide a different kind of evidence because the mutation happens without replacing the name on the marquee. The early *Adolescent Sex* and *Obscure Alternatives* records are heavier and more guitar-driven. The Moroder-produced “Life in Tokyo” in 1979 is a turn, but not a magic wand. The band was already moving. *Quiet Life* follows. *Gentlemen Take Polaroids* opens more space. *Tin Drum* in 1981 makes Richard Barbieri’s synthesizer programming, Mick Karn’s fretless bass, and Steve Jansen’s assembled rhythms structural parts of the music.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-23" aria-label="Source 23">23</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-24" aria-label="Source 24">24</a></sup>

Moroder did not single-handedly transform Japan. “Life in Tokyo” is a documented point in a change the band continued to make for itself.

The useful comparison is not “before Moroder” and “after Moroder” as if one producer flipped a switch. It is the sequence of decisions the band kept making. Karn’s bass becomes a lead voice without behaving like a conventional guitar. Barbieri’s synthesizers establish depth and atmosphere. Jansen can construct rhythm out of separate events rather than present a single uninterrupted drum-kit document. Sylvian’s vocal leaves behind some of the early records’ force and uses restraint as tension. By “Ghosts,” much of what a rock arrangement normally uses to reassure the listener has been removed, yet the song remains unmistakably dramatic.

Japan therefore sit at several borders without resolving them: glam residue, art rock, electronic pop, New Romantic image, and a rhythmic vocabulary that does not stay politely European. The mesh records the Moroder production edge and the later technical evidence. It leaves the band’s self-directed mutation intact.

## The future branches

Once the rock-band specification loosened, it did not produce one replacement model. It produced radiation.

The Cure can move from post-punk into a major goth and alternative lineage. London clubs can turn New Romantic music, fashion, makeup, and video into one designed public surface. Synth-pop can put electronic instruments at the center rather than use them as decoration. Industrial scenes can make the machinery harsher. Art-pop can keep changing the relationship between songwriting, character, studio process, and image.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-25" aria-label="Source 25">25</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-27" aria-label="Source 27">27</a></sup>

These are listening directions, not bins. Post-punk can describe what happened when punk’s permission met dub space, funk rhythm, art-school procedure, noise, and political pressure. Goth can name a darker performance and visual lineage without requiring every shadowy record to share a blueprint. Synth-pop can range from severe machine patterns to immaculate romance. New Romantic scenes make the club, clothes, makeup, pose, video, and song one designed event. Industrial music can treat the factory as sound, subject, or hostile environment. Art-pop remains the useful loose term for work that keeps composition and presentation visibly under construction.

Representative names clarify the branches while proving how badly a single taxonomy behaves. The Cure’s route passes through post-punk and goth without ending there. Kraftwerk’s independent electronic lineage feeds synth-pop and industrial practices while also reaching dance music and hip-hop. Japan can be placed near New Romantic culture while sounding unlike its most obvious club hits. Talking Heads can be filed as New Wave, post-punk, art rock, or pop depending on the record and the purpose of the shelf. The overlap is not an error. The overlap is the evidence.

These branches do not share one origin or one sound. They share a historical opening in which instrumentation, rhythm, stage identity, video, clothing, recording, and the definition of a band were all available for reconstruction.

MTV amplified the visual part after its 1981 launch. It did not invent New Wave. By the time the channel went on air, musicians and designers had already built much of the language it could distribute.

## Listen to the inheritance

Primus released the cover EP *Miscellaneous Debris* in 1992 and the cover collection *Rhinoplasty* in 1998. Across those two records, the band covered two songs from XTC’s *Drums and Wires* and two songs from Peter Gabriel’s third and fourth albums.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-34" aria-label="Source 34">34</a></sup>

That does not prove Primus intended a history lecture. It gives us an audible map of ancestry. The pairings are receipts.

## The weird people enter film and television

The Reubens branch comes back because the future of rock escaped the record bin.

Elfman’s Oingo Boingo route became a film-composing career after *Pee-wee’s Big Adventure*. Mothersbaugh’s Devo route opened into television, film, animation, games, and advertising after *Pee-wee’s Playhouse*. Their band histories did not disappear. They became toolkits for a different production system.

The transfer was not simply “rock musician gets orchestra.” Both men had learned to make identity, abrupt shifts, artificial voices, rhythm, and visual timing work together. Reubens’s projects needed music that could understand a constructed world without standing outside it and smirking. Elfman could make *Pee-wee’s Big Adventure* move with manic precision. Mothersbaugh could treat *Playhouse* as a place where a cue, a sound effect, a jingle, and a song shared the same unstable border.

Moroder’s film branch makes the same movement from another direction. “Cat People,” made with Bowie, places the electronic producer beside one of the central figures in the Eno branch. *The NeverEnding Story* gives the international release a Moroder song cycle and the Limahl title hit. The soundtrack itself is credited to Klaus Doldinger and Moroder, so Moroder should not swallow Doldinger’s score in the retelling.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-32" aria-label="Source 32">32</a></sup><sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-33" aria-label="Source 33">33</a></sup>

The experimental vocabulary had become ordinary enough to score a fantasy film, a children’s television show, a studio comedy, and the commercials between them. Howie’s future was no longer a section in the record store. It was part of the room.

## Moroder explains it afterward

Daft Punk returned to this history in 2013 with “Giorgio by Moroder.” Moroder narrates the search for a sound that belonged to the future, and the recording builds the technological history around his voice. The track is retrospective, but it does not feel like a museum label. It lets the old production argument happen again in sound.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-16" aria-label="Source 16">16</a></sup>

## Bruce Springsteen remains a problem

Howie was substantially right about the method and wrong about the casualty.

New Wave did become part of the future of rock and pop. Sequenced pulse, synthesizers as foundations, gated and constructed drums, studio assembly, interlocking repetition, art-directed identity, character, video, and musicians crossing into film and television all spread farther than the label itself.

Springsteen did not stop any of it. He also did not remain sealed inside the counterlineage Howie assigned him. Roy Bittan’s official biography points to the synthesizer riff on “Born in the U.S.A.” and the central keyboard work on “Dancing in the Dark.” Springsteen entered MTV-era visual culture while continuing to carry R&B, soul, Dylan, Spector, bar-band force, saxophone, and the American road with him.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-29" aria-label="Source 29">29</a></sup>

Several futures arrived at once. Some were made with patch cables, tape loops, gates, sequencers, makeup, art-school theories, and musicians turning themselves into screen composers. Another kept a saxophone in the room and filled arenas.

Chong barely heard the manifesto. He wanted the check.

Howie saw the future anyway.
