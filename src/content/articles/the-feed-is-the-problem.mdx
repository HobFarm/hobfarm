I set up an RSS reader because I was tired of looking for interesting things on social media.

I wanted archaeology and ancient civilizations, weird news, artificial-intelligence research, film, old media, science, unusual places, environmental systems, and anything else that might turn into an article. I made groups in Thunderbird and added sources. The point was to choose the publishers before I started reading instead of asking a public feed to reconstruct my interests from whatever made me stop moving my thumb.

I had already worked through the history and mechanics in [Hit the Source Directly](/articles/hit-the-source-directly/). This was the practical version. Build the folders. Subscribe to the sources. See what arrives.

Then Phys.org delivered a headline: [Doomscrolling at work wastes time, but the real cost is what happens after](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-doomscrolling-real.html).

I built the RSS reader so I would not have to look through social media to find interesting things. One of the first interesting things it found was an explanation of why that might have been a pretty good idea.

## The cost after the scroll

The story concerned two studies by Ian Hughes, Melissa Keith, Juseob Lee, and Cheryl Gray. They defined doomscrolling as compulsive browsing through negative information on social media and asked what happened when workers did it on the job.[^doom-paper]

The first study used a time-lagged sample of 352 workers. Participants reported doomscrolling, then reported rumination and work engagement the following day. The second gathered 630 daily observations from 126 workers. Across the two designs, more doomscrolling was associated with more rumination and lower work engagement. In the diary study, the connection between doomscrolling and rumination was stronger for people higher in neuroticism.

This is observational path analysis, not a clinical experiment. It does not show that a feed caused a diagnosis, a prescription, or a long-term illness. The finding is smaller and useful: the scrolling event can end while the psychological event keeps running.

The platform can record that I stopped, read, opened the comments, refreshed, and returned. It can count the session as engagement. My mind may spend the next hour replaying the threat, trying to solve a situation I cannot affect, and failing to return fully to the work in front of me.

Those are two completely different accounting systems.

<FeedFigure kind="accounting" />

The machine closes the event when the screen changes. The human event may continue as worry, distraction, or rumination. That mismatch turned one research story into a larger question about what a feed counts and what it cannot see.

## My first theory was too broad

I had been noticing that therapy sounded more common and medication for mental health sounded more common. Social media became a major public system during the same broad period. My first suspicion was that the feed might help explain the rise.

The treatment increase is real. CDC estimates from the National Health Interview Survey show that the share of U.S. adults reporting any mental-health treatment during the previous year rose from 19.2 percent in 2019 to 23.9 percent in 2023.[^cdc-trend] The category includes medication, counseling or therapy, or both. A separate CDC report for 2024 estimated that 19.3 percent of adults took medication for mental health and 14.0 percent received counseling or therapy.[^cdc-2024] Those groups overlap, so the numbers cannot be added.

They also cannot tell me why the increase happened. Need, access, awareness, stigma, diagnosis, economic conditions, the pandemic, survey conditions, and other changes may all matter. The National Academies reviewed evidence on social media and adolescent health and did not find support for a population-level conclusion that social media causes changes in adolescent health. Effects vary, causal direction is difficult to establish, and much of the available research has serious limits.[^academies]

I started by looking at treatment as if it were the problem. That was the wrong unit of analysis. Therapy is one place a person may go after a problem exists. Medication is a treatment, not evidence that the person should have been left alone with the problem.

Therapy is not the villain in this story. Medication is not the feed.

The useful question sits upstream: can an information system generate, sustain, or amplify distress, then interpret the attention produced by that distress as a request for more?

## Distress can look like interest

Facebook documented one version of this problem in 2015. Likes, comments, and shares were already News Feed signals. The company announced that time spent viewing a story would become another signal.[^facebook-time]

Its own explanation acknowledged the ambiguity. One person might spend ten seconds because a story was enjoyable. Another might spend ten seconds because the connection was slow. Facebook said it would compare the time spent on one story with the time that person normally spent on other stories. Comparatively longer viewing could indicate relevance, which could move similar posts higher in a later feed.

That announcement is a historical example, not a complete description of Meta ranking in 2026. Present systems use many signals and change constantly. The example matters because the ambiguity was visible from the start.

Maybe I liked the post. Maybe I was horrified. Maybe I could not believe someone published it. Maybe I opened the comments because thousands of people appeared to have lost their minds. The machine can measure that I stared at something. It cannot necessarily know why.

Behavior is observable. Motivation is inferred.

Once the inference becomes “relevant,” tomorrow's distribution can be adjusted around it. Distress does not have to be mistaken for approval. It only has to be mistaken for a reason to keep the material coming.

## A tiny advantage becomes a production lesson

The next wire led to headlines.

Claire Robertson and colleagues analyzed the complete archive of Upworthy headline experiments: 22,743 randomized tests, about 105,000 headline variations, roughly 5.7 million clicks, and more than 370 million impressions.[^negative-headlines] Their preregistered confirmatory analysis used 12,448 experiments. For a headline of average length, adding one negative word increased click-through rate by 2.3 percent.

The experiment supports a causal conclusion about headline wording inside that studied news environment. It does not prove that every negative article wins, that every audience behaves the same way, or that the emotional character of the article itself caused the click. Upworthy varied headlines for the same stories. The measured advantage belongs to the wording.

Still, a publisher can see the result. A creator can see the result. A two-percent advantage repeated across millions of impressions becomes visible in a dashboard.

A tiny advantage becomes a production lesson.

No editor has to announce a new policy called Make Everything Worse. Test two titles. Watch one win. Use the winning structure again. Put another negative word near the front. Notice that retention rose. Teach the pattern to the next person. Eventually the platform communicates through analytics.

Nobody needs to hold a meeting and decide that the internet should become more annoying. The incentives can handle that by themselves.

## The loop gets personal

Christopher Kelly and Tali Sharot tested the relationship between mood and the emotional character of ordinary web browsing across four studies with 1,145 participants.[^mood-browsing] Their observational studies found that people in worse moods tended to browse more negatively valenced pages, and that more negative browsing was associated with worse mood afterward. Their experiments manipulated mood and browsing to test the direction of the relationship.

The result supports a causal, bidirectional loop within the measured conditions:

```text
worse mood
-> more negative browsing
-> worse mood afterward
-> altered later selection
```

This is not a population theory of depression and it does not connect a temporary mood measure to therapy or medication. It supplies the missing middle of the system. The state of the person affects selection. Selection affects the state of the person. Later behavior reaches the next ranking system as fresh evidence.

Put the headline advantage, the mood loop, and the ambiguous attention signal together and a mechanism appears. Negative material has a measurable way to win a click. A low mood can shape what someone chooses. Negative browsing can worsen mood. Attention can be recorded without the reason for it. The resulting signal can influence later distribution.

The machine does not have to hate anybody. It only has to work.

<FeedFigure kind="loop" />

I called this a shit cycle in my notes. The phrase is crude, but the machinery is not imaginary. The figure separates measured relationships from my synthesis because no single paper tested the entire loop from mood to ranking to creator production and back again.

## The algorithm is not the only asshole

Then another Phys.org item complicated the argument.

Hongkai Mao and colleagues analyzed 2.05 billion comments in 2,150 Reddit communities.[^differentiation] They found that threads and communities became more negative over time and tested whether semantic differentiation helped explain the change. A positive opening can use up the obvious agreeable responses quickly. A person trying to contribute something distinct has more room to move through criticism, disagreement, or some other negative angle.

The researchers also ran an experiment with 3,685 participants. People encouraged to stand out became more negative over the course of the simulated discussion than people encouraged to conform, especially when the dialogue started positively. They were not instructed to become hostile. Negativity emerged as a side effect of trying to say something new.

The primary thread analysis stopped at the first 50 comments. The paper covers Reddit, not every conversation system. The researchers also did not argue that algorithms were irrelevant. Ranking, engagement incentives, and ad revenue remain part of the wider environment.

The useful correction is that the algorithm does not have to create every ugly input. People bring motives into the machine. Crowded discussion creates its own pressure. The ranking system can then measure, reward, and redistribute what those motives produce.

## Then creators learn the machine

Creators do not receive a plain manual for the distribution system. They post something, look at the numbers, post something else, and look at the numbers again.

Roland Verwiebe and colleagues interviewed 30 German YouTube creators.[^mercurial] The creators described the algorithm as nontransparent, unpredictable, number-based, powerful, and capable of communicating through metrics. Tatjana Hödl and Thomas Myrach interviewed 26 creators across eight countries about revenue-sharing platforms.[^creator-control] Their analysis found tensions between algorithm and audience, upload regularity and scheduling autonomy, analytics and creative decisions. Creators responded by improving metrics, planning work earlier, diversifying income, selling products, and seeking sponsorships.

That is labor inside an opaque distribution system. A creator can care about the audience, enjoy the craft, and still need the work to reach people. The metric is not merely vanity when it controls access to income.

YouTube made the scale explicit in September 2025. The company said it had paid more than $100 billion to creators, artists, and media companies around the world during the previous four years. Its CEO's summary was direct: “We built an economy.”[^youtube-economy]

The figure says nothing about equal distribution. Most creators do not earn a stable living, and the official number groups individuals, artists, and media companies together. It establishes that distribution is attached to a large economic system.

The platform ranks material. Ranking influences reach. Reach influences revenue. Metrics report the result to the creator. The next production decision happens under that pressure.

## The feedback loop has a curriculum now

The creator economy has become established enough for formal degrees.

Arizona State University's Walter Cronkite School now lists a Content Creation BA for the 2026-27 catalog.[^asu] The program description names content planning, video and podcast production, personal branding, social-media strategy, audience analytics, integrated communication, brand projects, and professional experience. The curriculum adds media writing, information gathering, multimedia, ethics, media law, digital audience work, editing, videography, podcasting, and a production capstone.

An Associated Press report placed the degree inside a difficult labor market: production, audience-retention strategy, brand work, monetization, and business relationships often remain invisible behind the fantasy that a creator simply points a camera and becomes rich.[^ap-degree]

The degree is not proof that colleges teach outrage. It is evidence that the feedback system has matured into professional infrastructure. Skills once learned by posting and staring at numbers now have course codes.

## The treatment question, corrected

I can return to my original suspicion with a better answer.

Treatment use increased. The evidence in this article also supports several narrower conclusions. Negative browsing can affect mood, mood can affect browsing, doomscrolling can leave rumination after the scroll, negative headline wording can win more clicks, creators adapt to metrics and money, and people trying to stand out can make a discussion more negative without being told to do it.

I cannot connect those findings into a claim that social media caused the increase in therapy or medication. I cannot say that people receiving treatment would be fine if recommendation feeds disappeared. I cannot use an adult treatment survey to explain adolescent health or a short-term mood experiment to explain a diagnosis.

The corrected question is still uncomfortable: what happens when an upstream system earns money from material that can leave people distressed after the measurable engagement event is over?

The answer is not that every person breaks. It is that the system has no reliable way to tell distress from interest at the moment it converts behavior into distribution. If the behavior performs, the system can keep the loop moving.

## A feed is not a friend

One more Phys.org story arrived through the same source. Veli Durmuş analyzed first-wave Global Flourishing Study data from 204,907 adults in 22 countries.[^relationships] People who reported greater satisfaction with adult friendships and relationships also tended to report better mental and physical health. Childhood relationships with parents showed a similar but weaker association after the included adjustments.

The study is cross-sectional and uses self-reported relationships and health. It cannot establish which direction the relationship runs, whether another factor affects both, or whether improving one friendship changes health. Friendship is not a substitute for therapy, medication, or medical care.

It belongs in this article because a ranked public feed and a mutual relationship account for people differently.

<FeedFigure kind="friend" />

A feed is not a friend.

That does not mean an online friendship is fake. People can meet online, care for each other, share history, and accept consequences in each other's lives. The distinction is not online versus offline. It is mutual relationship versus public distribution.

A friend can know why I went silent after reading something. A ranking system sees that I returned. A friend may want the spiral to stop. The public feed makes another impression available.

## Then I opened Thunderbird again

Phys.org published something. I subscribed to Phys.org. The article appeared.

The same source then delivered research on differentiation and online negativity, a report about content creation becoming a college degree, and the 22-country relationship study. That cluster became the evidence trail for this article.

The reader did not become useful because it diagnosed me, detected distress, or learned that doomscrolling held my attention. It became useful because I had chosen a source that regularly publishes material relevant to my work.

<FeedFigure kind="pipelines" />

RSS can deliver clickbait, propaganda, sponsored material, bad reporting, and a steady river of misery. I can build a terrible reader. A feed application can sort, filter, search, synchronize, and collect some operational data. Visiting a publisher's website can create ordinary web analytics.

The narrower difference is enough. I choose a source. The source publishes. The item enters the folder I selected. I decide whether to open it. My pause on one item does not ask the RSS protocol to find forty more items like it.

I can use social media to distribute work without using its public feed as my main discovery system. Those are separate jobs. Discovery works better for me when I organize known sources by subject, read what they publish, and follow the references outward.
