Last year I made a bloody Alice tea party at the Salton Sea, decided it was pretty cool, dropped it into a folder full of unrelated experiments, and forgot it existed.

About six months later I was cleaning that folder and found the images again. There she was: black bob, blue dress, brass teapot, wrecked chair, rabbit doll, and an enormous gray cat sitting in water the color of a crime scene. The image still worked. I just had no idea what it was for.

## Building a tea party at the Salton Sea

The starting subject was a Shutterstock photograph of an Alice figure at a blood-covered tea party. It already had the black hair, blue dress, brass pot, teacups, and wounded rabbit doll. I added a separate photograph of a large gray cat, then used photographs I had made at the Salton Sea to build the environment around them.

The source photos gave me the part a generic apocalypse prompt would have missed. One showed a waterlogged armchair collapsing on the shoreline. Another looked through the shell of a ruined structure toward a flat lake and long desert horizon. They had salt, exposed concrete, bad water, dead upholstery, and the peculiar calm of something that had already finished falling apart.

I used [StyleFusion](/workshop/stylefusion/) to keep the reference jobs separate. Alice supplied the subject. The cat supplied the companion and scale problem. My Salton Sea photographs supplied the chair, shoreline, ruins, salt, water, and horizon. The useful part of that workflow was not asking a model to make a picture “like” one reference. It was deciding what each image was allowed to contribute.

I built two principal scenes and made a motion experiment from each. One put Alice inside the broken structure with the cat looming beside her. The other moved the chair into the water and let the shoreline swallow the whole tea party.

## Why evil Alice was boring

The obvious answer was “evil Alice.” It was also the least useful answer.

The blood gave me an image. It did not give me a story. A corrupted Alice with a bloody teapot is a costume, not a character. It explains nothing about what she wants, how she lives, who knows her, or why the image should lead to a second scene. The Queen being a tyrant is not enough of a psychological engine. Alice spends the books confused, challenged, inconvenienced, and threatened. None of that automatically produces a villain in a stained apron.

I could have kept making horror variations, each one with more blood and less reason to exist. The first picture had already done that job.

## The better question was: what if she stayed?

That question produced a person instead of a Halloween variation.

What if Alice followed the White Rabbit, entered Wonderland, found a real route back, and chose not to take it? What would Wonderland do to her over time? What would she do to Wonderland? How would a visitor become a resident without becoming its ruler, mascot, or designated savior?

Choice gave the project a working engine. Alice follows. Alice remains. Later, she tests the boundaries of the place she chose. Each decision leaves a different kind of evidence.

It also changed the design problem. The familiar blue dress was useful shorthand in the prototype, but it kept the image inside somebody else's visual territory. As the character became specific, I dropped that shorthand and built a design that belongs to this project. The [Character / Mannequin work](/workshop/character-mannequin/) is where that kind of identity lock becomes practical: silhouette, face, clothing, material, posture, camera, and the traits that must survive the next image.

I also ruled out the easier version where Alice becomes a master combatant with a belt full of weapons. Other Alice is not a warrior, a murderer, or a sword-first hero. She gets through the world by noticing what it does, preparing for the specific problem, making useful things from local materials, changing scale when access matters, and working with people who know the ground better. Force can happen, but it is not her job description.

Living in Wonderland has changed her body and senses. Those traits are not a superhero upgrade list. They are evidence that the place has acted on her, and that she has learned how to use what she became. [The current Alice record](/presents/other-alice-adventures/cast/alice/) explains those adaptations in detail. For this origin, the important point is that intelligence and invention give me better story problems than handing her a weapon.

## Wonderland does not reset

The original books give Alice an exit. [Alice's Adventures in Wonderland](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11/11-h/11-h.htm) returns her to the riverbank. [Through the Looking-Glass](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12/12-h/12-h.htm) ends by asking who dreamed whom. I did not need to prove that Carroll intended a persistent shared universe. I only needed to notice the opening he left.

From Wonderland's side of the story, Alice arrives, disrupts a trial, insults the court, changes size all over the place, interferes with a tea party, and disappears. Do the residents remember? Does the court revise its procedures? Does a route close behind her? Does a story about the visitor become law, warning, fashion, or bad scholarship?

The project started working when Wonderland stopped resetting. Visitors leave objects, habits, organisms, phrases, routes, debts, and damage. Local people decide what to use, reject, regulate, or repeat. History accumulates because somebody has to live with the aftermath.

From Wonderland's point of view, Alice is a natural disaster with good manners.

## The blood was a prop. The Wasteland was the idea.

The Salton Sea supplied the part I could keep building.

Wonderland grew into a wet, organic, enclosed world: fungi, roots, humidity, curved routes, inhabited systems, and materials that swell, drip, ferment, or remember contact. The Wasteland gave it an opposite. It is dry, open, mineral, exposed, and geometric. Salt replaces water. Ruins become hard planes. A horizon can behave like a trap because there is nowhere for it to hide.

That contrast gave Alice somewhere genuinely unknown to investigate. It also gave the larger serial a way to change its physical grammar without replacing Wonderland before Wonderland had become a world.

## Psychedelia before “psychedelic”

Other Alice draws from spectacle that looked psychedelic before popular drug culture fixed the word to a familiar icon kit. Impossible scale, dream cuts, ornamental geometry, bodies turning into patterns, painted science, theatrical machinery, and visual nonsense were already doing the work.

Busby Berkeley is one clean example. In the 1930s he moved the camera where a theater audience could never sit and turned dancers, water, coins, violins, and human faces into mechanical kaleidoscopes. [The 1933 Warner Bros. article](/presents/3-degrees-of-dick-miller/1933-the-year-warner-bros-built-a-world/) follows that production system in detail. It is also exactly the kind of sideways visual history I want the [Magazine Time Machine](/presents/magazine-time-machine/) to keep finding.

The dates keep the argument honest. LSD was first synthesized in 1938, and its psychoactive properties were discovered in 1943. R. Gordon Wasson's “Seeking the Magic Mushroom” appeared in *LIFE* on May 13, 1957 and carried psilocybin mushrooms to a huge American magazine audience. He did not discover the knowledge. Maria Sabina and Mazatec communities already held ceremonial practices and expertise that his article exposed to outsiders.

The visual archive was strange long before the marketing category arrived.

## From one image to a world

Other Alice Adventures now uses fiction, portraits, maps, motion, field notes, character records, world systems, and the website itself. I am not trying to retell the original trip with additional gore. I want a serial that can keep opening holes because its places remember what came through the last one.

It is also a work in progress: an organic graphic novel developing in public. The published pages show the world taking shape instead of pretending it arrived complete. Designs, methods, and story details can change as the work teaches me what it needs, while the site keeps a visible record of how those choices evolved.

That requires character continuity as well as world continuity. Alice had to stop being the woman in the source image. The current design carries the question raised by the prototype, not its literal costume or props.
