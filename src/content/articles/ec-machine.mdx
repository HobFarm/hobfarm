I learned about _Son of Mad_ while watching _A Hard Day’s Night_. I thought Paul McCartney was reading it. He wasn’t.

It was Shake, one of the Beatles’ fictional road managers, sitting on the train with his face buried in a paperback that said _William M. Gaines’s Son of Mad_. John Junkin played Shake; Norman Rossington, sitting beside him, played Norm.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup> Following the name on that cover led backward through _Mad_, EC Comics, _Tales from the Crypt_, a fatal boat collision, and finally the newspaper strips, soap coupons, printing presses, pulp magazines, radio programs, and newsstands that helped turn comics into an industry.

<EcFilmFrames />

## The book on the train

The close frame identifies the book more precisely than I expected. The little code above the Signet logo reads S1701. The Grand Comics Database records that stock number as a 35-cent New American Library paperback first issued in October 1959, edited by Albert B. Feldstein, with a Kelly Freas cover. The same stock number survived several printings, so the exterior visible in the movie cannot prove which printing Shake holds.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

That limit is useful. I can identify the object without pretending the camera showed its indicia.

The object had already traveled. _Mad_ material left the comic-book rack, entered the mass-market paperback system, crossed the Atlantic, and became ordinary furniture inside the Beatles’ first movie. There is no evidence here of a paid placement. The book is doing something more interesting anyway: it is lying around.

## Following the name backward

The cover gives William M. Gaines top billing, but William did not found EC. His father, Max Gaines, built Educational Comics after two decades spent moving among printers, syndicates, advertisers, distributors, and the companies that became DC.

William Gaines did not build the original machine. He inherited it after the man who built it was suddenly removed.

That sentence changes the story. EC stops looking like a flash of 1950s inspiration and starts looking like the second generation of a publishing experiment.

## Before comic books were comic books

Historians can name several “first comic books” because different experiments established different parts of the form. Newspaper syndicates already distributed recurring characters and serialized visual stories. Color Sunday pages had trained readers to expect comics as a weekly ritual by the 1890s. Those pages also created material that could be licensed, cut down, rearranged, and sold again.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

Dell’s _The Funnies_ appeared in 1929 as a short-lived, tabloid-sized publication sold apart from a newspaper. Its shape was wrong for the later comic rack, but it tested the idea that a package of comics could stand on its own.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

Other industries supplied the rest. Pulp magazines had already sorted cheap fiction into bright genres. Printers had presses and paper. Advertisers wanted premiums. Newsstands had national routes for disposable magazines. Radio anthologies taught audiences to return for a new story under a familiar title and voice. During the Depression, a thin, portable object priced at a dime could offer a great deal of world for very little money.

The American comic book was assembled from those systems. No lone inventor stood beside a blank press and summoned it.

## Soap coupons and portable funnies

Max Gaines became one of the central figures in making the package repeatable. Working with sales manager Harry Wildenberg and Eastern Color Printing, he helped produce _Funnies on Parade_ in 1933. It collected reprinted Sunday strips in a small, saddle-stitched booklet. Procter & Gamble used it as a premium for customers who mailed in coupons from soap products.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

The comic book was partly born as branded content: familiar newspaper material folded into a cheap object, financed by an advertiser, and delivered through a new channel.

Follow-up promotions proved that people wanted the object. _Famous Funnies: A Carnival of Comics_ moved the package toward retail. Then the continuing _Famous Funnies_ series reached newsstands in 1934. The Library of Congress calls its first issue the emergence of the modern comic book while noting that its contents were reprinted newspaper strips.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

That is a sound place to put the milestone, with the machinery left visible around it. The size, binding, price, distribution, and repeat schedule had begun to lock together.

## From reprints to an industry

The container soon needed its own material. Original stories reduced royalty dependence and created characters a publisher could control. Gaines worked with McClure’s syndicate operation, then formed All-American Publications in partnership with the business network around National and Detective Comics.

All-American published the original Flash, Green Lantern, Hawkman, and Wonder Woman among other features. Their writers and artists created the characters; Gaines supplied publishing judgment, financing relationships, editorial infrastructure, and a place on the rack. He did not create Superman, Batman, or the DC universe. His importance lies in helping the comic-book format move from recycled newspaper inventory toward an industry that manufactured original stories and owned durable properties.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-7" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

Gaines sold his All-American interest in the mid-1940s and retained the educational line that became the foundation of his next company.

## Educational Comics

Max treated the comic book as a delivery system. _Picture Stories from the Bible_, _Picture Stories from American History_, and related science and history titles put approved knowledge into the same accessible container that had carried old newspaper jokes.

The later irony is almost too orderly. A company built to deliver scripture, history, and instruction became famous for corpses, murderers, and smart-aleck ghouls. Yet both versions of EC shared the same underlying belief: pictures, captions, recurring formats, and cheap distribution could carry ideas into a reader’s hands.

The container survived while its material, audience, and editorial purpose changed.

Cheap paper did not distinguish education from entertainment. Editors did that work, and the same industrial shell could carry either one.

## The accident and the payload change

On August 20, 1947, Max Gaines took his friend Sam Irwin and Irwin’s young son William onto Lake Placid in a motorboat. Another boat struck them. Gaines and the elder Irwin died; the child survived. Contemporary reports placed the collision at Lake Placid and a later coroner’s finding blamed negligent operation of the other boat.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-8" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>

Max’s 25-year-old son inherited the company. William had been studying chemistry and expected to teach it. He had little interest in publishing at first, then began remaking the business with Al Feldstein, Harvey Kurtzman, Johnny Craig, and a group of artists whose signed styles became part of the product.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-9" aria-label="Source 9">9</a></sup>

William Gaines inherited Educational Comics and transformed it into the EC operation remembered today.

The late-1940s rack encouraged quick mutation. Publishers followed romance, Western, crime, science-fiction, and horror sales. Existing titles could change genre while keeping their issue numbers and, when accepted, the mailing machinery attached to them. EC used the old container as a test chamber.

## The New Trend

Gaines and Feldstein tried horror stories inside _Crime Patrol_ and _War Against Crime!_ before committing whole titles. Their influences already had routes into the office. Pulp stories supplied plots and atmosphere. Radio programs such as _Inner Sanctum_, _The Witch’s Tale_, and _Lights Out_ supplied anthology structure, recurring hosts, cackles, and bad puns. Wholesalers’ early sales checks told EC that the experiments were working.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-10" aria-label="Source 10">10</a></sup>

The rack titles mutated. _Crime Patrol_ became _The Crypt of Terror_ with issue 17, then _Tales from the Crypt_ with issue 20. _War Against Crime!_ became _The Vault of Horror_. The Western _Gunfighter_ became _The Haunt of Fear_. The strange numbering was business residue, not a secret chronology: continuing a title could preserve an existing second-class postal permit, although the Post Office forced _Haunt_ to reset after three issues.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-11" aria-label="Source 11">11</a></sup>

<EcTitleMutations />

The “New Trend” was an editorial system as much as a genre label. Gaines brought story springboards. Feldstein plotted and scripted for particular artists. Johnny Craig, Jack Davis, Graham Ingels, Wally Wood, Reed Crandall, Bernard Krigstein, and others developed recognizable signatures. The Crypt-Keeper, Vault-Keeper, and Old Witch addressed readers directly. Letters pages, house ads, signed work, and the EC Fan-Addict Club turned purchase into membership.

Most stories closed like traps. Cruelty came home. Murderers exposed themselves. A pun waited beside the body.

## The argument over morality

The fight over EC was a fight between competing definitions of morality.

EC’s horror stories often used poetic justice. Abusers, killers, swindlers, and exploiters received elaborate punishment. Its “preachies” attacked racism, antisemitism, mob rule, and institutional cowardice, sometimes with the same shock tactics. Qiana Whitted’s study of EC’s social-protest comics shows how closely that reform impulse was tied to the company’s salesmanship and sensational form.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-12" aria-label="Source 12">12</a></sup>

The censorship coalition defined morality through childhood protection, respectability, parental authority, and social order. Fredric Wertham supplied psychiatric claims. Catholic and Protestant decency organizations, civic groups, teachers, parents, police officials, retailers, distributors, and politicians supplied different kinds of pressure. The comics industry eventually supplied its own enforcement mechanism.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-13" aria-label="Source 13">13</a></sup>

Many early comic-book publishers and creators were Jewish, often working in a disreputable field that offered opportunities closed elsewhere. That history matters, especially beside campaigns led in part by Christian organizations. It does not reduce the conflict to two religious camps. The pressure network was broader, the industry was internally divided, and EC’s own moral record included stereotypes as well as unusually direct attacks on prejudice.

The Senate Subcommittee on Juvenile Delinquency put the dispute on television in April 1954. Its evidence included covers, staff-written plot summaries, public correspondence, Wertham’s testimony, and William Gaines’s defense of his line. The famous exchange about a severed-head cover came from that hearing record.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-14" aria-label="Source 14">14</a></sup>

The Comics Code that followed was an industry rule, not a law. Government attention gave it force; distributors and retailers made the seal commercially decisive. Its restrictions on horror, crime, authority, sexuality, and endings narrowed what could reach the rack. When the Code later objected to reprinting EC’s anti-segregation story “Judgment Day!” with its Black astronaut reveal intact, Gaines printed it anyway.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-15" aria-label="Source 15">15</a></sup>

## Mad changes containers

Harvey Kurtzman and William Gaines created _Mad_ as a ten-cent comic book in 1952. Kurtzman used the comic form to take apart other comics, movies, advertising, and the rest of mass culture. The same EC habit remained: a recognizable editorial voice, specific artists, direct address, and a reader invited to know how the trick worked.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-16" aria-label="Source 16">16</a></sup>

Issue 24, dated July 1955, became a 25-cent, magazine-sized publication.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-17" aria-label="Source 17">17</a></sup> The common shorthand says Gaines made the change to escape the Comics Code. Magazine status did place _Mad_ beyond the Code’s comic-book jurisdiction, which became crucial. Gaines’s own account gives the immediate reason: Kurtzman had an attractive offer from _Pageant_ and wanted a magazine. Gaines changed the format to keep him.<sup className="source-ref"><a href="#source-18" aria-label="Source 18">18</a></sup>

Kurtzman left in 1956 after a dispute over control. Feldstein took over and built the long-running version of _Mad_ that reached millions. The Gaines operation survived by changing the container again.

Then it changed again. Magazine pieces were rearranged for small paperback pages and sold through book racks. _Son of Mad_ gathered work from the magazine and older Kurtzman-era material under William Gaines’s name. By 1964, a copy had reached John Junkin’s hands on a British train set.

Shake keeps reading while the machinery around the Beatles rattles forward. The paperback has passed through several formats and two generations of Gaineses before reaching his hands. Max helped turn newspaper strips into a portable commercial product. William filled that product with horror and satire. _Mad_ became a magazine, then a paperback, then a prop inside a Beatles movie, where it waited long enough for me to misidentify the reader and follow the machine backward.
