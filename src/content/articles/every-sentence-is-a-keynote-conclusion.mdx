I saw two LinkedIn posts in the right order.

The first came from a senior content marketing strategist named Kasey Steinbrinck. He argued that AI could produce acceptable material, maybe even something a little better than good enough, but memorable work still needed a person somewhere inside it. His line was that content only "knocks people's socks off" when at least some part comes from a real person with a big idea and a point of view.

The post had plenty of LinkedIn machinery in it. It opened with "Correct me if I'm wrong." It built toward three rhetorical questions. It ended with "keep it real, homies." I could hear the content-marketing seminar in the walls.

It also sounded like somebody.

The weird capitalization, the bunny rabbits on the trampoline, the slightly clunky "people who I respect," and the final attempt at being a homie all left fingerprints. I could disagree with the argument and still recognize a person making choices.

Then Daniel Pink appeared in my feed.

Pink's profile identifies him as a number-one *New York Times* bestselling author of seven books. His official biography says he has written seven bestselling nonfiction books, sold more than five million copies, and had two number-one *New York Times* bestsellers.[^pink-bio] The post was about a new study of AI and books.

It began with a claim that the number of books published each year had tripled since ChatGPT arrived. Researchers at Cornell and the University of Minnesota, Pink wrote, looked at what all that abundance "actually produced ... and found" that average quality sank as output rose.

That little construction stopped me.

A normal sentence could have said the researchers found that average usage declined as releases increased. Pink's sentence installed a soft dramatic ramp between the researchers and their finding. They looked at abundance. They saw what it actually produced. Then they found the result. Output "soared." Quality "sank." The sentence moved upward and downward at the same time, like a tiny animated business graphic.

The next paragraph removed any remaining doubt I had about the voice. It opened with "Here's the thing," invented an anti-AI objection, pivoted to a vague business metaphor about value moving somewhere, and built a matched pair: production stops being scarce, judgment becomes scarce. Then came the three-part list of what is worth making, fixing, or deleting.

The final paragraph did the rest. Taste is not born, it is built. Read better things. Study good work. Throw away the B-minus draft. The machines generate. Humans decide. Follow Daniel Pink for more.

I cannot open his draft history and prove how the post was made. I am still convinced it came from a chatbot, probably from some version of "make a LinkedIn post about this study, stay positive about AI, and say judgment matters." More important, almost every default structure survived publication.

That is the problem I care about.

Using AI did not ruin the post. The post was diminished by language that could have been generated for almost anyone, about almost anything. A specific research paper entered the machine. A long LinkedIn keynote conclusion came out.

And the post itself told me to throw away the B-minus draft.

## The thought-leadership compiler

Pink's paragraph is useful because the pattern is so complete.

A fact enters first. The writing quickly abandons the concrete subject and moves into broad nouns: abundance, output, production, value, judgment, taste, work. An objection appears even though nobody in the post made it. The writer occupies the reasonable center. A clean binary replaces the messy mechanism. Three examples create the feeling of completeness. A universal lesson arrives. The audience receives instructions. The paragraph ends in a sentence built for a quote card.

The whole process can be written as a production path:

```text
specific fact
→ premature abstraction
→ invented objection
→ reasonable-person position
→ vague analytical metaphor
→ symmetrical opposition
→ rhythmic list
→ universal lesson
→ coaching voice
→ humanist finish
→ call to action
```

This language existed before ChatGPT. Business books, conference talks, marketing blogs, self-help copy, TED-style presentations, and LinkedIn built the genre. Language models learned it because people had already produced mountains of it.

The models made the genre mechanically available.

Now a person can feed a research link into a chatbot and receive a polished version of the same rhetorical sequence in seconds. Other people read that language all day. Some start imitating it. Models train on human writing that already sounds templated, then generated versions return to the web and become part of the next information environment. Human and machine prose begin circling the same drain.

This makes authorship detection less interesting than it first appears. I do not need to prove that Pink pressed a button. A human can write this way from scratch. A model can produce it. A person can write the idea, ask a model to compose it, and leave the model's rhythm intact. The resulting paragraph has the same weakness in every case: the language contributes less point of view than the name above it suggests.

Steinbrinck's post supplies a useful control sample. It uses thought-leadership structure, but the structure has friction. The examples do not fit together perfectly. Shooting film, lip-syncing, and synthetic rabbits involve different questions about technique, deception, performance, and audience expectation. The word "real" does more work than the argument earns. Still, those mismatched examples are evidence of somebody reaching for a thought.

Pink's post feels processed. The seams have been aligned.

The distinction matters because polished writing often receives credit for being considered writing. Fluency looks like thought when the reader is moving fast. The paragraph has momentum, the symmetry closes, and the advice arrives on time. Nothing makes the reader stop and ask whether the claim still has its original measurements attached.

The annoying parts of a person's prose can carry information. A strange example exposes the route taken. An awkward reservation shows where the claim resisted compression. A detail that does not fit the genre can keep the article connected to the thing that caused it. Removing every snag can remove the evidence that anybody traveled through the material.

That resistance is useful. It tells me where the article still has a problem instead of rewarding me for making the problem sound settled.

## What the book study actually found

The paper underneath Pink's post is much more interesting than "quantity up, quality down."

Imke Reimers of Cornell and Joel Waldfogel of the University of Minnesota studied Amazon ebooks released from 2020 through 2025. Their NBER working paper, first posted in January and revised in July 2026, uses a stratified random sample of more than 330,000 titles. A nearly 50,000-title sample supports the AI-detection analysis. The researchers report that monthly releases nearly tripled between 2022 and late 2025 as large language models spread.[^nber] That is narrower than the claim that all books published each year tripled. It concerns a particular platform, format, and release measure.

"Quality" also needs translation. The researchers used marketplace and usage signals such as the number of reader ratings, sales rank, and average star rating. They were not sending novels to a panel of critics. Their detector worked from the 1,000-word previews Amazon made available, and partially detected titles were counted in the broader AI category. The method can describe the sample. It cannot reconstruct how every book was written.

Those measures declined on average. Books containing detected AI text were used less, and their share grew to more than half of 2025 releases. That rising share drove much of the average decline. The flood also increased the number of modestly used books. The authors' economic model estimated roughly seven percent more consumer surplus in 2025 than the pre-LLM process would have produced. Author selection explained most of the observed usage gap, and previously active authors increased their release rates without reducing their non-AI output.[^nber]

That is a complicated result.

The market received far more books. Many did poorly. Average usage declined. Some additional books still found readers. Established authors did not simply stop writing. Consumers may have gained a little from the wider selection even though much of the new supply had little measurable use.

Pink converted all of that into a lever:

```text
quantity rises
quality falls
production becomes cheap
judgment becomes valuable
```

The lever is easy to remember. It also removes the actual questions. What kind of books? What did quality mean? Who produced the AI-heavy titles? Did existing authors lose output? Did readers gain anything? Were the new books useless, modestly useful, or commercially meaningful?

The research contains those questions. The keynote version does not.

## The books do not have to be good to matter

A second study, first posted in July 2026 and revised on August 3, looks directly at the self-published genre-fiction market. It is a working paper under review, so its findings need the caution that belongs to a recent preprint.

The researchers analyzed the full text of 14,419 randomly selected self-published Amazon ebooks released from January 2023 through March 2026 and matched them to daily sales records through June 2026. They grouped the books into three bands: no detected AI text, light AI text up to 25 percent, and substantial AI text above 25 percent.[^book-flood]

Books in the substantial-AI group made up 20 percent of the sample, but earned 12.1 percent of sales and 11.3 percent of revenue. Books with no detected AI text made up 62.9 percent of the sample and received 71.7 percent of sales and 72.5 percent of revenue. Only 10 percent of the top-selling five percent came from the substantial-AI group.[^book-flood]

That sounds like reader rejection until scale enters the picture.

From early 2023 through early 2026, the cumulative catalog in the study grew 38.3 times. The number of titles selling in a quarter grew 19.2 times. Unit sales grew 7.3 times and revenue grew 8.9 times. Books arrived much faster than money or reader attention. Revenue per selling book fell across most genres, including among books with no detected AI text.[^book-flood]

The AI-heavy books also moved beyond the bottom. The top substantial-AI book in the sample generated about $643,000 in gross consumer revenue from 80,431 sales. One observed author identity generated about $1.7 million across eight titles.[^book-flood]

Cheap, mediocre products can reshape a market through volume. They occupy listings, rankings, recommendation surfaces, reader time, and pieces of a revenue pool that grows more slowly than the catalog around it. A title does not need to become a classic to alter discovery for everything beside it. It only needs to take a slot, catch some attention, and make the next release compete in a denser field.

The paper has important limits. It covers one platform and a set of self-published genre categories. Its AI classifications depend on detection methods. The results are observational. The authors cannot prove why an individual reader chose a book or what disclosure would have changed. The study does not establish that every AI-heavy book is bad, that every no-AI book is good, or that one specific title copied another.

It does establish that the flood is not imaginary.

We still do not have the sentence-level study I want. Nobody has counted how many of these books use "here's the thing," matched scarcity claims, staged reversals, tidy triplets, or a lesson at the end of every section. The available research is close enough to make the question serious.

## AI slop has a grammar

Researchers have already measured repeated syntactic templates in generated text.

A 2024 EMNLP paper defined templates as recurring part-of-speech structures rather than repeated words. The authors found that models produced templated constructions at higher rates than human reference texts. They traced 76 percent of templates in model output to pretraining data, compared with 35 percent in human-authored reference text, and found that fine-tuning and alignment did not simply erase them.[^templates]

That does not prove that one phrase marks AI. It supports the larger observation: model output has recurring structural habits.

A separate controlled study asked people to write argumentative essays alone, with GPT-3, or with InstructGPT. Writing assisted by InstructGPT became more similar across authors and showed lower lexical and content diversity. The human-written portions did not show the same reduction. The homogenizing effect came mainly from the model-contributed text.[^diversity]

Professional writers can name the defects even when they do not use the same vocabulary I do. For a CHI 2025 study, 18 writers edited 1,057 model-generated paragraphs and made exactly 8,035 fine-grained changes. Their seven-category taxonomy included clichés, unnecessary exposition, purple prose, poor sentence structure, lack of specificity, awkward phrasing, and tense problems. Expert-edited passages were preferred over model-edited passages, which were preferred over the raw generations.[^salvaged]

Sentence cleanup only reaches so far.

StoryScope, accepted for COLM 2026 and revised on August 10, compared 10,272 human-written story prompts with versions generated by five language models. The corpus contained 61,608 stories of roughly 5,000 words each. Narrative features alone separated human and AI stories with 93.2 percent macro-F1, nearly matching systems that also used surface style.[^storyscope]

The models shared deeper habits. AI stories explained their themes more directly, favored tidier single-track plots, and resolved more of the action through the protagonist. Human stories used more temporal complexity, moral ambiguity, subplots, and loose ends. Individual models also developed their own tendencies. Claude produced flatter escalation. GPT returned to dreams. Gemini leaned toward external character description.[^storyscope]

This is why deleting an em dash does not solve the problem.

The visible punctuation is a clue. The deeper fingerprint lives in what the model decides a paragraph or story is supposed to do. It explains. It balances. It closes the loop. It makes the theme legible. It resolves tension into a lesson.

A person can clean every obvious phrase and leave the model's architecture untouched.

## Once you see the model

I learned the same lesson through image generators.

The first good output from a new model can look impossible. It solved hands better. It understood lighting. The skin or paint or fabric finally behaved. Then I generated twenty more images. The same face appeared. The same depth haze returned. The same body position, lens choice, color relationship, ornamental clutter, or dramatic backlight kept sneaking into unrelated prompts.

After enough comparisons, I stopped evaluating only the picture. I started seeing the model.

Video has its own version. One system makes people walk with a particular float. Another turns every head movement into the same little performance. Cameras drift in familiar ways. Fabric catches up late. Bodies accelerate and stop with house motion. A six-second clip can reveal the generator before the subject has done anything interesting.

Formal attribution research finds recoverable source traces in synthetic images and video. One WACV 2025 study tested 12 text-to-image generators and found model-specific signals at several levels, with learned style representations outperforming raw RGB data for attribution. A newer video preprint reports spatial and temporal source signatures across generated clips.[^visual-fingerprints] Those studies support the existence of model-specific traces. They do not validate my exact aesthetic method or turn a hunch into forensic identification.

My recognition is practical. It comes from running the systems, comparing versions, and watching unresolved prompt decisions fall into the same defaults. A generator has to put the camera somewhere. It has to choose the light, the distance, the posture, the motion, the amount of ornament, and what happens in the empty space. When the prompt leaves those decisions open, the model shows its preferences.

Model literacy comes from comparison.

The same effect appears in writing. In a 2025 ACL study, annotators who frequently used ChatGPT for writing read 300 nonfiction articles produced by humans and commercial language models. A majority vote among five experienced users misclassified only one article. Their explanations went beyond suspicious vocabulary. They noticed formality, originality, clarity, and larger textual behavior.[^human-detectors]

That study does not mean everybody can identify AI writing. Another 2025 study compared German short stories written by GPT-3.5 with stories from nonprofessional student writers. In a reader experiment with 380 participants, the groups reported similar novelty and enjoyment. Readers were transported slightly less by the AI stories, but they did not universally reject them.[^reader-response]

Both results can be true.

Some readers do not notice the machinery. Some notice and do not care. Some enjoy the predictable clarity. People who spend a lot of time generating, comparing, and correcting model output can become extremely sensitive to it.

That last group may become the least tolerant audience for raw AI prose. We know what it costs to produce. We have seen the same paragraph appear in a hundred disguises. Once the structure becomes visible, the writing asks us to spend ten minutes receiving something our own chatbot could produce in ten seconds.

## The machine eats what it makes

The book flood also creates a training-data problem.

A 2024 *Nature* paper studied what happens when generations of models learn indiscriminately from data produced by earlier models. The authors called the result model collapse. Rare patterns in the original distribution disappeared first. Later generations converged toward a smaller, less accurate version of the underlying data. The paper specifically warned that large-scale publication of generated text could pollute web datasets collected for successor models.[^collapse]

Language diversity can shrink before a model becomes visibly useless. A NAACL 2024 study recursively trained language models on synthetic text and found declines in lexical, syntactic, and semantic diversity, especially on creative tasks.[^linguistic-collapse] Image models show a related problem. The ICLR paper *Self-Consuming Generative Models Go MAD* found progressive losses in quality or diversity when generative models repeatedly consumed synthetic output without enough fresh real data.[^mad]

This is the uncontrolled recursion I worry about:

```text
human work
→ model training
→ generated work
→ mass publication
→ web and catalog ingestion
→ future training
→ narrower generated work
```

The exact real-world outcome is not settled. Synthetic data is not poison by definition. Researchers have shown that keeping original real data and accumulating synthetic data alongside it can avoid collapse in tested settings.[^collapse-counter] OpenAI says it deliberately uses synthetic prompts and examples where data is sparse or imbalanced.[^openai-training] Curated synthetic data can expand coverage, protect privacy, or teach a model a task.

The dangerous part is provenance loss and indiscriminate replacement.

OpenAI's public data-partnership program asks for material that is not already easy to find online and specifically highlights long-form writing and conversations that express human intention.[^openai-data] The company also describes cleaning auto-generated artifacts from contributed datasets. The public pages support a narrower conclusion: known, intentional, provenance-rich material has become valuable. They do not disclose a secret hunt for pre-ChatGPT books.

A million generic AI books do more than crowd a store. They enter search indexes, quotations, summaries, recommendations, scraped datasets, and the writing habits of people who encounter them. The feedback loop includes humans too. People absorb the assistant cadence, use it as the new sound of professional writing, and feed the result back into the culture before any model trainer touches it.

The phrase "judgment becomes scarce" might eventually become more common because models keep generating it, people keep publishing it, and other people learn that this is how an intelligent point is supposed to sound.

I do not have a study proving that exact phrase-level loop. The surrounding mechanisms are real enough to investigate.

The boundary is worth keeping clear. The collapse studies are controlled experiments, not a time-lapse film of the entire public web. The publishing papers observe Amazon markets, not successor model weights. My feedback-loop argument connects those separate environments. It is a plausible system risk and a reason to preserve provenance, not a measured forecast of how much culture will disappear.

## How I use the thing

This article was built with ChatGPT.

That sentence does not settle anything.

For years, writing was one of the weakest parts of my AI workflow. Models could produce grammatical copy, but they kept turning specific ideas into brochures, explainers, content-marketing posts, and little keynote speeches. I would give the model an odd connection and get back a paragraph explaining that the connection was "not just" one thing, "but" another more important thing. Every section wanted a takeaway. Every conclusion wanted to become advice.

The systems have become more useful as they have gained tools, longer context, project instructions, and access to source files that they can actually read. I can now work inside a thread for hours, give the model research to inspect, bring in previous articles, and make it carry several lines of thought at once.

This thread began with two LinkedIn posts. Then came the language teardown. Then the book paper. Then another book-market study. Then syntactic templates, editing research, narrative structures, reader response, image and video fingerprints, model collapse, and the question of whether readers will skip generic books and ask their own AI instead.

The article did not exist inside the first prompt.

I supplied the irritation and the point of view. The model helped hold the growing pile in memory, find research paths, compare studies, and test possible structures. Each new source changed the piece. The NBER paper complicated Pink's summary. The market study showed that low-performing books can still take meaningful market share. StoryScope pushed the problem below punctuation. The reader studies prevented an easy claim that everyone will reject AI prose. Model-collapse research gave the recursion concern a technical foundation and a necessary caveat.

That is where AI saves me time. It helps connect material that arrived in fragments.

It also helps me inspect the wires between articles. A post about AI books can open into image models, video motion, data provenance, reader behavior, and the way I build HobFarm, but proximity does not make every connection useful. The model can propose branches. I still decide which relationship changes the article, which one belongs in a link, and which one should wait for another piece.

The useful setup takes a long time to build.

My HobFarm project contains source files for article development, research standards, visual systems, site structure, previous work, and prose rules. One of those files begins with this instruction:

> Write like a person with a clear point, not like a model demonstrating balance.

The file names the structures I want removed: decorative contrasts, over-balanced sentences, generic transitions, abstract noun piles, repetitive three-part lists, polished summary flourishes, compulsory lessons, and conclusions that soften into motivational copy.

That is more useful than "sound human."

"Human" is too vague. A model can imitate slang, insert fragments, make a typo, and still build every paragraph from the same synthetic frame. A working editorial system needs observable failure modes.

The source files also carry risk. An old instruction can keep steering new work after the project changes. A weak early theory can survive because it appeared near the beginning of a long thread. A wrong fact can be restated until it feels established. Generated copy can re-enter the context and become evidence for itself. Ten mediocre examples can teach the model the wrong style.

The small workflow can reproduce the same recursion problem as the larger training environment:

```text
bad source
→ plausible generation
→ accepted draft
→ new project context
→ repeated error
```

So I maintain the context. I replace old files. I separate research notes from publishable prose. I tell the model which source wins when instructions conflict. I mark memory, inference, and verified fact differently. I reject paragraphs that have the correct information and the wrong shape.

The verification pass is usually less elegant than the draft. For this piece, it meant opening the current paper instead of trusting the abstract, checking revision dates, finding the exact sample behind a percentage, and writing down what a detector actually saw. It meant separating the results of a controlled recursion experiment from my concern about the public web. It meant keeping an author-supplied screenshot out of the page because a private reference is enough to support my recollection but not a reason to republish somebody else's post.

The model is useful during that work because it can hold a queue of unresolved claims and compare the language in the draft with the language in the sources. It is also capable of resolving an uncertainty by quietly choosing the most convenient interpretation. A citation at the end of a confident sentence does not tell me which of those things happened. I still have to inspect the source and decide how narrow the sentence should be.

That work is authorship in this system.

The model can compose a final draft from material I developed. It cannot decide which strange connection deserves an article unless I let it. It does not know which side trail belongs to a future piece, which personal detail changes the argument, which unresolved part should stay unresolved, or which clean paragraph quietly erased the reason I started.

A CHI 2026 study offers a useful complication. Twenty-eight MFA writers produced text alongside three language models, and readers compared human work with prompted imitation and author-specific fine-tuning. Expert judges preferred human writing 82.7 percent of the time in the in-context condition. After author-specific fine-tuning, expert preference moved toward the model output at 62 percent.[^fine-tuning] Default model prose is one operating mode, not an eternal limit.

My project files are not fine-tuning. They work at the context and editorial layers. The direction is related: reduce the number of important decisions the generic model has to invent, then inspect everything it adds anyway.

## The slop bypass

Pink's post produced a reaction he probably did not intend.

I did not follow him for more. I gave the subject to my own AI setup.

The system found the original paper, pulled apart the claims, located a newer study, compared methods, and followed the language question into research on templates, homogenization, editing, narrative construction, reader response, visual fingerprints, and recursive training. The result became an article that Pink was not trying to write.

Generic AI prose can cause readers to bypass the writer.

Old content farms competed by summarizing available information into search-friendly pages. Generated books and articles can do that at enormous scale. The reader now has another option. A personal AI system can retrieve the underlying sources, compare them, answer follow-up questions, and format the material around the reader's existing context.

A generic sixty-thousand-word AI book faces a strange competitor: the reader's own chatbot.

Why spend eight hours reading somebody else's default generation session when I can ask my system to inspect the same subject, show me the receipts, connect it to work I already know, and stop when I have the part I need?

A book or article survives that bypass by carrying something the reader cannot reconstruct from a generic prompt. The writer's route through the material matters. So do lived experience, specific research decisions, odd examples, private archives, field knowledge, humor, visual evidence, and connections that would not appear in a standard summary.

That claim has limits too. A personal chatbot may summarize badly, miss a paywalled source, invent a citation, or narrow the reader's view around whatever context it was given. Asking a model is not the same as reading the work. It does, however, change the competitive environment for writing that offers only generic synthesis. A summary-shaped book now competes against a machine that produces custom summaries on demand.

AI can help assemble the material that survives the bypass. It can also flatten it.

The difference shows up in the edit.

This article went through the same machine it describes. The model produced a coherent draft from the research, notes, source files, and argument developed here. Then I compared it with the thread. I checked the papers again. I looked for claims that had become cleaner than the evidence. I removed staged pivots, balanced lessons, and paragraphs that concluded because the model thought every section needed closure.

Now I will read it out loud and delete the sentence that sounds finished only because both halves line up.
