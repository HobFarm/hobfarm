## Welcome to the worst beach

I was not researching Ghanaian waste management. I was watching Wendall walk around Accra.

At 21:24 in *Ghana: Humans Shouldn’t Live Like This*, he reached the Jamestown shoreline, looked across it, and said, “Welcome to the worst beach I’ve ever seen.” Half a minute later he was still carrying an empty bottle. “I’m holding on to my drinks bottle because I don’t want to litter.” The gesture was sensible and almost ceremonial against the volume already on the sand.[^wendall-jamestown]

People were cleaning. Piles burned along the beach. When Wendall asked why, a fisherman explained that the government had called for people to clean their environment. Rain brought rubbish through the lagoon and the sea returned it. The men gathered what arrived, but gathering was not the end of the route. “That’s our problem.”

Ghana had declared July 10 and 11, 2026 National General Cleaning Days after severe flooding. The official order did not stop at sweeping streets and clearing drains. Local governments and waste companies were told to provide trucks and remove the collected material immediately so another rain would not wash it back.[^ghana-cleanup]

That instruction contains the whole problem. A broom can move garbage from one side of a boundary to another. Disposal requires a destination.

A 2019 report from the same shoreline found sanitation officers gathering waste every morning. Without a practical removal route, they burned it near the beach. The report followed other material through Accra’s drains, the Odaw River, Korle Lagoon, the Gulf of Guinea, and back to Jamestown.[^jamestown-2019]

Picking up the garbage was not the same as disposing of it.

## The beach is downstream of everything

The physical route is simple enough to draw and too large to blame on one neighborhood. Street litter, household waste, market debris, sewage, and whatever else enters gutters across the catchment can reach the Odaw. The river enters the Korle system. The lagoon opens beside Jamestown. Rain moves material toward the coast; tides can slow it, reverse it, hold it near the mouth, or help return it to shore.

The beach was not the beginning of the waste problem. It was where the city got some of it back.

## Then Wendall found the river

The connection became visible in Wendall’s next Accra video. He went into Old Fadama to meet people outside the tourist circuit. At about 03:54, he reached a waterway where garbage covered so much of the channel that the water was almost a background detail. People nearby objected to his filming. The video establishes the objection. It does not establish whether it came from privacy, distrust, embarrassment, frustration, or something else.[^wendall-fadama]

Old Fadama is an informal settlement beside the Odaw and upper Korle system. Current research describes inadequate roads, drainage, water, sanitation, and waste-management infrastructure, with refuse filling gutters.[^old-fadama]

A conventional garbage truck needs a street wide enough to enter, an accessible pickup point, a route to a transfer station, money for the trip, and a final site willing and able to receive the load. Dense informal development can break any of those steps. Residents are not the antagonist. Old Fadama is where several urban failures occupy the same frame.

People were living where the formal garbage chain stopped.

## The river gets the job

Every city produces at least three different waste streams. Solid waste needs collection, transfer, sorting, recycling, treatment, or a landfill. Domestic wastewater needs a sewer or septic system and treatment. Industrial wastewater needs source control, pretreatment, an effluent plant, monitoring, and a permitted discharge or reuse plan.

When those systems end early, the wastes do not disappear. A pile becomes a transfer station. A gutter becomes a collection route. A river becomes a garbage truck, sewer pipe, and industrial outfall at the same time.

The scale of the material keeps growing. The World Bank estimates that the world produced 2.56 billion tonnes of municipal solid waste in 2022. It does not publish one defensible global percentage for everything uncollected or openly dumped, but it does report collection rates as low as 31 percent in Sub-Saharan Africa and 67 percent in South Asia. It also finds that the combined economic damage from uncollected waste, open burning, dumping, waterway discharge, and flooding from blocked drains exceeds the cost of operating proper systems.[^what-a-waste]

A 2021 model estimated that more than 1,000 rivers account for 80 percent of modeled annual riverine plastic emissions to the ocean, somewhere between 0.8 and 2.7 million metric tonnes a year. Small urban rivers mattered more than earlier models suggested.[^thousand-rivers] That is only plastic. Rivers carry much more.

## Accra’s missing middle

Accra does not divide neatly into formal collection and careless dumping. In an April 2025 survey, informal collectors handled more than 70 percent of household waste. Seventy-five percent of what they collected went to unregulated dumpsites where burning was common.[^accra-collectors] Those collectors were performing most of the first mile. The weakness came after pickup.

The August 2026 flood response made the middle of the route unusually explicit. Ghana News Agency reported that five of Accra’s six transfer stations were operating. The sixth, at Mallam, was closed because it lacked an access road. The Jamestown station was taking about 800 tricycles a day, while an estimated 4,000 worked across Greater Accra. Articulated trucks then hauled the waste overnight to engineered landfills outside the city.[^accra-transfer]

The transfer station existed. Its access road did not. Small collectors existed. The closest engineered landfill was too far away for them to use directly. The broken chain was not a moral mystery. It was a missing road, a queue, a fee, a long haul, and more refuse arriving before the last load could leave.

## Researchers counted the trash

Researchers watched four Odaw bridges during eight sampling days from March through May 2021. Two bridges were in the tidal zone. After one 5.3-millimetre rainfall event, plastic transport at the upstream tidal bridge rose to 7.3 times its other-day average. Rainfall and modeled river discharge mattered more upstream; tide mattered more near the mouth, where material could move in both directions and accumulate.[^odaw-study]

The researchers estimated about 140 to 380 kilograms of floating plastic per day across their summarized dry-season conditions. They had not sampled the wet season, the entire water column, or enough years to turn that range into an annual total. It is a baseline, not a multiplication exercise.

Rain did not create the garbage. It gave the garbage a ride.

## Some of it floats. Some of it dissolves.

The visible river is only one register. Whole bottles, bags, clothes, tires, fishing gear, furniture, food waste, and carcasses can be counted in a photograph. Fibers, fragments, suspended solids, bacteria, and viruses require sampling. Nutrients, pesticides, dyes, solvents, oils, metals, and pulp chemicals may dissolve, react, settle, or accumulate in sediment and food webs.

UN-Water estimated that 42 percent of household wastewater was not safely treated in 2022, releasing about 113 billion cubic metres with inadequate or no treatment. Industrial reporting was too sparse for a responsible global percentage: only 22 countries, representing 8 percent of the world’s population, supplied data.[^un-water]

Some of it floats. Some of it dissolves. Some of it is shit. Some of it comes out of a factory pipe. The river does not care which department was supposed to deal with it.

## Dhaka: every waste system in the same water

Dhaka shows what happens when all three routes crowd the same network. In February 2026, the World Bank reported that about 20 percent of residents had piped sewer connections and another 2 percent used functional fecal-sludge management. More than 80 percent of untreated wastewater and sewage entered interconnected waterways. More than half the city’s canals had disappeared or become clogged. The same assessment counted more than 7,000 factories releasing an estimated 2.4 billion litres of untreated wastewater daily.[^dhaka]

The Dasherkandi sewage-treatment plant supplies a sharp infrastructure paradox. It can treat 500 million litres a day. A July 2026 government evaluation found that it received about 280 million during the dry season because the sewer network needed to deliver the rest had not been built. Rainy-season inflow climbed to about 480 million, but much of that was stormwater.[^dasherkandi]

Accra has collectors whose waste struggles to reach transfer and disposal. Dhaka has a treatment plant that much of the sewage cannot reach. Building the destination without building the connection leaves the river at work.

## Two more river systems

The Citarum in West Java has often been described as one of the world’s most polluted rivers, usually with textile factories in the foreground. Industrial effluent matters. So do domestic sewage, municipal waste, livestock, and agricultural runoff. A World Bank assessment of the upper basin attributed at least two-thirds of its modeled pollution load to domestic and municipal activities, followed by industrial and agricultural sources. Its conclusion was not that factories were innocent. It was that treating only one stream would fail.[^citarum]

In Guatemala, the Las Vacas carries sewage and solid waste from the capital into the Motagua system and toward the Caribbean. AIDA, citing a UN Development Programme project, reports that 66 percent of Guatemala’s urban solid waste is not collected and that 88.32 percent of disposal sites are illegal or lack municipal authorization.[^motagua-aida] The Ocean Cleanup estimates that as much as 20,000 tonnes of plastic move through the basin annually. That is the organization’s model-based estimate, not a settled measurement.[^motagua-estimate]

The Inter-American Development Bank approved a $250 million basin program in 2025 to expand collection and disposal, treat effluent, and improve monitoring across a watershed of 96 municipalities and about four million people.[^motagua-idb] The intervention is upstream infrastructure because a barrier in the river cannot supply sewer connections or municipal collection.

The IWMF photo essay *River of Trash* follows Motagua debris onto the Honduran coast. Its inventory resembles my Vũng Tàu photographs: foam, bottles, household objects, wood, and the apparently mandatory cracked sandal.[^iwmf] I used the essay as reporting and comparison. I did not copy its photographs.

## What the ocean gives back

I asked someone in Vietnam why so much garbage reached these beaches. He blamed the constant commercial traffic and smaller fishing boats, saying some crews avoided the cost of landing garbage at port. That is a conversation I remember, not a verified source assignment for the objects in my photographs.

International rules make the distinction important. MARPOL Annex V generally prohibits ships from discharging garbage at sea, with narrow exceptions for specified materials and conditions. It prohibits plastics. The rules also require adequate port reception facilities because compliance depends on ships having somewhere to put waste ashore.[^marpol] The existence of a rule does not prove which vessel obeyed it, and a photograph of rope does not name a boat.

Vũng Tàu also receives enormous seasonal pulses from land. In May 2025, VTV reported 50 tonnes removed during one morning after debris covered three kilometres of Back Beach. The municipal service described two or three large events a year between May and November, driven largely by the Đồng Nai river system and the Cần Giờ estuary.[^vung-tau-may] In November, workers removed more than 400 tonnes from another event and estimated its total above 1,000 tonnes after storms and high tides moved river debris seaward and currents returned it to the beach.[^vung-tau-november]

A clean tourist beach is not necessarily a beach that receives no garbage. It may be a beach somebody cleaned at six that morning.

## The clean-looking river

The Hậu and Cần Thơ waterways do not look like the channel in Old Fadama. Brown water in a delta can contain natural suspended sediment, so appearance alone establishes little. The cleaner the surface looks, the more the photograph needs help from a sampler.

<RiversPhotoGrid photos={canThoPhotos} label="Two views of working waterways in Cần Thơ" columns={2} />

A two-year passive-sampler study detected 83 pesticide residues in the Cần Thơ River. The researchers compared 27 prevalent compounds in detail and found several above their applied thresholds in at least one sampler or site. That is narrower than saying all 27 exceeded a limit, and it is what the paper supports.[^can-tho-pesticides]

Another study sampled the Mekong water column from Cambodia to Vietnam. Concentrations rose from two microplastic particles per cubic metre at rural Kampi to 64 in the Cần Thơ River. Fibers made up 53 percent of the particles and fragments 44 percent. Across the sampled sites, 86 percent were below the surface.[^mekong-microplastics]

My photograph of woven rice and feed sacks used as walls and roofing shows one plausible material pathway. Ultraviolet light, heat, rain, and abrasion can make woven plastic brittle. Fibers can enter a drain or river. The measured particles might also come from clothing, wastewater, fishing gear, packaging, or other sources. Connecting them to these exact sacks would require polymer matching and more evidence than a photograph can supply.

The less recognizable the waste becomes, the cleaner the river can look.

## I grew up beside one

I grew up near the Penobscot when mill pollution and sewage still defined its reputation. People knew not to touch the water. The river could foam, smell, and look contaminated. That memory does not make Maine and Ghana equivalent. It does make a river used as missing infrastructure familiar.

The EPA record gives the memory a timeline. In 1967, towns along the Penobscot were still sending the untreated waste of roughly 32,000 people into the river, while seven pulp and paper mills discharged untreated industrial waste continuously. State standards, industrial treatment, municipal plants, federal investment, and Clean Water Act permits changed the river while mills were still running. By 1977, biochemical oxygen demand had fallen about 80 percent from its 1964 level.[^penobscot-epa]

Robert A. G. Monks recalled waking in a Brewer motel with burning eyes and looking out at “great mounds of white foam” moving down the Penobscot. In the official transcript of the 2003 documentary *The Corporation*, he called the arrangement a “doom machine”: nobody he knew wanted the pollution, but the system produced it anyway.[^monks]

The Bucksport mill passed from Champion to International Paper in 2000 and to Verso in 2006. Verso closed it in December 2014. About 570 jobs disappeared; the mill had supplied roughly 44 percent of the town’s property taxes.[^bucksport] Demolition followed. Closure removed an industrial load, but it came decades after treatment had already improved the river. It also removed wages, suppliers, and a tax base.

The Passagassawakeag and Belfast Bay supply the smaller, stranger family memory. My father remembered shoreline rocks becoming slimy with chicken fat when Belfast’s poultry plants discharged processing waste. EPA records from the period document wastewater high in fats, feathers, blood, offal, and viscera, including material discharged raw to the bay.[^belfast-poultry]

The modern photographs are useful because they are not spectacular. Sailboats, marina docks, seawall, bridge, weather. Belfast Harbor does not look rehabilitated. It just looks like Belfast Harbor. You have to know the history to understand what is missing.

I left Bangor in 2000 because there was little opportunity suited to me. Twenty-six years later, I still see no practical reason to move back. I do not think growing up in Maine tells me what it is like to grow up in Ghana. It does mean I recognize what a place looks like when there are not enough ways to get ahead, when people take the work that exists, and when leaving becomes one more expense they cannot carry.

A cleaner river can raise property values and make a waterfront pleasant without restoring the jobs of the people who lived beside it when it was foul. Visible recovery does not erase older contamination either. The former HoltraChem plant in Orrington discharged mercury into the Penobscot; cleanup, court orders, sediment studies, and food-web concerns continue after the plant’s 2000 closure.[^holtrachem]

## Return to Trash Mountain

A landfill, transfer station, garbage truck, sewage plant, septic system, and industrial effluent plant all answer the same question: Where does the waste go?

They can be expensive, ugly, badly designed, politically unpopular, or too far away. Their absence does not eliminate the waste. It transfers the work to gravity, rain, fire, rivers, and the sea.

The Penobscot did not clean itself. We built somewhere else for the shit to go.

[*Trash Mountain*](/articles/trash-mountain/) was the story of garbage reaching its destination. *Rivers of Trash* is about garbage that never got one.
