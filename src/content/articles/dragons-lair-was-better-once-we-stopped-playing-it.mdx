There was always a small crowd around *Dragon’s Lair*, but I do not remember anybody I knew putting money into it.

We watched the attract mode. We watched Dirk the Daring stumble into another beautifully animated death. We admired the cabinet and then moved toward the games we intended to play.

That memory came back because a Boing Boing item arrived in the RSS reader I wrote about in [Hit the Source Directly](/articles/hit-the-source-directly/). The headline said *Dragon’s Lair* only cost fifty cents to beat in 1983. Technically, that could be true. If you already knew every move, hit every direction inside the correct window, and never made a mistake, one credit could carry you through.

But that was not the transaction available to a kid encountering the machine.

The transaction was two quarters for three short chances to guess what a Don Bluth cartoon wanted. Then you paid again to find out whether you had remembered the lesson.

## The first fifty-cent game

*Dragon’s Lair* is the first and only arcade game I clearly remember costing fifty cents when the ordinary price was a quarter. The difference sounds small now. Inside an arcade, it changed the decision.

Two quarters were not an abstract price. They were two games. They could also be two turns, one for me and one for a friend. The peer-group judgment was immediate: *Dragon’s Lair* was a waste.

The machine arrived in 1983 with something no other cabinet could match. Instead of building its world from the pixel graphics people expected, it used a LaserDisc player to call up sequences of full animation. Rick Dyer developed the interactive system. Cinematronics published the cabinet. Don Bluth’s studio supplied the thing that made everybody stop walking: a real animated adventure, with elaborate backgrounds, expressive characters, comic timing, monsters, fire, swinging blades, and spectacular ways for Dirk to die.[^strong-dragon]

The controls looked familiar. The joystick and sword button suggested direct action. The actual exchange was closer to a timed memory test. At particular moments the machine expected one correct input. Success advanced the disc to another scene. A mistake advanced it to a death.

The cabinet looked more alive than anything around it while offering less freedom.

## What got my quarters instead

I played arcade games. I just did not want to spend twice as much on that one.

I wanted *Spy Hunter*, with its steering yoke, weapons buttons, oil slicks, road changes, and the urgent version of the *Peter Gunn* theme. I wanted *Rampage*, where three monsters could climb buildings and punch the city into lunch. I wanted *Gauntlet*, especially with four people crowded around it, each player trying to keep a character alive while the machine announced that somebody needed food badly.

I wanted *Ghosts ’n Goblins*, which was also brutally hard. I wanted *Altered Beast*, with its transformations and its booming command to rise from the grave. Difficulty was not the objection. Those games gave me a continuous relationship between the controls and the screen. If I died, I usually understood what I had tried to do.

Then there was *LED Storm*. A friend and I competed at it repeatedly. That cabinet never became the central game in the public history of the arcade, but it became one of ours. We learned its rhythm, watched each other, and returned to improve.

I played *Pac-Man* and *Donkey Kong* too. They were part of the room, not the focus of my room. The arcade history I remember is not a tidy procession of the games that later became the approved classics. It is a map of whichever cabinets earned another quarter.

## The cartoon was not the problem

Don Bluth had worked at Disney, left, returned, became a directing animator on *The Rescuers*, directed animation on *Pete’s Dragon*, and left again in 1979 with Gary Goldman and John Pomeroy.[^d23] Their new studio made *The Secret of NIMH*. The film did not need to resemble the simplified television animation surrounding it. It moved like an argument that theatrical animation could still be lush, strange, frightening, and handmade outside Disney.

That was the studio Dyer approached.

The result mattered because the animation was not decoration placed over an ordinary game. It was the machine’s reason to exist. A contemporary Washington Post report described a control system sending commands to a disc player, while Bluth situated the result between the arcade and film businesses.[^post]

That intersection explains both the wonder and the frustration. The cabinet had to preserve expensive finished footage. It could not let a player walk anywhere, swing at anything, or improvise a solution, because the animation for unlimited choices did not exist. The branching path had already been drawn.

*Dragon’s Lair* was not a bad cartoon. It was a spectacular cartoon trapped inside an expensive machine that kept interrupting it.

## Hard was fine. Value was the problem.

Arcade games were designed to end. The operator needed the next quarter. Difficulty, short lives, escalating speed, and unforgiving patterns were part of the business. Nobody mistook *Ghosts ’n Goblins* for charity.

But a hard action game could still produce a long present tense. You moved, reacted, recovered, experimented, panicked, and sometimes found a better way through. *Dragon’s Lair* often felt like a future tense. The good part was always the scene you might be allowed to see after you learned the command.

This distinction disappears when the game is discussed only as a technical landmark. It was one. The LaserDisc delivered cinematic image quality that ordinary game hardware could not generate in real time. The machine drew a crowd and became a sensation. It also charged a premium for the least forgiving learning loop in the room.

Those facts belong together.

<ArcadeFigure kind="timeline" />

The arcade did not stop producing landmarks after that. *Street Fighter II* arrived in 1991 and turned the cabinet into a direct contest between two people. *Mortal Kombat* followed in 1992 with digitized performers, blood, fatalities, and a crowd that gathered because the person at the controls could do something astonishing.[^fighters]

I played *Mortal Kombat* in the arcade first. The home ports came later. The Genesis and Super Nintendo arguments were partly about how much of the arcade experience had survived the trip home. By then the important reversal was underway. Home was no longer where you accepted the reduced version of the real game. Home hardware was becoming its own center.

## I did not lose interest in games

We had Atari at home first and Nintendo later. Each changed the price of failure. Once the cartridge was in the house, dying did not create another transaction. It created another attempt.

The bigger change came with the computer.

*Wolfenstein 3D* made the PC feel fast and spatial. *Doom* made it feel almost bottomless. The game arrived in 1993 through shareware and network distribution, then kept expanding because players could build and exchange new levels and altered graphics.[^doom]

That was more interesting to me than the arcade had ever been. A general-purpose computer did not only contain the game. It exposed files, editors, directories, utilities, and the possibility that the player could become a maker. The relationship moved from how long one quarter lasted to what could be changed after the software arrived.

<ArcadeFigure kind="hardware" />

I did not outgrow games. Games grew into the machine I already wanted to understand.

## The machines that remain

Most modern redemption arcades feel like slot-machine floors for children. That is an impression, not a claim that every ticket game is legally gambling. The rooms I mean are organized around cards, jackpots, coin pushers, spinning lights, prize walls, and the conversion of money into a secondary currency whose value is difficult to feel.

They are bright, loud, efficient, and strangely empty of games I want to learn.

The Pinball Hall of Fame in Las Vegas feels different. Admission is free. The machines take coins. You can move through decades of mechanical and electronic design without pretending the prize counter is the point. The place is a nonprofit, and Travel Nevada reports that excess revenue supports charities.[^pinball]

That is close to what I still want from an arcade: a room full of machines with distinct physical personalities, where the money activates the object and then gets out of the way.

## The game finally became a movie

Years later, a friend and I were talking about Don Bluth. *The Secret of NIMH*. *An American Tail*. *The Land Before Time*. The conversation reached *Dragon’s Lair*, and I realized I had never seen most of it.

So we found a complete playthrough on YouTube and watched it straight through.

Without the coin slot, the delayed commands, and the repeated return to the opening rooms, the thing I had wanted in the arcade was finally available. The deaths became comic variations instead of financial penalties. The backgrounds could be watched. The character animation could breathe. The branching structure collapsed into a brisk Don Bluth movie.

It was better.

That does not mean the arcade cabinet was a mistake. The cabinet is why the animation existed, why crowds saw it, and why the image of Dirk swinging through the castle survived. The awkward machine carried a beautiful object through a technological gap. Ordinary arcade hardware could not draw that future, so *Dragon’s Lair* put the future on a disc and asked the player to press the correct direction while it played.

We thought we were walking away from an impossible game.

We were waiting for the movie to escape.
