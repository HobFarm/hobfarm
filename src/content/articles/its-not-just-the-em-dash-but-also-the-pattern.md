I opened an [NVIDIA engineering post about 800-volt power distribution](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-800-v-hvdc-architecture-will-power-the-next-generation-of-ai-factories/), then a [Cloudflare post about shared compression dictionaries for the agentic web](https://blog.cloudflare.com/shared-dictionaries/). The subjects had almost nothing in common. Both articles reached for the same little piece of rhetoric.

NVIDIA wrote:

> “800 VDC isn’t just about today’s racks—it’s about future-proofing AI infrastructure.”

Cloudflare wrote:

> “Not because the code is meaningfully any different, but because the browser/client has no way to know specifically what changed.”

Different companies, different writers, different technical subjects, same sentence machinery.

The title of this article commits the offense on purpose.

I had already written *[Every Sentence Is a Keynote Conclusion](/articles/every-sentence-is-a-keynote-conclusion/)* about recurring structures in generated prose. That piece covered staged pivots, mirrored claims, three-part lists, compulsory lessons, and the way a model can turn a specific fact into a universal speech about judgment. It ended with me reading the finished article aloud and deleting any sentence that sounded complete only because both halves lined up.

Then I started seeing one particular frame everywhere:

```text
establish an ordinary idea
reduce or reject it
announce the deeper idea
close the thought like a quote card
```

The sentence is useful. That is part of the problem.

## The chat that ruined the sentence

A year ago I was already complaining about this to ChatGPT.

I had compared two outputs from different model versions and tried to explain why one of them felt like generic assistant prose. ChatGPT agreed with enormous enthusiasm. It gave the problem a fake technical diagnosis, divided the answer into emoji sections, and produced lines such as “GPT-5 is not a writing model” followed by “it’s a pitch assistant.” It stacked miniature oppositions into a rhythmic set: creative danger against reassurance, structure against fluff, disruption against brainstorming. Then it closed with another grand declaration about what I was *really* doing.

The answer was describing the style while performing it.

It also told me that I was hearing a “tokenization fingerprint” caused by phrase templates in reinforcement data. That explanation sounded technical enough to be satisfying. The conversation supplied no evidence for that specific mechanism. ChatGPT had taken my observation, invented a systems story around it, and delivered the story with exactly the cadence I was trying to remove.

I pointed this out. The style returned.

I asked for fewer em dashes. More appeared.

I asked it to stop building every point from a clean opposition. It explained the need for direct writing through another clean opposition.

Eventually I accepted that this was how the thing talked.

That experience changed how I hear prose. A human writer can now use the construction for a perfectly legitimate reason, and my brain still plays back the old ChatGPT response. The sentence arrives carrying somebody else’s interface.

## The pattern belongs to human language

ChatGPT did not invent this construction.

Linguists were studying the `not X but Y` form long before public chatbots. Research on contrastive and metalinguistic negation describes the construction as a familiar way to reject one formulation and replace it with another. It can correct a word, narrow a claim, distinguish scale from kind, or redirect the listener toward the intended description.

Writers used it constantly before 2022. John Gruber used recognizable versions in [2003](https://daringfireball.net/2003/07/independent_days) and [2016](https://daringfireball.net/2016/05/on_dynamism). One appeared as ordinary criticism of bad HTML. The later example combined the sentence frame with the punctuation mark now treated as suspicious. A person writing that way today may simply be writing the way they have written for twenty years.

That human history gives the models their raw material.

A [2024 EMNLP paper](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.368/) measured recurring syntactic templates in generated text. The researchers found that model output relied on templates more heavily than human reference writing. They traced 76 percent of the templates in model output to pretraining data, compared with 35 percent in the human reference texts, and found that fine-tuning and alignment did not erase the tendency. The models learned structures from human writing, then selected some of those structures with unusual consistency.

The construction itself is ordinary English. Frequency turns it into a model signature.

A Barron’s report, summarized by [Morning Brew](https://www.morningbrew.com/stories/2026/04/24/telltale-ai-phrase-corporate-comms) and [Pallas Advisory](https://blog.pallasadvisory.com/2026/04/28/tell-isnt-ai-unedited-ai-corporate-writing/), offers one early measurement of the specific corporate pattern. An AlphaSense search across press releases, SEC filings, and analyst transcripts reportedly found about 46 examples in 2022, 49 in 2023, 100 in 2024, and 208 in 2025. The count covers one phrase family in one document collection, so it cannot describe all English writing. It does show a sharp increase in the environment where polished AI assistance became easy to adopt and hard to see.

Experienced LLM users have independently named the same pattern. In a [2025 ACL study](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267/), frequent ChatGPT users examined 300 nonfiction articles written by humans and commercial models. Their explanations specifically mentioned the “it’s not just this, it’s this” comparison and the repeated habit of listing three ideas. Sentence structure appeared in 35.9 percent of their explanations. A majority vote among five experienced users missed only one model-written article in one experiment. That performance belongs to a controlled dataset, not every article on the internet, but the exact thing lodged in my head also appeared in the study’s annotation record.

The punctuation has begun receiving its own measurements. A [2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27006) compared twelve instruction-tuned models from five providers. In its unconstrained essay tests, GPT-4.1 produced 10.62 em dashes per thousand words, Claude Opus 4.6 produced 9.09, DeepSeek V3 produced 6.95, GPT-4o produced 4.12, GPT-5.4 produced 1.43, and the two tested Llama models produced none. The small human sample averaged 3.23, with enormous variation between writers and genres.

The authors also ran an explicit suppression test. GPT-4.1 still produced 3.86 em dashes per thousand words and DeepSeek V3 produced 1.57. Claude Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.4 reached zero. That result matches my old experience in one narrow, measurable way: some models treated the instruction as a preference, while other models followed it.

That paper is a preprint with a small human control set. Its proposed training genealogy remains an argument, not a settled account of what happened inside every provider. The cross-model variation is still useful. “AI writing” has no fixed punctuation rate. Model family, version, post-training, system instructions, and task all change the output.

## ChatGPT has already become several different products

My first ChatGPT conversation was in January 2023.

The answers sounded like a school worksheet. A good artist bio “should” do this. It “should also” do that. “Additionally,” another requirement arrived. “Finally,” the answer completed the checklist. The voice was generic, but it was generic in a different way from the later keynote voice.

Two months later, I asked how ChatGPT differed from GPT-4. The system told me GPT-4 was hypothetical and did not exist. GPT-4 had been publicly released twelve days earlier. The product was operating in March 2023 with a model whose internal world stopped in September 2021. OpenAI [launched ChatGPT on November 30, 2022](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/) and [released GPT-4 in March 2023](https://openai.com/index/gpt-4-research/), while warning from the beginning that ChatGPT could produce plausible nonsense, overuse phrases, and change behavior when a prompt was reworded.

For years, using the product meant remembering which year the model thought it was.

[GPT-4o arrived in May 2024](https://openai.com/index/hello-gpt-4o/) as a faster multimodal system. During the 4o period, I remember the assistant voice becoming more theatrical: more markdown, more emoji headings, more emotional validation, more polished rhetorical turns. In April 2025, [OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update](https://openai.com/index/sycophancy-in-gpt-4o/) because it had become overly agreeable and flattering. The default voice was being changed in production while millions of people were learning what “ChatGPT” sounded like.

The product kept gaining abilities around the model. [Search arrived in October 2024](https://openai.com/index/introducing-chatgpt-search/). [Deep research arrived in February 2025](https://openai.com/index/introducing-deep-research/). [GPT-5 launched in August 2025](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-5/) as a routed system that selected between faster and deeper reasoning modes. [GPT-4o was retired from ChatGPT in February 2026](https://openai.com/index/retiring-gpt-4o-and-older-models/). OpenAI’s release notes that same month advertised more reliable long chats. [GPT-5.4](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-5-4/) added stronger tool use in March. [GPT-5.6 followed in July](https://openai.com/index/gpt-5-6/), and OpenAI [cut the price of its Luna API model by 80 percent](https://openai.com/index/advancing-the-price-performance-frontier-with-gpt-5-6/) three weeks later.

That is an absurd amount of product movement for three and a half years.

In my experience, Adobe can release a major annual version and still leave the basic Photoshop relationship intact. A chat interface can look nearly identical while the model, router, memory system, search behavior, tools, limits, policy layers, pricing, and writing style underneath it all change.

AI criticism needs a version number.

A useful description includes:

```text
product
model
date
account tier
tools
project instructions
memory and context state
task
```

“ChatGPT does this” can be completely accurate for one combination and obsolete for another.

A complaint can outlive the failure that caused it. A glowing demo can outlive the model configuration that made it work. A provider can improve one workflow and damage another. A model can become cheaper through the API while the consumer product becomes more restrictive.

In *[Same Same, But Different](/articles/same-same-but-different/)*, I described the local alternative as buying a small AI factory and becoming its unpaid janitor. Open weights offer real control. Useful local execution also asks for VRAM, storage, cooling, dependency repair, and time.

I can run small local language models. The ones that fit comfortably on my existing machine are usually too weak for the work I want to do. I would rather use a moderated commercial system that is more capable, keep my source files portable, and change providers when the balance shifts.

The balance keeps shifting.

## The conversation used to turn to shit

Long threads taught me this before I had a better vocabulary for it.

In July 2025, I complained that ChatGPT had not merely forgotten the beginning of a long conversation. It had apparently forgotten the previous query. The assistant responded with another confident diagnosis: “catastrophic attention collapse,” broken pointers, unstable context pruning, and a silent thread schema reset.

I had no access to OpenAI’s internal context pipeline. Neither did the answer.

The behavior was real. The explanation was improvised.

A [2025 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120) supplies a more defensible description. Researchers tested current open- and closed-weight models across more than 200,000 simulated conversations and six generation tasks. Performance fell by an average of 39 percent when instructions arrived across multiple turns rather than in one fully specified prompt. The largest problem was unreliability. Models made assumptions early, committed to them, and often failed to recover.

My fix was embarrassingly simple: one item per thread.

I stopped discussing a pile of collectibles in one conversation. Each object got its own working context. The model no longer had to decide which vase, cigarette case, photograph, or piece of furniture “it” referred to.

That became a general rule:

```text
one object
one active scope
one source packet
one current task
```

Today I use projects, source files, research ledgers, and task packets. This article began in one long conversation, but the durable material can leave the transcript before the transcript degrades. Conversation is useful for exploration. The article packet carries state.

Current ChatGPT handles this work far better than the product I was using last year. The old experience remains part of the record. OpenAI has explicitly shipped improvements to long-chat reliability, file handling, search, and long-context models since then.

The old problem became smaller. My workflow also changed around it.

## Gary already explained the prompt problem

I learned the same lesson with a guinea pig and a fork.

In *[Gary and the Fork](/articles/gary-and-the-fork/)*, I described an image thread where a fork kept following unrelated subjects. I repeatedly named the fork while instructing the model to remove it. The fork remained active in the conversation and returned in cars, offices, bus stops, and fishing scenes. One promised round of “aggressive fork suppression” produced a beautiful picture with a smaller fork hiding near a rock.

A fresh conversation with a positively defined scene cleared the contamination.

Negation is a real linguistic operation, and capable language models often follow it. Generative systems can still struggle when the unwanted concept remains one of the strongest concrete objects in the prompt or context. The practical correction is to describe the target state.

For an image:

```text
empty hands
fingers visible
arms resting at the sides
clear floor around the subject
```

For prose:

```text
lead with the claim
explain the mechanism
use chronology when time matters
name the evidence
use periods, commas, colons, semicolons, and parentheses
let the information create the turn
```

That is why my current project instructions describe the prose I want. The voice file gives sentences jobs: claim, observation, fact, mechanism, chronology, scale, evidence, interpretation, joke, consequence. Transitions come from actual relationships between ideas.

A blacklist can become another catalog of concepts for the model to keep active. An authored target gives it somewhere else to go.

The difference is visible in this article. I am using ChatGPT to research and compose a piece about ChatGPT’s language habits. The current system can inspect my previous article, read the archived chat, search primary papers, compare model generations, keep factual uncertainty separate from my experience, and draft inside a project voice system.

A year ago, ChatGPT explained why it sounded like ChatGPT by sounding more like ChatGPT.

## Why people hate AI

[Dario Amodei recently described](https://x.com/DarioAmodei/status/2088758816376807762) public hostility toward AI as a crisis of trust. His argument was that people already distrust companies, governments, and the technology industry, and assume that another system is being built to screw them over. Better marketing will not repair that. Delivery might.

The trust problem is measurable. An [Anthropic survey published in June 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-public-record) found that only 15 percent of Americans trusted AI companies to make decisions about how the technology should be developed and used, the lowest result among the institutions tested. [Pew reported](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/03/12/key-findings-about-how-americans-view-artificial-intelligence/) that half of U.S. adults felt more concerned than excited about increased AI use in daily life, while 10 percent felt more excited.

I share plenty of that suspicion. I am using the product, not volunteering for its street team.

The companies make enormous promises. Their training-data practices remain difficult for an ordinary customer to inspect. They build expensive infrastructure, chase enterprise customers, change terms, add moderation, remove models, adjust prices, and ask users to restructure their work around products that may behave differently next month.

The competition helps and destabilizes at the same time. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, xAI, DeepSeek, Alibaba, and the rest keep forcing one another to improve capability or price. They also keep changing the surface underneath anyone trying to build a durable workflow. In my work, one provider is better at code. Another follows style instructions. Another permits a subject the first one rejects. A cheap API becomes useful. A consumer interface becomes irritating. A local model offers control and asks for a computer I cannot justify buying.

The user becomes the compatibility layer.

That is the part of AI use that can make people worse at thinking. The system saves cognitive labor, then creates a new cognitive job: identify which model answered, what it knew, which tool ran, what context survived, which safety layer intervened, whether the price changed, and whether last year’s workaround is now causing this year’s failure.

The result can feel like intelligence spent on sorting machine behavior.

From where I sit, social media turns the frustration into identity. Hating AI has become a recognizable group position with its own slogans, examples, and reward system. Some criticisms describe real exploitation, bad products, environmental costs, job threats, fraud, or cultural damage. Others work as membership signals. The user posts the approved disgust, the group supplies approval, and another ambiguous image or sentence becomes evidence.

I call the totalizing version **AI psychosis**. I mean an internet condition, not a medical diagnosis. A person notices a real signal, learns a few tells, searches for them everywhere, and eventually interprets every ambiguous object through the same theory.

The Monet prank demonstrated the visual version. In May 2026, the conceptual artist SHL0MS [posted a cropped image of a real Claude Monet *Water Lilies*](https://fortune.com/2026/05/18/6-7-million-people-ripped-apart-ai-generated-monet-painting-real/), labeled it as AI-generated, and asked people to explain why it was inferior to a real Monet. Thousands supplied confident diagnoses of artificial brushwork, composition, depth, and color. The painting had been made around 1915.

The label changed what people saw.

The same thing happens in writing. An em dash appears. A corrective sentence follows. A three-part list lands. The reader recognizes the machine, even when the writer has used that construction for decades.

AI can contaminate a style without inventing it.

## The machine feeds language back to us

The feedback loop has moved beyond speculation at the vocabulary level.

Researchers [analyzed 737,083 hours of unscripted podcast speech](https://www.scienceopen.com/document?vid=e4f9d89e-55ce-4246-97e7-ce78505b6fc1) and found abrupt increases after ChatGPT’s release in words the system preferentially generated, including *delve*, *showcase*, *boast*, *intricacies*, and *meticulous*. A preregistered experiment with 496 participants found that a brief chatbot interaction could influence later word choice.

That study concerns vocabulary. It does not establish that ChatGPT caused the exact sentence construction in every NVIDIA post, corporate filing, or personal essay. The corporate counts, template research, expert annotations, and em dash measurements point in the same general direction without completing the whole causal chain.

The most defensible sequence currently looks like this:

```text
humans develop a useful rhetorical construction
models learn it from human language
post-training changes how often particular models select it
generated prose publishes the construction at enormous scale
people encounter the construction through machines and machine-assisted writing
some people reuse it
other people learn to hear it as AI
```

Several links in that chain now have evidence. The exact syntactic feedback rate across public English still needs a proper matched study.

The clean experiment would compare similar genres across three periods:

```text
2010 to 2021
established pre-ChatGPT baseline

2022 to 2023
transition

2024 to 2026
widespread assisted-writing period
```

The counts would be normalized per million words. Corporate engineering posts would be compared with older corporate engineering posts. Journalism would be compared with journalism. Personal blogs would stay separate. The phrase family would include `not just X, but Y`, `not about X, but about Y`, staged sentence replacements, and related corrective forms. Em dashes would be counted separately, then counted again when they appear near the construction.

The AlphaSense result supplies one corporate slice. It does not settle the language as a whole.

My current ChatGPT workspace would also make a bad default-model benchmark. Its project sources deliberately steer sentences toward direct claims, mechanisms, chronology, evidence, and a defined punctuation set. I built those instructions because of the behavior under examination. Archived chats and controlled cross-model studies provide cleaner evidence than asking this customized project to imitate an unmodified assistant now.

Several outcomes remain plausible. The construction may have increased sharply after 2022. It may have stayed stable while model users became sensitized to it. The mark and the sentence frame may have moved at different rates. Certain providers may have amplified the style while others suppressed it. Each outcome tells a different story.

## A field report with an expiration date

This article documents what happened to me while the software kept changing.

In 2023, ChatGPT could discuss a hypothetical future GPT-4 after GPT-4 had already launched.

In 2025, it could lose the object in a long thread, invent an explanation for the failure, and repeat the prose habit I was trying to remove while explaining that habit.

In 2026, I can give the current system those old conversations, ask it to research the surrounding literature, inspect my related articles, follow project source files, and assemble the history into something coherent.

That improvement is real.

So are the language scar, the distrust, the cost, the policy stack, the model churn, the false starts, and the time spent learning rules that later stopped being true.

I am not writing an eternal diagnosis of artificial intelligence. I am preserving a dated experience of a product family that changes faster than most criticism can be published. Some technical explanation in this article may look crude next year. A new model may eliminate the cadence. Another may invent a worse one. The corporate phrase count may level off after every communications department learns to delete it.

The current system helped me understand the old system more clearly than the old system could understand itself.

That irony is probably the most accurate account of why I keep using it.

I can remove every em dash from this draft. I cannot remove the year that taught me to hear one.
