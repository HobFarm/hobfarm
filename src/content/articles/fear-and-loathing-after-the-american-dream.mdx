I rewatched *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas*, then watched a Criterion clip of Hunter S. Thompson reviewing Terry Gilliam’s adaptation.

The setup is already a strange loop. Thompson is watching Johnny Depp play Raoul Duke, the fictionalized Thompson who moved through a reconstructed version of the Las Vegas Thompson had entered while trying to report on the original one. Writer, alter ego, actor, movie, writer again.

Then the movie shows a roadside billboard:

> **DON’T GAMBLE with MARIJUANA**<br />
> **IN NEVADA:**<br />
> **POSSESSION: 20 YEARS!**<br />
> **SALE: LIFE!**

Thompson stops to explain that the sign was real.

It was. The playing-card artwork belonged to the 1998 production, but Nevada had erected a billboard outside Las Vegas carrying essentially that warning by late 1970.[^weed-sign]

The sign looks like satire now, but the distance between then and now is not as large as it feels. In January 1998, the same year Gilliam’s movie came out, *COPS* aired a Las Vegas segment in which a man told officers that marijuana should be taxed to help pay down the national debt.[^cops] Nevada still treated possession of any amount as a potential felony at the time. Later that year, voters approved the first of two required votes for medical marijuana. The measure passed again in 2000. The Legislature implemented it and reduced low-level possession penalties in 2001. Adult use passed in 2016, legal possession began in 2017, and licensed recreational sales followed that July.[^nevada-history]

Today people smoke weed constantly around the Strip, even though public consumption remains illegal outside private property and licensed lounges.[^nevada-law]

This is not an article about weed.

The weed is a calibration device.

It shows how radically one part of the culture can change while the structure around it moves in the opposite direction. The illegal product became a regulated commodity. The quality improved. The price dropped. A friend who owns a legal grow operation once explained to me that the competition is now so aggressive that the customer can get an extraordinary deal while the producer struggles to make the numbers work.

The same kind of thing happened to televisions. The screen became larger, sharper, flatter, and cheaper.

Meanwhile, McDonald’s became expensive.

The vice became ordinary. Ordinary life became a luxury.

You can smoke the joint now. You just cannot afford the room.

Federal policy may move again, but the current process is rescheduling, not national recreational legalization. The active federal proposal would move marijuana from Schedule I to Schedule III. That would change research, taxation, and regulatory conditions without turning the state market into one legal national market.[^federal-weed]

The sign is useful because it compresses the entire argument into one image. The country stopped threatening life in prison over the product. It did not restore the affordable life that was supposedly waiting for anyone who followed the rules.

## Before the Wave Broke

I had already encountered an earlier version of this story while researching Susan Denberg.

In the mid-1960s, Denberg passed through the Stardust as part of the Bluebell company before moving into Hollywood films, television, and an August 1966 *Playboy* feature. The magazine described a movie career as “every young girl’s dream.” It presented her as a new arrival moving naturally from Europe to London, Las Vegas, Warner Bros., Desilu, and a successful future waiting just outside the page.

The existing HobFarm article follows the machinery behind that pitch: professional dancing reduced to a colorful prelude, a studio trying to rename her, a publicity profile turning work into destiny, and a career being announced at almost the same moment its known screen record was closing.[^denberg]

Susan Denberg received the polished brochure.

Five years later, Thompson inspected the boiler room.

The timing matters. Thompson’s 1965 article “The Motorcycle Gangs” for *The Nation* led into the book that established him nationally.[^angels] He was working in and around the Bay Area during the same middle-sixties period he would later remember from Las Vegas as the high-water mark, the brief instant when the scene seemed to possess enough momentum to keep going.

When *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas* appeared in *Rolling Stone* in 1971, that memory was already five or six years old. The wave passage was not a prediction. It was an obituary delivered from the next room.

One machine was still advertising upward motion through showgirls, studios, magazines, cars, and movie contracts. Another was producing the outsiders, bikers, musicians, writers, artists, activists, and freaks who believed the old structure could be outrun or replaced.

Denberg was photographed inside the brochure.

Thompson was living among the people gathering outside its margins.

The two routes meet in Las Vegas. The city can advertise transformation and expose the fraud in the same block. That is why it remains such a useful place to look at America.

## Aspen Before *Aspen Extreme*

Before Thompson reached Las Vegas, he tried local politics.

In 1970 he ran for sheriff of Pitkin County on the Freak Power ticket. The campaign was funny because Hunter Thompson running for sheriff is inherently funny, but the stakes underneath it were real. Artists, younger residents, activists, workers, and local outsiders were trying to gain power in a place already being reorganized by development and money.[^freak-power]

I knew Aspen mostly through *Aspen Extreme*, which is now more than thirty years old. Even in that movie, Aspen is a rich-person ski fantasy entered through service work, romance, luck, or crime. Two regular guys arrive from Detroit to work as instructors. One gets access to the expensive world through a wealthy woman. The other moves toward disaster.

There are no cougars in my world.

I am not the young man anymore, either. At my age, I am supposed to be the successful person with the house, the useful connections, and enough spare money to rescue somebody younger from a bad decision. Instead, I work inside expensive experiences built for people with more money than I have.

Aspen is an early version of a process I have watched repeatedly.

A place remains affordable long enough for artists, workers, musicians, cooks, writers, dropouts, organizers, and people with unstable plans to live near one another. They form a scene. The scene gives the place character. Character attracts visitors and investors. Property becomes more valuable than community. The people who created the atmosphere move outward.

Then the place sells their former presence as history.

The system removes the people and keeps their pictures.

Aspen still has Thompson. It has preserved his story, his campaign material, and the idea of Freak Power. The economic conditions that allowed a young writer and a collection of local freaks to become part of the town are much harder to reproduce.

The place survives.

The scene does not.

## Thompson or Duke?

By 1978, Thompson had another problem.

The BBC *Omnibus* documentary *Fear and Loathing on the Road to Hollywood* pairs him with Ralph Steadman on a trip from Las Vegas toward Hollywood, while also returning to Aspen and the mythology that had formed around both men.[^omnibus]

The film captures Thompson complaining that in the early days he could enter a situation, observe it, and leave with the story. By 1978, his presence altered the event before he could begin. People knew the costume. They expected the sunglasses, cigarette holder, gunfire, chemical excess, and deranged pronouncement. He was no longer sure whether somebody wanted Hunter Thompson or Raoul Duke.

The documentary proves his complaint while he is making it.

A film crew arrives to capture Hunter Thompson. The arrival creates a situation in which Thompson must decide how to perform Hunter Thompson for a documentary about the impossibility of separating the writer from the performance.

The observer has become the attraction.

The film introduces Bill Murray and Brian Doyle-Murray as Thompson’s actor friends. The timing catches another network forming around him. Both brothers had worked in the National Lampoon comedy orbit. Within two years Bill would be playing a loose Thompson figure in *Where the Buffalo Roam*, while Brian would help write *Caddyshack*.[^murrays]

That deserves a separate article. The connection from Thompson through National Lampoon, the Murray brothers, *Where the Buffalo Roam*, and *Caddyshack* is its own machine.

The larger Thompson documentary to watch alongside *Omnibus* is Alex Gibney’s *Gonzo: The Life and Work of Dr. Hunter S. Thompson*. It places the Hell’s Angels reporting, Aspen campaign, Las Vegas book, presidential politics, and the persona problem into one chronology. One of its clearest arguments is that the public Hunter Thompson eventually began consuming the writer who created him.[^gonzo]

Thompson’s problem now belongs to everybody.

Every person online is expected to maintain some version of Duke: the professional identity, the political identity, the sexual identity, the outraged identity, the aspirational identity, or the character an algorithm inferred from three seconds of attention.

My coworkers tell me X is nothing but politics or pornography. My HobFarm account gets AI models, production tools, image systems, and technology news because that is what I follow. We are using the same platform and receiving different manufactured realities.

Thompson wondered whether the visitors wanted Thompson or Duke.

The platform does not ask.

## Live Steady

Thompson later wrote this in *Fear and Loathing: On the Campaign Trail ’72*:

> “Live steady. Don't fuck around. Give anything weird a wide berth -- including people. It's not worth it. I learned this the hard way, through brutal overindulgence.
>
> ...Back to Chicago; it's never dull out there. You never know exactly what kind of terrible shit is going to come down on you in that town, but you can always count on *something*. Every time I go to Chicago I come away with scars.”
>
> — Hunter S. Thompson, *Fear and Loathing: On the Campaign Trail ’72*

He advises everyone to stay steady, avoid weird people, and keep clear of situations that create scars, then immediately heads back into political machinery that reliably creates them.

That is politics.

You can know the machine is poisonous and still be convinced that this election, this emergency, or this opponent requires one more trip inside.

In 2004, I worked with the League of Pissed Off Voters in Portland, Maine. I was studying video production, working around news, and still believed that information could fix things. A voter guide might help. A documentary might help. Maybe younger people would make different decisions if someone explained what the government and media were doing.

Then the election reduced itself to supporting a candidate most of us did not particularly want because the other one felt worse.

That was the lesson.

We no longer vote for who is good. We choose the type of shit we believe we can tolerate because the other type appears more dangerous.

The parties interpret the result as support.

The voters are told to repeat the process.

Go vote.

Vote for what?

By the time the ballot arrives, the districts have been drawn, the candidates have passed through the fundraising system, the parties have selected which races deserve support, and outside organizations have purchased their portion of the public conversation. Super PACs may accept unlimited contributions. Gerrymandering reduces many races to formalities. Donors receive access ordinary constituents rarely receive.[^political-machine]

Voting is real. Outcomes can matter. That does not make “go vote” a complete answer to a structural problem.

The public electorate selects from the ballot.

The financial electorate helps construct it.

Back then there was a group called the League of Pissed Off Voters.

Now the whole country feels like the League of Pissed Off Voters.

Everybody has a reaction. Every statement requires a response. Every bad joke becomes a moral emergency. Every event becomes evidence that one microculture is correct and another is evil.

Not everything needs a reaction.

Not everything that appears bad is important.

Not everything stupid deserves to become the organizing principle of the day.

The political system has become a content genre. The performance can be sincere. Sincerity makes the engagement machine run better.

It is hard to imagine a dream while living inside a system designed to keep everyone permanently pissed off.

## The American Expat Dream

By 2016, I had mostly stopped following American politics.

I had spent two years in Vietnam living what I thought of as the American expat dream. I could travel, take photographs, meet people from everywhere, spend time with locals and other expatriates, and build a community in Vung Tau. My Vietnamese relatives owned businesses, houses, vehicles, and comfortable lives while earning a fraction of what comparable Americans would need.

That does not mean Vietnam solved economics.

It means income is meaningless without asking what the income can buy, what the family already owns, how housing works, and whether ordinary labor can produce a durable life.

The American Dream looked more functional from outside America.

Then my passport expired.

I was married to a Vietnamese-American woman and tried to arrange a visa-exemption certificate while remaining in Vietnam. After a long bureaucratic runaround, I learned that the document and passport problem could not be solved the way I expected from inside the country. I had already been making tourist-visa runs to Cambodia, which was inconvenient but also pretty cool. I came back with photographs and stories for another article.

I could not keep rebuilding my legal status one border run at a time.

I had to return to America.

My wife had grown up in Southern California. She had never experienced a Maine winter and had never seen the place where I came from. I had not lived there in roughly fifteen years.

So we said fuck it. Let’s try Maine.

We left Santa Ana on May 1, 2016, and headed east on Interstate 10.

We were not following the election.

Politics followed us home.

## Three Hours Into America

A few hours outside Southern California, near Buckeye and the western edge of the Phoenix metro, traffic stopped.

A car had crossed the median and hit another vehicle head-on. We were close enough behind it to arrive before the whole emergency system had settled into place. Fire engines, police, wreckage, responders moving through the desert scrub. A helicopter landed on the highway and carried people away.

I assumed someone had died. I still do not know. The photographs are evidence of what we saw, not of the final outcome.

We lost half a day before the trip had properly begun.

I remember thinking: fuck, this sucks. We just started and already the country is introducing itself like this.

We carried on and reached Flagstaff for the night.

The next day we moved onto Interstate 40 and the long chain of roadside America running through Arizona, New Mexico, and the Texas Panhandle. We stopped at a Navajo and Hopi trading post, bought some things, and kept moving.

The road became driving, snacks, music, gas stations, audiobooks, and the repeated calculation of how far we could make it before one of us was too tired to continue.

I listened to Stephen King’s *It*, narrated by Steven Weber, across much of the trip.[^it-audio]

That book is almost forty-five hours long. It turns the highway into a temporary Derry. Every culvert, abandoned building, storm drain, small town, and dark basement begins offering itself as a possible entrance.

That became a problem in Texas.

## The Basement in Texas

Around Amarillo, we found the Slug Bug Ranch, a Route 66 answer to Cadillac Ranch built around half-buried Volkswagen Beetles and a decaying roadside complex.[^slug-bug]

The cars were painted, stripped, tilted, and gradually becoming something between sculpture and garbage. The surrounding motel-cafe structures looked abandoned long enough for the road itself to begin reclaiming them.

Nearby, I found a house that gave me the chills.

The outside was empty and overgrown. Inside, a large chair had been placed on top of a bed. A blanket hung over a bright window. The ordinary furniture made the building worse. It suggested that whatever had happened there had ended recently enough for the room to remember its shape.

Then I found the basement stairs.

There was probably nothing in the basement. My imagination had been listening to *It* for hours and had access to *The Texas Chain Saw Massacre*, every abandoned-house story I had ever heard, and the basic understanding that no useful version of the afternoon required me to descend into a black hole beneath a dead house in the Texas Panhandle.

I took the picture and left.

Nothing happened.

That is often the correct ending to a horror story.

We also passed the deliberately leaning Britten water tower in Groom, an old roadside advertisement designed to make drivers leave the highway and visit a truck stop that no longer exists.[^britten]

That is another version of the process: remove the function, preserve the image, let the ruin continue advertising an experience that can no longer be purchased.

## The Wall, the Coal, and the Superdelegates

In Amarillo, we stopped for breakfast in the middle of the night. A table of local police officers was talking about Trump and the border wall.

I was not following campaign schedules and do not remember where he had spoken in Texas. What mattered was what the officers were discussing. In Texas, the campaign arrived as the wall.

We continued east.

At the Interstate 44 welcome center near Joplin, Missouri, we found a mural called *Detour*, created by Matthew Dehaemers from paint and hundreds of license plates. It was built like a giant vintage postcard and road-game collage, turning the regional story into a wall of highways, faces, history, and discarded state identities.[^joplin]

The mural is almost too perfect for this story. We were moving through a national election that kept changing its message as the landscape changed. The country was one continuous surface made from state identities, old roads, local heroes, regional wounds, and the same federal campaign rearranged at every stop.

On May 4, signs along the road said “May the Fourth Be With You.” That is the kind of timestamp a road trip produces. The election was everywhere, but so were dumb roadside jokes, snacks, songs, odd museums, and the pleasure of continuing east.

By May 5 we were in Charleston, West Virginia.

Trump was there too, holding a rally built around coal. The mall was packed. People walked around wearing Trump coal shirts. The message was not subtle: the old industry could be switched back on, the regulations could be removed, and the miners could return to work.[^wv-rally]

Texas got the wall.

West Virginia got coal.

I want to be clear about what I saw. I visited Charleston, the state capital. I did not tour the small coal towns carrying the worst of the region’s collapse. Those communities have their own economic history and their own reasons for responding to the promise. I saw the promise after it had arrived in the capital as merchandise and crowd energy.

I had expected West Virginia to be miserable because most of what I knew about Appalachia outside Maine came through stereotypes and movies like *Deliverance*. Maine is the northern edge of the same broad mountain world, but Maine also tends to treat itself as its own country and ignore the rest of the region.

Charleston surprised me.

I liked it. We stayed for a couple of days, walked around, and toured the state capitol.

Then we continued north and east.

A nearby sign explained the Gettysburg Campaign and the movement of armies through the same region. The modern road crossed the old invasion route while the country conducted another campaign through screens, rallies, slogans, and merchandise.

By the time we reached Portland, my father and stepmother happened to be there for the Maine Democratic convention. My father was involved as a superdelegate aligned with Bernie Sanders. Sanders had won Maine’s caucuses, while the convention was fighting over how superdelegates should relate to the actual state result. Delegates approved a rule change aimed at making future votes proportional to the caucus outcome.[^maine-convention]

Maine got party procedure.

Everywhere we stopped, the campaign found the regional anxiety and turned it into a message.

Build the wall.

Bring back coal.

Fix the delegate system.

Stop the other candidate.

Restore the version of America this place believed it had lost.

Then we reached Bangor, and I discovered that the cheap apartments I remembered were no longer cheap.

That was the part none of the restoration campaigns could explain.

## Back in Derry

We reached the Bangor area on May 7.

One of the first places I revisited was the old Bird Conservation Club birdbath from my childhood, the object I later connected, probably incorrectly but usefully, to the birdbath in Stephen King’s *It*.

That story became [“I May Have Inspired Stephen King’s *It*”](https://hob.farm/articles/i-may-have-inspired-it/), a local-history essay about the birdbath, the Thomas Hill Standpipe, the mills, schools, and the problem of growing up inside the geography another person transformed into Derry.

Listening to *It* during the drive had turned the return into a long approach toward a fictionalized version of home.

The movie did not arrive the same week. It came out the following year, in September 2017.[^it-film] By then I was still in the Bangor area and saw it in a local theater. When familiar Bangor imagery appeared, the audience cheered.

That reaction stayed with me.

The crowd was watching its own town return through another place’s production. Bangor had become Derry, Derry had become a global horror property, and the people living inside the source geography applauded when they recognized the extraction.

That is not necessarily bad. It was fun. I cheered too.

It is also another example of the system keeping the pictures.

A place becomes a story. The story becomes an asset. The people in the place recognize themselves in the finished product and briefly receive the thrill of being visible.

## What the Road Showed Me

Hunter Thompson went to Las Vegas looking for the American Dream.

I went coast to coast and found a helicopter on the highway, a painted trading-post tipi, half-buried Volkswagens, an abandoned motel, a basement I refused to enter, a leaning water tower advertising a dead truck stop, a mural made from hundreds of expired license plates, coal shirts in a packed mall, a gold statehouse dome, a mountain cut open by the interstate, a fight over superdelegates, and a birdbath that may or may not have entered a horror novel.

Most of the trip was still driving, snacking, listening to music, and trying to reach the next room before midnight.

The road did not deliver a single revelation. It delivered a sequence.

The accident was real emergency.

The roadside attractions were old commerce becoming folk history.

The abandoned buildings were economic afterimages.

The capitol buildings were polished permanence.

The political campaign changed costume at every state line.

The hometown had become expensive while I was away.

The American Dream appeared everywhere as a restoration promise and nowhere as an operating system.

## Corpo Land and Let’s Party Town

Years later, I ended up in Las Vegas, working backstage in the city Thompson used as an autopsy table.

The modern Strip tries to contain two incompatible places in the same corridor.

One is Corpo Land: convention halls, premium events, resort packages, branded temporary environments, preferred guests, expense accounts, sports weekends, and companies buying the city by the ballroom.

The other is Let’s Party Town: weed smoke, alcohol, fake showgirls, hustlers, vomiting tourists, abandoned drinks, people pissing where they should not, and the ordinary wreckage produced when thousands of adults arrive specifically to suspend judgment.

One afternoon I was walking down Las Vegas Boulevard and saw a man smoking an enormous joint. He had created a cloud around himself. Next to him stood two fake showgirls in thongs, working the sidewalk for photographs.

At that exact moment, a family came out of a store and a little boy walked directly into the cloud.

He got a face full of weed smoke and showgirl ass.

I watched the scene and thought: what the fuck is going on here?

That is contemporary *Fear and Loathing*. No hallucinated lizards required.

The weed is normalized. The showgirls are independent photo hustlers carrying the simplified visual remains of a production tradition the casinos mostly eliminated. Families walk through the middle of it as if the Strip were Disneyland. Above everything, corporations sell the same block as a controlled premium destination.

The system removed the show and kept the costume.

Las Vegas should remain a place where adults can come to get fucked up. That is part of the city’s actual function. People should be able to drink, dance, gamble, attend a banda rave, make regrettable decisions, and wake up wondering why their shoes are wet.

There is still a limit.

At a recent banda show I worked, people became extraordinarily drunk. They vomited, pissed, created messes, and left the venue looking like several bodily systems had declared independence at once.

That is not an argument against the event or the audience. They came to party. Vegas is built around partying.

The conflict begins when the same environment is treated as an ordinary family resort while the adults disappear into the casino.

Someone I know who works in casino security has dealt with unattended children, children wandering properties alone, and parents treating hotel or bar staff as emergency childcare while they gamble and drink. Those cases do not describe every family. They reveal the contradiction built into the product.

The Strip’s main businesses are adult consumption: gambling, alcohol, nightlife, sex-adjacent spectacle, expensive entertainment, and temporary escape from ordinary responsibility.

It is not Disneyland.

It is an adult playground that discovered families also possess credit cards.

## What the Preferred Guests Leave Behind

Corporate Las Vegas presents itself as controlled abundance.

A company arrives, occupies a ballroom or convention hall, and builds a temporary world. Furniture, signs, printed graphics, carpet, fabric, screens, branded objects, food, packaging, scenery, decorative walls, and custom pieces designed for one presentation.

Then the preferred guests leave.

Sometimes moving the material elsewhere costs more than abandoning it. The financially efficient choice is to throw it away.

The event ends. The workers tear down the temporary universe and discover how much of it was always garbage with a useful life of six hours.

That waste joins everything else flowing through Las Vegas: cups, bottles, food containers, party wreckage, discarded clothes, broken promotional items, vomit, urine, and the ordinary filth produced by millions of people treating the city as a place where consequences can be left behind.

Tourists defile Vegas one body at a time.

Corporations do it by the dumpster.

The company uses invoices, loading docks, and waste contracts, which makes the destruction look professional.

This is the Vegas the premium advertising tries to hide whenever the preferred guests arrive. The luxury facade, the sidewalk hustle, the people living in flood channels, the workers on the loading dock, the abandoned event materials, the fake showgirls, and the family walking through the smoke all belong to the same city.

Thompson found a circus with a license to steal.

The license became a scalable business model.

## The City Changed Customers

Old Vegas used cheap rooms, cheap food, cheap drinks, and cheap shows to draw regular people toward the gambling floor.

The surrounding experience was bait.

Modern Vegas learned to sell every component separately.

The room is a profit center.

The meal is a profit center.

The parking is a profit center.

The concert is a profit center.

The sports event is a profit center.

The convention is a profit center.

The fee for participating in the profit center is another profit center.

Vegas is not dead.

It changed customers.

The ordinary tourist still arrives, but the dependable machinery is corporate events, conventions, premium entertainment, and people spending money earned somewhere else or charged to somebody else.

That is the nature of my work. I do live production. Back in another version of Las Vegas, somebody in my union might have spent more time on Elvis, a resident production show, or a showgirl spectacle designed to make the casino trip irresistible. Now corporate events are a major part of the schedule.

The work exists because the city can build a temporary universe for thousands of people and remove it overnight.

I make around thirty-five dollars an hour. That sounds like good money because it is good money in many parts of the country.

If I made that in West Virginia, I would probably be comfortable.

The job is not in West Virginia.

My job is in casinos, arenas, hotel ballrooms, convention centers, concert venues, and large entertainment markets. The affordable places do not contain the volume of work. The places containing the work charge me for permission to remain near it.

People say “move somewhere cheaper” as if a job were a file I could drag onto another desktop.

Fine. I will move.

Send the Strip, the Sphere, the hotel conventions, the touring acts, the production calls, and the union contract over when you get a chance.

The cheap place and the good call exist in different locations.

I am paid enough to remain available to the machine.

Backstage access is not class mobility.

## The Future Got Cheap

The weird part is that some things genuinely improved.

The weed got better and cheaper.

The television got better and cheaper.

The feed is free.

The products used to escape ordinary life have become astonishingly accessible.

Ordinary life has not.

Housing became expensive.

Food became expensive.

Concerts became expensive.

Healthcare became expensive.

Insurance became expensive.

Time became expensive.

Community became difficult because everyone is working, moving, recovering, or staring into a personalized machine designed to keep them agitated.

My grandfather worked as a milkman, supported a family of seven, and owned a house he bought for fifteen hundred dollars.

My wife and I both make around thirty-five dollars an hour. We have no children. We rent and remain financially squeezed.

The mythology says this must be my fault.

I moved too much. I chose unstable industries. I failed to buy the correct house at the correct moment. I did not invest in the correct object. I fucked around.

I did fuck around.

I also found out.

A friend’s brother bought a Las Vegas house for around one hundred thousand dollars after the crash. He recently sold it for around five hundred thousand, bought another house in New Hampshire, and carried the remaining equity with him.

He did not create five times more shelter.

He owned the shelter while the market repriced it.

For the person who already owned the house, the crisis produced wealth.

For the person arriving later, the same house became evidence of personal failure.

In 2018, I could rent a three-bedroom Las Vegas house for around thirteen hundred dollars. The same kind of house is around two thousand now.

It is still cheaper than Portland, which says more about Portland than it does about Las Vegas.

The American Dream was never equally available. By the time I was old enough to pursue even its selective version, the offer was already being withdrawn.

Thompson was documenting the failure before I was born.

## The Dream Becomes Vulgar

The strangest part is that the phrase itself now sounds contaminated.

Say “the American Dream” and people assume you are selling a mortgage, delivering a campaign speech, or trying to make America something again.

The phrase arrives with political metadata before anybody hears what it means.

I am not talking about nationalism.

I am not talking about returning to an imaginary 1950s social order.

I am talking about a basic promise:

Useful work should eventually produce a durable life.

A place to live.

Some security.

Some time.

A family, if you want one.

The ability to build something without one rent increase, medical bill, or lost month destroying it.

Enough stability to know other people long enough to form a community.

That should not belong to a political tribe.

But it is hard to have a dream when everyone is living inside a permanent reaction machine.

Back then there was one League of Pissed Off Voters.

Now everybody has a voter guide.

Everybody has an emergency.

Everybody has an enemy.

Everybody has a Duke.

The American Dream did not simply die. It was repriced, converted into investment inventory, reduced to individual blame, and handed over to political branding.

Finally, even admitting that you miss it began sounding like a declaration of allegiance.

## Building a Small Replacement

That is why I am building HobFarm.

I am not building the website because I hate Las Vegas and want to escape.

I like Las Vegas.

I want enough leverage that remaining here becomes a choice.

My production income is geographically locked. The website is an attempt to make part of my income portable, scalable, and owned by the work rather than the location.

Articles, images, characters, tools, products, memberships, and whatever else grows from it can travel.

Internet money can arrive in Las Vegas, Maine, Vietnam, or somewhere I have not considered yet.

This is not the fantasy where I quit my job, become a creator, and pretend to work beside a swimming pool.

I like building shows.

I would prefer that my entire financial life did not depend on remaining available to somebody else’s machine.

The first meaningful victory is simple:

The website pays for the house.

Then the union money can become savings, equipment, travel, investment, or time. Work becomes something I choose because it is worth doing, rather than the only structure keeping everything upright.

Hunter Thompson went to Las Vegas looking for the American Dream and found a circus with a license to steal.

I crossed the country and found wrecked cars, roadside monuments, abandoned attractions, regional campaign slogans, statehouse grandeur, repriced hometowns, and a birdbath waiting at the end of the road.

Then I ended up backstage in the same city Thompson used to explain the country.

The show is still running.

The joint is legal.

The television is enormous.

The rent is due.

The people building the spectacle cannot afford a seat.

It is hard to have a dream when you are living a nightmare.

It is harder when the nightmare keeps insisting it is the dream.
