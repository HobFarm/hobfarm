## The wall above the stairs

The staircase at Atlatl Rock rises along a cliff in Nevada's Valley of Fire. From the ground, the landing looks like another piece of park hardware bolted against sandstone. At the top, a dark panel opens across the rock. Bighorn sheep, circles, lines, human figures, and shapes that resist ordinary nouns have been pecked through desert varnish.

I photographed the panel on May 19, 2020. The day matters because a photograph is its own mark in time. It records where I stood, what the light revealed, which figures I noticed, and what the park allowed a visitor to see from the platform. It does not date the petroglyphs.

The interpretive sign beside the panel says petroglyphs have existed in the region for more than 4,000 years. Nevada State Parks' current history page gives a narrower attribution for the park's most obvious carvings: Basketmaker people about 2,500 years ago, followed later by Early Pueblo culture.[^valley-history] Those statements address different scopes. Neither one supplies a direct date for every figure in my photograph.

That gap is the subject.

The carvings are the most visible human layer on this cliff. They are also recent beside the history underneath them. Hominins had already lived, moved, adapted, divided, and met one another for millions of years. Lakes had spread across land that is now desert. Coastlines had exposed plains now under the sea. Animals had crossed regions where their species no longer live. The panel survives near the top of a much deeper and less visible file.

## A modern wall with better metadata

Six months later, I photographed a wall in Rhyolite, Nevada. The mining town began in the first decade of the twentieth century and emptied almost as quickly. Roofless buildings and broken walls turned it into a place where later visitors kept adding names, dates, messages, and drawings.

One addition reads **COVID-19** in large white letters. I know the photograph was made on November 16, 2020 because the camera kept a timestamp. The word identifies a historical event still underway when I arrived. The wall has an addressable location, a construction history, and other inscriptions with legible names and years. It is a rich record compared with an undated line on desert varnish.

It remains incomplete.

The photograph does not identify the writer. It does not say whether the message marked fear, boredom, grief, disbelief, or the simple pleasure of getting paint onto a ruin. Even with a readable word and a date narrowed to months, motive escapes the frame.

That makes Rhyolite a useful control. Archaeological interpretation often begins with less. A figure may have no attached language, no surviving eyewitness, no excavated object in direct association, and no certain relationship to the marks beside it. The image is evidence that somebody acted. Every additional claim needs its own support.

## Humans keep making walls into records

Petroglyph and pictograph name different techniques. A petroglyph is cut, pecked, incised, abraded, or otherwise worked into a rock surface. A pictograph places pigment on the surface. Sites frequently contain more than one method, and later people can add marks to a panel already old.[^nps-markings]

The behavior spans the world. Hands were stenciled against cave walls. Animals were carved into exposed rock. Human figures were painted above rivers. Travelers wrote names and scripts along desert routes. Film workers altered a prehistoric panel to make it show up on camera. Tourists scratched initials into stone. A pandemic became a word on a ghost-town wall.

Those actions should not be collapsed into one motive. A boundary marker, a hunting record, a ceremonial image, a family history, an instruction, a prayer, a boast, and vandalism can share a surface without sharing a purpose. Similar shapes can arise from different ideas. Different shapes can serve the same social work.

The map below makes a smaller claim. Durable images recur across continents and across an enormous range of dates. Some sites preserve long sequences. Others preserve a minimum age for one image. The points do not form a migration route, and the newest securely dated discovery does not make every neighboring image equally old.

<RockArtWorldMap />

The oldest demonstrated minimum age now belongs to a hand stencil at Liang Metanduno on Muna Island, part of Sulawesi. A 2026 *Nature* paper dated mineral crust over the stencil to 71,600 plus or minus 3,800 years, establishing a minimum age of 67,800 years.[^sulawesi] Chauvet Cave in France preserves more than a thousand images, with its earliest phase dated around 30,000 to 32,000 years ago. A rockfall sealed the cave around 20,000 years ago, protecting the interior until its 1994 discovery.[^chauvet]

Hima in Saudi Arabia records another kind of span. People used a route with water sources for at least 7,000 years. Petroglyphs and inscriptions accumulated in Musnad, South Arabian, Thamudic, Greek, and Arabic scripts, with travelers continuing to leave marks into recent centuries.[^hima] The archive did not close after one culture finished its work.

## The Sahara changed around the images

The Sahara is where the relationship between image and environment becomes hard to ignore.

Tadrart Acacus in Libya contains thousands of paintings in very different styles dated across a range from about 12,000 BCE to 100 CE. UNESCO describes them as a record of major changes in fauna, flora, and ways of life.[^acacus] Across the border, Tassili n'Ajjer in Algeria contains more than 15,000 known drawings and engravings. Its record includes water-dependent animals, pastoral cattle scenes, horses, and later camels.[^tassili]

An elephant carved into Saharan rock is evidence of an image and of somebody's decision to make it. When the animal appears within a broader archaeological and paleoenvironmental record, it also contributes to a landscape history. It places a species, a memory of a species, or a transmitted image in a region where an elephant now looks ecologically out of place.

The green periods were recurrent rather than singular. A paleoclimate synthesis identified more than 230 African humid episodes over the past eight million years. Stronger monsoons repeatedly expanded savanna and water systems northward, then weakened and fragmented those connections.[^green-sahara] The last major humid interval created lakes, rivers, grasslands, and travel corridors across regions now dry.

Lake Mega-Chad gives that vanished water a surviving outline. Around 7,000 years ago, the lake covered more than 400,000 square kilometers, slightly more than the modern Caspian Sea. Elevation data still reveals beach ridges and sand spits hundreds of kilometers from modern Lake Chad.[^mega-chad]

Rock art survives this kind of transformation because stone can remain after an ecosystem reorganizes. The image may outlast the species distribution it depicts, the shoreline used to reach it, the population that made it, and the language that once explained it. Survival preserves information and separates it from context at the same time.

## Seven million years will ruin a clean diagram

Popular human-evolution graphics like a parade: one figure straightens into the next until a modern person walks off the right edge. The evidence is messier.

*Sahelanthropus tchadensis* lived in Chad between seven and six million years ago. *Homo erectus* appears much later and persisted across a huge range from about 1.89 million to 110,000 years ago.[^smithsonian-human-origins] Our own species emerged within Africa hundreds of thousands of years ago. Genetic evidence indicates connections among weakly differentiated ancestral populations inside Africa rather than one sealed founding population.[^african-stem]

Outside Africa, modern humans met and reproduced with other hominins. A 2024 review found evidence for at least three introgression events from distinct Denisovan populations into modern humans.[^denisovan-introgression] Neanderthal ancestry supplies another clear record of contact. The family diagram needs branches that reconnect, populations that overlap, and long periods where the surviving evidence is thin.

<DeepTimeFrames />

The nested frames expose an uncomfortable proportion. The 67,800-year minimum age for the Sulawesi hand is startling within recorded history. Inside seven million years of hominin evolution, it occupies the recent end. The same is true of almost every visible mark discussed here.

Rock art is ancient on a human calendar. On the hominin clock, it is surviving media from the closing portion of the record.

## Evidence is stronger than motive

A responsible reading begins with what the surface can carry.

At Atlatl Rock, I can establish that the marks are pecked into a varnished face, that figures occupy the same panel, and that differences in weathering are visible. Archaeological work can compare style, superposition, tool traces, mineral deposits, nearby material, and regional traditions. Direct dating may sometimes provide a minimum or maximum age. Each method narrows the problem.

Meaning is a different claim. An atlatl-shaped figure may support an identification of an object. It does not reveal the complete occasion for making it. A sheep figure establishes a recognizable form more securely than it establishes a story about hunting ritual. Spirals invite interpretation because they are familiar and abstract at once. Familiarity can become a trap.

The National Park Service offers several possible functions for rock markings, including records, celebrations, maps, and practical knowledge, then states the central limit: many meanings remain uncertain.[^nps-markings] That is a useful evidence ladder. Technique and location sit near the bottom. Relative sequence and dating require more work. Cultural affiliation requires regional evidence and living knowledge. A specific motive belongs higher still.

Alteration adds another layer. At Barker Dam in Joshua Tree, the red and white forms in my 2010 photographs look unusually vivid. A Joshua Tree National Monument park newspaper from 1992 records why: a film crew painted over authentic carvings in an attempt to make them more visible.[^barker-receipt] The notice does not name the production, the workers, or a date. Repeated online claims often supply those missing details without showing the receipt.

The safe statement is already dramatic. A film production modified a prehistoric site for legibility. The intervention became part of the object's history, while its own documentation remained incomplete.

## Part of the map is missing

Archaeology is biased toward places where evidence can survive and be found. Dry caves, sheltered walls, durable stone, and exposed deposits become legible. Coastlines, river mouths, floodplains, acidic soils, and continental shelves can remove, bury, scatter, or submerge the record.

At the last glacial maximum, global sea level was roughly 120 to 130 meters lower than today. The land between Britain and continental Europe included plains, rivers, wetlands, and human habitat now called Doggerland. By about 7,000 years ago, the North Sea and English Channel were close to their present extent.[^submerged-england]

Finds still return from that drowned landscape. Krijn, the first recognized Dutch Neanderthal, is represented by a skull fragment dredged from the North Sea. A bison bone around 13,500 years old carries a zigzag design whose function remains unknown. Fishing and aggregate extraction recovered the objects without the controlled layers an excavation would provide.[^north-sea]

Penghu 1 came from another submerged corridor. A fishing net brought the robust jaw up from the Penghu Channel west of Taiwan. The channel had been dry land during Pleistocene low sea levels. The find lacks a precise stratigraphic context, and its possible age remains broad. Ancient protein analysis published in 2025 identified the jaw as male Denisovan, expanding the known geographic range of that population.[^penghu-2015][^penghu-2025]

<SubmergedRecord />

This missing ground changes how absence should be read. A blank coastal map may mean nobody made images there. It may also mean the campsite lies under sediment and seawater, the painted surface eroded, the object was trawled out of context, or nobody has found it yet.

The visible distribution of rock art is a map of past behavior filtered through geology, preservation, access, research history, and luck.

## The Southwest keeps changing too

Atlatl Rock can feel fixed because the cliff is still there. The wider Southwest was never still.

Tule Springs, north of Las Vegas, preserves repeated wetlands between about 100,000 and 12,500 years ago. Columbian mammoths, camels, horses, bison, and other animals lived around spring-fed ponds, marshes, and streams.[^tule-springs] The fossil beds sit in a desert valley now crossed by roads and subdivisions.

Farther south, the Salton Trough repeatedly held Lake Cahuilla when the Colorado River entered the basin. Archaeological evidence records fishing, birds, shoreline settlements, and human use around high stands, while the specific communities around the lake varied through time and place. There was no single timeless "Lake Cahuilla people." The Torres Martinez Desert Cahuilla Indians remain a living nation in the basin, with a documented history in Martinez Canyon and an active cultural-preservation program.[^lake-cahuilla][^torres-martinez]

The marks I photographed near the Salton Sea belong to a much shallower layer. One says **1877**. Another reads **B. Moore 1919**. They are recent enough to tempt a search for a named person and a single story. The photographs establish inscriptions on varnished desert rock. They do not establish who made them, whether the dates mark the year of carving, or what those people were doing in the basin.

Tule Springs changed from wetland to desert. Lake Cahuilla filled and receded. The modern Salton Sea arrived after a canal failure and Colorado River flood in 1905. Rock panels accumulated ancient and historic marks. The region did not wait for human observers before changing state, and people never stopped entering the record.

## The recent part

Back at Atlatl Rock, the wall holds because stone survived long enough for one set of actions to remain visible.

The figures do not supply a complete story. They supply marks, relationships, materials, and a place. Archaeology adds dates where it can, compares traditions, studies overlapping lines, and rebuilds environmental context. Genetics reveals meetings no wall recorded. Fossils recover populations without names. Drowned landscapes warn that the map itself has lost territory.

The right response to missing context is not to drain the image of meaning. It is to keep the strength of each claim attached. A sheep is visible. A technique can be described. A minimum age may be measured. A regional tradition may be supported. A motive can remain open.

The rock art is the recent part because the human story began long before the surviving image and continued long after its explanation stopped traveling with it.

The file survived. The documentation didn’t.
