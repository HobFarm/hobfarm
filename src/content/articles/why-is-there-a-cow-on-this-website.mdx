*I photographed this cow at Date Fest in Indio, California, in 2011. I was there volunteering at the Salton Sea State Recreation Area booth. Fifteen years later, the picture finally got a job.*

This cow has been waiting fifteen years to become a hero image.

I photographed it at Date Fest in Indio, California, in 2011. I was there volunteering at the Salton Sea State Recreation Area booth. Around the same time, I was building three websites: one for a real estate agent, one for the Sea and Desert Interpretive Association, and one for the Salton Sea History Museum.

The cow had nothing to do with any of that. It stuck its tongue out, I took a picture, and the file followed me into the future.

I posted it on social media at some point. As usual, almost nobody saw it. The feed absorbed the picture, gave it whatever microscopic opportunity it was going to get, and moved on. That is what feeds do. Every photograph becomes a tiny audition conducted in front of an audience that may never be shown the stage.

Now the cow is on HobFarm.

Why is a site full of AI workshops, anime characters, avatars, experimental image-generation apps, strange cars, ancient rivers, African national parks, rock art, abandoned infrastructure, and an Alice game that no longer contains Alice using a tongue-out cow as the hero image for an article?

Because it is funny.

More importantly, I already had it.

That is increasingly what HobFarm is about: taking a large archive of things I have made, photographed, written, researched, or simply noticed, then finding new ways to connect and reuse them.

From the outside, the site currently looks like several unrelated websites crashed into one directory. From inside the work, the same process keeps repeating. I notice something. I keep it. I follow it. A later idea gives it context. A newer tool gives me another way to use it.

The structure appears after enough of that happens.

## A place to put the things

I am tired of putting work into social platforms that treat every post as temporary feed material.

The formatting changes. The preferred image dimensions change. The platform decides whether a follower who deliberately followed you should actually see what you posted. The feed keeps demanding familiar engagement rituals from people who want their work distributed: ask a question, provoke a reaction, manufacture a disagreement, post at the correct hour, comment on your own post, and keep supplying the machine with signs of activity.

Eventually everything begins sounding like some variation of:

> What's the matter, you don't like girls like me? Comment something so I can sell you shit, improve my engagement numbers, and make money.

I have no interest in building HobFarm around that.

A website gives the cow somewhere permanent to stand.

I control the page, the sequence, the crop, the caption, the related links, and the amount of space the subject deserves. An article can be discovered tomorrow or four years from now. An old photograph can acquire context long after the platform that ignored it has become unusable, irrelevant, or both.

I still need to make the pages technically legible. Titles, descriptions, links, structured data, image names, captions, and useful internal navigation give search engines a fair chance to understand what is here. That is less glamorous than chasing engagement, but it produces a durable object instead of another item falling through a feed.

There is also RSS.

RSS is simple. HobFarm publishes a feed containing its new articles. Copy that feed address into Thunderbird or another feed reader, and the reader checks it for new posts. Thunderbird supports dedicated feed accounts and subscriptions, then presents new items much like messages in an inbox.[^thunderbird-rss] No recommendation system needs to decide that today's article about Zambia should be replaced with a clip specifically selected to make you angry.

The website is the publication. Social platforms can point toward it when they are useful. They no longer get to be the place where the work lives.

## The archive was already there

A lot of these articles begin because I finally find a use for an image I wanted to use years ago.

I had photographs of overflowing trash cans. [A landfill article gave them a home](/articles/trash-mountain/).

I had an old Buffalo Bill image announcing “I AM COMING” that was too good to leave buried in a folder. [Eventually the right article appeared](/articles/they-had-names-doll-family/).

I have years of photographs from Vietnam and Cambodia, along with actual experience in both places and ties to the Vietnamese-American community. Those will become articles when the right questions pull them forward.

I have a large archive of Salton Sea material, and the Salton Sea will generate many more articles. The cow itself entered the archive through that part of my life. I was at Date Fest because of the Salton Sea booth. The Salton Sea led to websites, volunteering, photographs, history, infrastructure, environmental questions, and now a cow introducing an article about archives.

I also have farm pictures. Lots of them.

<FarmArticleFigure kind="tongues" />

The cow came from Date Fest in 2011. The goat and horse came from the Blue Hill Fair in Maine in 2017.

I did not set out to produce a photographic study of farm animals sticking their tongues out. I saw animals making faces and took pictures. Years later, the pattern is obvious because there is finally enough material to make it visible.

One photograph looks random.

Three photographs become a collection.

Enough collections begin to look like a publication.

This is why I do not need to decide in advance whether HobFarm is an AI site, a travel site, a car site, a history site, a photography site, or a farm site. Those categories may become useful later. Right now they would mostly describe the containers before enough material exists to know what belongs together.

The archive can coagulate first.

## The horse has had several careers

The horse provides a nearly complete demonstration of how this works.

<FarmArticleFigure kind="horse-before-after" />

I photographed it at the Blue Hill Fair in 2017. The original image includes a halter, rope, wooden stall, bucket, and a red decoration rising out of its mane. The horse has its tongue slightly out and looks tired of participating in whatever humans had scheduled for it that day.

Later, I manually removed the halter, rope, stall, and surrounding material in Photoshop. I reconstructed the obscured areas, cleaned the edges, and isolated the horse. The work took an hour or two.

The resulting cutout became the profile picture for another account of mine. It is still in use, including on Spotify. The horse never disappeared into the archive completely. It became a small piece of durable identity infrastructure.

Years later, I brought the same Photoshop horse into ChatGPT and asked it to become an Instagram influencer.

<FarmArticleFigure kind="horse-influencer" />

The result may be a better influencer than anything Instagram currently pushes at me.

The horse has a ring light. It has heart-shaped glasses. It has avocado toast and a green drink. A neon sign says “good hay only.” The phone is aimed correctly. The entire scene has been optimized for the recognizable visual language of lifestyle content.

At least the horse is honest about being a horse.

It is not pretending that a sponsored product changed its life. It is not asking whether you would still date it. It does not need you to comment “YES” to prove that good people support horses. It has no supplement code, no manufactured grievance, and no reason to convert your reaction into recurring revenue.

A photograph from 2017 became a Photoshop project, then a profile image, then source material for a generated joke, then visual evidence in an article about how HobFarm works.

That is recombination.

## The goat took about a minute

The goat demonstrates the practical difference between the old workflow and the new one.

<FarmArticleFigure kind="goat-before-after" />

The original goat photograph has a metal gate cutting through the frame and ropes crossing above its head. I wanted a clean portrait.

In the older Photoshop workflow, I would select each unwanted object, remove it, clone or paint new material into the empty space, reconstruct the background, clean the edges around the fur, and keep correcting the image until the edit stopped attracting attention.

That is essentially what I did with the horse.

With the goat, I painted over the gate and ropes and let generative fill reconstruct the missing parts. The result is not completely perfect. It does not need to be perfect for this example. It took about a minute.

The horse took an hour or two.

The goat took about a minute.

The idea did not come from AI. The original photograph did not come from AI. The judgment that the gate and rope were distracting did not come from AI. The decision that the result was sufficient did not come from AI.

AI compressed the tedious middle.

That is also what it does for the articles.

I do not need AI to notice that a national park in Zambia receives an absurdly small number of visitors. I do not need it to wonder whether visible river channels across Chad once carried water. I do not need it to remember an old photograph, become interested in an unusual Cadillac, or decide that a cow belongs at the beginning of an article.

Those are the inputs that matter.

AI helps me research several related questions, compare numbers, organize sources, inspect possible structures, identify missing receipts, work through visual ideas, and turn a long discussion into a production packet. I still have to keep the point clear, reject wrong turns, distinguish evidence from interpretation, and decide what the article is actually saying.

The useful claim is not that AI gave me the ability to do the work.

It changed how much of the work I can realistically finish.

## I had to relearn how to build websites

The same shift happened with HobFarm itself.

When I took the cow photograph in 2011, I was building websites by hand. I would design a wireframe, interpret the layout, decide which div belonged inside which other div, construct the HTML, then write the CSS for margins, padding, alignment, columns, navigation, and every other visible relationship on the page.

Then came the text system: `h1`, `h2`, `h3`, `h4`, paragraphs, links, lists, captions, and all the exceptions that appear once real content enters a design.

None of this was mysterious. It was granular, repetitive, and incredibly time-consuming.

I became good enough at implementing websites that the transferable skill became professional work. The machinery surrounding my ideas gradually became the thing I was being paid to build.

WordPress saved time on some of the presentation work. It also turned me into the security department, database administrator, plugin referee, PHP debugger, and MySQL technician. The abstraction removed one category of labor and introduced several others.

AI-assisted development changed the job again.

I had to relearn how to build a website around Astro, Cloudflare, VS Code, GitHub, agents, repository instructions, `AGENTS.md` files, Google Cloud, Alibaba Cloud, authentication systems, APIs, deployment pipelines, and an expanding collection of command-line tools.

This is far more complicated than writing a CSS file by hand.

At the same time, an individual feature is much easier to produce.

I can describe the behavior, provide the repository context, let an agent build the implementation, inspect what it did, test the result, and send it back through another revision. A website project that might have consumed a year of spare time can now move forward in a week.

The stack became more complicated so the work I care about could become simpler.

AI also took a bite out of the kind of implementation work I used to do professionally. I have a better job now, so fuck it.

More importantly, the same technology that threatens some of the work I used to get paid for has made the work I originally wanted to do practical again. Fifteen years ago, I could spend an evening developing an idea or spend that evening fixing a layout. The layout usually won because nothing else could proceed until the container existed.

Now an agent can handle much of the container while I figure out why Kafue National Park has lions, elephants, and almost no visitors.

That is a better allocation of my time.

## It saves time by creating more work

None of this works by opening a free account and typing some shit into a box.

I have spent a lot of time building the project context that helps ChatGPT understand what a HobFarm article is supposed to do. The system includes an editorial lens, prose rules, research and receipt procedures, an article workflow, a visual editorial system, quality checks, and instructions for handing finished material to Codex.

I keep reconfiguring it.

A model can still seize on the wrong implication, interpret an observation as a different argument, flatten a joke, overstate a claim, or confidently begin writing an adjacent article I never asked for. I can reduce those failures with better context, examples, constraints, and correction loops. I cannot eliminate them.

Every useful instruction comes from seeing the system fail in a particular way.

Every new instruction can create a different failure somewhere else.

The work has moved from manually producing every sentence, CSS rule, and pixel toward designing and supervising systems that produce useful drafts, code, research packets, and visual assets. The direct labor falls. The orchestration work grows.

That is why AI can save an enormous amount of time while also creating more work.

The equation still works at my current volume. Building an elaborate editorial system for one article would be ridiculous. I am publishing every day. The setup cost can be spread across hundreds of pieces.

Once the context exists, I can drop a cow, goat, and horse into the project and ChatGPT already has a reasonable chance of understanding why we are looking at them.

Reasonable chance is doing some work in that sentence.

## One article a day becomes a corpus

I have published one article a day for the past month. As I write this, I am about two weeks ahead, and I want the scheduled queue to reach a month.

Most of the articles are longer than 2,000 words. They require images that need to be found, inspected, renamed, processed, captioned, uploaded, and placed. A substantial article can still take most of a day, but I can usually develop two or three at the same time and schedule them as they finish.

The daily cadence changes the archive quickly.

One article a day for five years produces roughly 1,825 articles. At a baseline of 2,000 words each, that is more than 3.6 million words, before counting photographs, diagrams, screenshots, source ledgers, research notes, generated assets, captions, code, and unfinished branches.

That is no longer a pile of blog posts.

It is a corpus.

Five years from now, I may be able to hand a much better AI system one hundred old HobFarm articles and ask it to find the book hiding inside them.

I do not mean concatenating the articles and writing a generic summary. I mean finding the ideas that kept reappearing under different subjects, locating accidental series, identifying places where my thinking changed, matching photographs to later research, finding contradictions worth preserving, and showing me which chapters still need new reporting.

The same archive could produce videos, visual essays, maps, regional collections, photo books, documentary outlines, updated editions, or projects that cannot be identified yet because most of their source material has not been made.

Future AI cannot discover patterns in work that was never created.

The useful thing I can do now is keep creating the material.

## A mesh instead of a filing cabinet

<FarmArticleFigure kind="node-mesh" />

A conventional site taxonomy is a tree. This article goes in one category. That category sits under a larger heading. The navigation gives every subject one approved location.

My interests work more like a mesh.

The Salton Sea connects to volunteering, Date Fest, the cow, old websites, local history, water infrastructure, environmental change, desert photography, and future articles that do not exist yet.

The horse connects to the Blue Hill Fair, farm photography, Photoshop, profile images, generative AI, social-media satire, and this article.

An article about Chad connects rock art, ancient rivers, human migration, satellite imagery, climate history, archaeology, and the practical problem of getting there.

Zambia connects the State Department map, neglected media coverage, Google Earth, Kafue National Park, safari infrastructure, tourism statistics, and the fact that Las Vegas processes a year of Kafue visitors before lunch.

Vietnam and Cambodia will connect first-hand photographs, travel, history, architecture, infrastructure, and the Vietnamese-American community.

The AI workshop connects image generators, apps, characters, avatars, coding systems, failed experiments, production tools, and whatever those tools later help me make.

Each finished article adds a node. Links, shared images, repeated subjects, and later discoveries create the edges.

The site currently looks random because the graph is young.

## Anyway, there is this park in Zambia

This whole article began while I was trying to get back to the Africa material.

I would like to go to Chad. That trip will probably have to wait a decade or two. While looking at the State Department's travel-advisory map, I noticed that Zambia was Level 1, “Exercise Normal Precautions,” the same overall advisory category as Canada.[^travel-advisories]

So I opened Google Earth.

That led me to Kafue National Park, a massive landscape containing lions, elephants, leopards, wild dogs, hippos, buffalo, and an extraordinary range of other wildlife.[^kafue-wildlife]

African Parks, Kafue's co-manager, reported 23,828 visitors in 2024, the highest annual attendance in the park's history.[^kafue-visitors] Las Vegas recorded 38.5 million visitors in 2025, an average of about 105,500 per day.[^las-vegas] Las Vegas receives a Kafue year of visitors in roughly five hours and twenty-five minutes.

That is the next article.

Why does a random site called HobFarm, which contains an AI workshop for making anime chicks, an elf-like Alice, an Alice game without Alice, image-generation apps, weird cars, old Photoshop horses, and a cow with its tongue out keep publishing articles about Africa?

I do not have a formal answer.

Africa keeps being more interesting than trying to make an AI generate another picture that does not suck.

So I will keep publishing one article a day, keep building the archive, keep connecting the nodes, and let the larger structure show up when enough of it exists.

Maybe eventually somebody in Zambia, Chad, or somewhere else in Africa will find the site and invite me to come take pictures of their rad country.

Maybe nobody gives a shit for a while.

Either way, the cow finally has a page.
