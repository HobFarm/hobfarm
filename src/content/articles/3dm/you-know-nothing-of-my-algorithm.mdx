Frank McHugh opened the wrong door.

I was not looking for Marshall McLuhan, experimental television, recommendation systems, or a theory of why every social platform eventually fills with the same video wearing twelve different wigs. I was looking at Frank McHugh's credits.

That is how 3DM works. A familiar supporting face becomes a production, the production becomes a network experiment, and the experiment becomes an argument about the machine currently deciding what you see.

One of McHugh's television credits is an episode called *To Wally Pantoni We Leave a Credenza*. NBC broadcast it on March 31, 1968, as part of *NBC Experiment in Television*. John Guare wrote the play. TIME's listing describes an elderly couple worried about leaving a cherished possession without an heir. McHugh was there. The accessible listings do not agree cleanly enough about his exact role or the director for me to print either as settled fact.

That uncertainty is useful. The credit is not the conclusion. It is the doorway.

## What NBC was experimenting with

*NBC Experiment in Television* ran from 1967 into 1971. It was not one continuing drama and it was not a normal weekly variety show. It was an anthology slot where the network could test forms that did not fit the usual schedule.

The surviving record is unusually broad. The Peabody archive describes original plays, a program associated with Harold Pinter, and Federico Fellini's first television venture. The series introduced television audiences to work by John Guare. Jim Henson and Jerry Juhl made *The Cube* after NBC funded the project through the same experimental unit. A National Theatre of the Deaf program put Deaf actors using sign language on nationwide American television. In 1968, editor Peter V. Punzi received an Emmy nomination for work on the series.

This does not prove that late-1960s network television was secretly a permanent artists' laboratory. The anthology was exceptional because most network hours were not built this way. A broadcaster with a fixed schedule could still reserve one hour, often on Sunday afternoon, for a program whose purpose was to discover what television might do.

NBC was experimenting with television.

The important distinction is what happened to the audience. Viewers could respond by watching, changing the channel, writing a letter, or affecting later ratings. They were not reorganizing the program in real time. The signal traveled mainly one way.

> **NBC experimented with television. Social media experiments on the audience.**

## McLuhan inside the machine

On March 19, 1967, the anthology broadcast *This Is Marshall McLuhan*. The McLuhan Foundation identifies it as a 51-minute film produced by Ernst Pintoff, narrated by Edward Binns, and built around McLuhan explaining his work inside the medium he was analyzing.

This is nearly too perfect. McLuhan argued that a medium matters because of the changes in scale, pace, and pattern it introduces into human affairs. The content matters, but the arrangement around the content may matter more. A railroad can carry wheat or comedians. Its larger message is the new geography of speed, cities, labor, and distance it creates.

Television does not only deliver a play. It rearranges the room, the evening, the advertising business, political performance, and the sense that millions of people are watching the same event together.

Then television puts McLuhan on television to explain that television is doing this.

## The man behind the poster

Ten years later, McLuhan performed the cleanest possible version of this trick in *Annie Hall*.

Alvy Singer is trapped in a cinema line behind a man loudly explaining McLuhan. Alvy steps outside the scene's normal realism, pulls the actual McLuhan from behind a poster, and lets the subject correct the expert.

McLuhan's short verdict is the title of this article: “You know nothing of my work.”

The joke lands because ordinary life does not provide footnotes with legs. The person being interpreted does not usually emerge from the wall and settle the argument. Film can make that fantasy happen because film controls the cut.

Now the scene has another layer. McLuhan became a portable reaction image before reaction images existed. A complicated body of work collapses into one perfect rebuttal. The clip can circulate without the movie, the movie can circulate without the books, and the sentence can circulate without McLuhan.

The medium keeps editing the message after the author leaves the room.

## The broadcast schedule

Broadcast television organizes culture as appointments.

The network decides that McLuhan appears Sunday at four. The viewer must arrive. The schedule has authority because scarcity is physical. There are only so many channels and so many hours. A strange program can reach someone who did not request it because it sits between other things in a common stream.

That system has obvious limits. A small group of executives controls access. A missed broadcast may be gone. Local stations can preempt it. The viewer has little power over timing.

It also creates accidental adjacency. A Frank McHugh viewer can become a McLuhan viewer because the network placed both inside the same experimental label.

## The streaming catalog

Streaming changes the primary object from a schedule to a catalog.

The viewer no longer has to arrive at four. The library waits. Search, rows, genre labels, artwork, editorial collections, and recommendations help a person choose. This is genuine abundance. Work that could not survive one national time slot can remain available for a small audience over a long period.

The catalog also creates a new problem: abundance must be ordered. A title buried on row nineteen is technically available and practically invisible. The interface becomes a sequence of invitations. Artwork, placement, labels, and predictions become part of the program.

The service still chooses, but now it chooses how choice appears.

## The social feed

Social media changes the object again. The feed is neither a fixed schedule nor a stable catalog. It is a sequence rebuilt while the audience moves through it.

YouTube publicly identifies clicks, watch time, survey responses, sharing, likes, and dislikes among the signals that inform recommendations. The company also says those signals are weighted differently for different viewers and can be overridden by quality and safety systems. That is more complicated than “engagement wins,” but it confirms the essential structural change.

The audience response is now part of the next programming decision.

The creator sees some of that response through analytics. A thumbnail wins. A hook holds attention. A short version travels farther than the careful one. The creator adjusts. Other creators observe the visible success and adjust too.

The algorithm is not only selecting the television schedule. It is teaching everyone how to audition for it.

## The funnel bucket

I call the resulting creative container a funnel bucket.

A funnel bucket is not a genre. It is a repeatable costume that turns culture into a predicted action. The pet needs one more follower. The artist needs a comment. The expert has a secret guide. The restaurant is empty and you can save it by sharing. Different subjects enter. The same behavioral request comes out.

This does not mean the people are insincere. The artist may need the sale. The restaurant may be empty. The creator may believe the guide will help.

> **The creator may be sincere. The bucket does not care.**

The bucket is an interface relationship. It connects an easily recognized emotional package to a measurable action, then routes that action toward another offer. Once the pattern works, it becomes teachable. Once it becomes teachable, it becomes software. Once it becomes software, anyone can manufacture variations.

## The audience is also the experiment

The phrase “the algorithm” can make the system sound like one evil robot with a master plan. It is usually many models, business rules, product choices, moderation systems, advertising goals, safety constraints, and human decisions interacting.

That complexity does not make the effect imaginary.

A broadcast experiment asks whether television can carry a new dramatic form. A social experiment asks which arrangement keeps this viewer watching, which signal predicts satisfaction, which creator can supply more of the pattern, and which version produces another response.

The test continues after the program begins. The audience is the measurement surface.

The abundance is real. So is the conformity.

## AI slop did not begin with AI

AI makes the cycle faster because it lowers the cost of variation. One successful format can become fifty scripts, fifty voices, fifty thumbnails, and fifty edited clips before lunch.

But AI did not invent the reward structure. Clickbait existed before text generators. Content farms existed before image models. Reality television learned how to manufacture emotional beats at industrial speed. Search pages were filled with mechanically rewritten material long before a chatbot could produce it.

AI did not invent slop. It reduced the cost of manufacturing what the platforms were already rewarding.

That is an interpretation, not a platform confession. The supporting mechanism is visible: systems measure response, creators receive feedback, and cheap production makes repetition easier. The conclusion is argued from those facts.

## The website has machine readers too

Leaving social media does not mean leaving machines.

Google describes search as automated crawling, rendering, indexing, and analysis. A website communicates through titles, descriptions, headings, links, image alt text, canonical URLs, sitemaps, and structured data. Social preview systems inspect Open Graph metadata. Feed readers inspect RSS. Accessibility tools depend on semantic structure. AI crawlers may request the same public page under their own policies.

The difference is not human versus machine. The difference is what kind of bargain the structure makes.

On this page, the research notes are readable by people and marked as a list. The connection chain is visible and encoded in the article data. The illustrations have descriptive alt text instead of hiding the argument inside pixels. The canonical URL identifies the durable home. The social crop is a doorway back to the full piece, not a replacement for it.

The site still wants discovery. It does not need to pretend discovery is the work itself.

## Return to Frank McHugh

The route began with one supporting actor in one television listing.

Frank McHugh leads to *The Last Hurrah* in 1958, where he shares the cast with John Carradine. Carradine leads to *The Howling* in 1981, where Joe Dante puts him in the same colony as Dick Miller. Two degrees. Receipts attached.

The shorter route proves the connection. The detour through NBC and McLuhan reveals why it matters.

McHugh spent decades inside studio systems that taught audiences how to recognize a character actor on sight. NBC briefly placed him inside a program built to test the borders of television. McLuhan entered the same program to explain that media reorganize the people using them. Then the schedule became a catalog, the catalog became a feed, and the feed began changing the work before the work was finished.

Frank McHugh opened the wrong door.

It was the right one.
