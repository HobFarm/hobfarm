*Broadway Babies* ends with $170,000 in gangster money and a Broadway show.

Percé Gessant, an unusually pleasant Canadian bootlegger, wins the cash in a crooked poker game. Before he can marry chorus girl Delight Foster, rival gangsters shoot him. Dying, he gives the winnings to Delight and her stage-manager boyfriend Billy. They use it to finance their own musical.

Blood money becomes production capital. The girl gets her man. Broadway gets a new show. Everybody goes home happy.

First National began filming *Broadway Babies* on February 11, 1929, and released it on June 30. The [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/research/data/us-business-cycle-expansions-and-contractions) places the economic peak in August. The contraction associated with the beginning of the Great Depression followed, and Wall Street crashed at the end of October. The movie reached theaters while the party was technically still going, although somebody underneath the floorboards had already started removing the supports.

The film did not predict the Depression. It preserved the final months when Hollywood could still present an enormous pile of illicit cash as a reasonable solution.

Four years later, the same director and cinematographer would make a musical in which the money disappeared before the first number had finished.

## The Broadway Musketeers

The source story was called "The Broadway Musketeers," and the name explains the movie better than its eventual title.

Delight, Florine, and Navarre are chorus girls, friends, roommates, advisers, witnesses, and emergency repair crews for one another's romantic decisions. Alice White is unmistakably the star. The movie was conceived as her talking-picture debut, and she received the publicity campaign, top billing, songs, romance, gangsters, and ending. Marion Byron and Sally Eilers provide something the central love story cannot manufacture by itself: a working female world.

The three actresses had arrived from different corners of the silent industry. The new microphone placed them in the same room, but it did not send them down the same road.

Alice White became a demonstration of how to build a sound star.

Marion Byron brought the physical mechanics of Buster Keaton and Hal Roach into a musical obsessed with recorded dialogue.

Sally Eilers moved from a backstage jackpot fantasy into films about rent, medical bills, marriage, and ordinary people running out of money.

Together, they make *Broadway Babies* more useful than its plot. The movie becomes a junction box.

## Alice White and the manufactured voice

Alice White entered movies through secretarial and script work before moving in front of the camera. By 1928, *Show Girl* had made her a recognizable flapper personality. First National planned *Broadway Babies* as her talking debut and treated the production as a coordinated product launch.

She was scheduled to sing material from the movie during a national radio broadcast alongside Warner and First National stars. Jantzen arranged to feature her in print advertising and window displays at more than 10,000 retailers. After the film succeeded, trade reports said she received a new Warner Bros.-First National contract, a substantial raise, and a luxury automobile.

The new talking star did not supply all the talking movie's sounds.

Belle Mann dubbed White's singing in the sound version. A silent edition was also prepared for theaters that had not installed sound equipment. One movie therefore existed as two technological products, while its leading lady existed as a composite performance: White's body and personality, Mann's singing voice, a Vitaphone orchestra, radio appearances, studio publicity, and thousands of department-store windows.

Hollywood assembled Alice White.

That does not make her fraudulent. It makes her modern. The machinery around her resembles the system later used for pop stars, television personalities, virtual performers, branded influencers, and any other personality created simultaneously through performance, technology, publicity, and distribution.

White's screen character appears impulsive and relatively innocent, but the industrial operation surrounding her is highly organized. First National knew exactly what it hoped to sell. The uncertain part was whether audiences wanted a talkie star built like a silent-era flapper.

For a while, they did.

## Marion Byron and the female comedy experiment

  <img src="https://cdn.hob.farm/3dm/broadway-babies/marion-byron-buster-keaton.jpg" alt="Marion Byron standing beside Buster Keaton in Steamboat Bill, Jr." width="1343" height="953" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption>Marion Byron opposite Buster Keaton in <em>Steamboat Bill, Jr.</em>, before every performer also had to survive a microphone.</figcaption>
</figure>

Marion Byron arrived carrying a different kind of training.

She made her feature debut opposite Buster Keaton in *Steamboat Bill, Jr.*, a movie built from bodies, architecture, weather, machinery, timing, and the possibility that a wall might fall on somebody. She then worked at Hal Roach, where the studio paired her with Anita Garvin in an attempt to establish a female equivalent to Laurel and Hardy. They made three shorts together, including *A Pair of Tights*.

The pairing ran on visible physical contrast and escalating inconvenience. Garvin and Byron did not need an elaborate mythology. They needed a car, an ice cream cone, cheap dates, bad decisions, and enough time for one small problem to ruin everybody's afternoon.

The experiment ended before it became a durable series, but the attempt is revealing. Hal Roach was testing whether two women could carry a repeatable comedy identity like the male teams already attracting audiences.

Then Byron entered *Broadway Babies*.

The pair became a trio. Slapstick became backstage dialogue. The silent comedian entered a production shot in both sound and silent forms. Even her billing shifted. The [AFI record](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/3050-BROADWAY-BABIES) notes that she appeared onscreen as Miriam Byron, her birth name, rather than the professional name under which she is now remembered.

The larger Warner comedy circuit continued without a proven line of influence. Joan Blondell and Glenda Farrell later became a durable working-girl team in pictures including *Havana Widows*. Byron did not cause that pairing. She belongs to an earlier version of the same production question: how do you package female chemistry so audiences will return for another movie?

Roach's version was physical. Garvin and Byron read each other's bodies.

Warner's Depression version was economic. Blondell and Farrell read the room, the men, the money, and the nearest available exit.

*Broadway Babies* sits between them. Its women still inhabit a Jazz Age fantasy, but they already behave like a small survival unit.

## Sally Eilers and the sixteen-hour talkie

Her experience on *Broadway Babies* shows what the transition to sound required from performers who were supposedly benefiting from it.

A contemporary report preserved by AFI said Eilers arrived at eight in the morning, rehearsed for four hours, filmed silent material for six hours, then filmed talking scenes for another six. The studio needed versions for theaters with sound and theaters without it. The actress supplied the conversion labor.

Sixteen hours is not a workday. It is a hostage situation with choreography.

Two years later, Eilers starred opposite James Dunn in Frank Borzage's *Bad Girl*. The title sounds like another flapper story. The movie is about two ordinary people trying to survive love, marriage, pregnancy, pride, and medical costs. Fox adapted material several studios had considered too difficult or too censorable, then turned it into one of the defining working-class romances of early sound cinema.

The distance between the two films is only two years, but the money behaves differently.

In *Broadway Babies*, $170,000 arrives as a jackpot. The money comes from gambling and bootlegging, but once it reaches the heroine it becomes clean enough to finance a musical.

In *Bad Girl*, money appears as wages, rent, furniture, doctor bills, and the private terror of not being able to provide. The spectacle has collapsed inward. A Broadway production is no longer at stake. A marriage is.

Eilers supplies the article's fastest route from Jazz Age fantasy into Depression realism. She does not merely appear in movies made on opposite sides of an economic event. Her characters live inside the change.

The chorus girl becomes a working wife.

The theatrical jackpot becomes a medical bill.

The show-business fantasy becomes the cost of staying alive.

## Where the Broadway Babies ended up

All three Broadway Musketeers lived long enough to watch the primitive sound equipment surrounding *Broadway Babies* become television, widescreen spectacle, color broadcasting, and modern blockbuster Hollywood.

None lived long enough to see their movie streamed from a giant nonprofit computer library inside everybody's house.

Sally Eilers was the first to die. Her leading-lady period faded during the late 1930s, although she continued making occasional films through *Stage to Tucson*, released in 1951. She died in 1978.

Alice White made her final screen appearance in *Flamingo Road*, released in 1949. She died in 1983.

Marion Byron's parts became progressively smaller during the 1930s, and she withdrew from acting before dying in 1985.

The three young women in *Broadway Babies* remain in 1929, arguing backstage, fixing each other's problems, and waiting for Alice White's gangster boyfriend to provide the money. Outside the film, they kept moving. Their movie outlived all three, then survived long enough to become a damaged digital file inside an article about the economic collapse none of them knew was coming.

The Broadway Babies are gone. Their cheap, battered little talkie is still working.

## The same filmmakers return after the crash

Four years after *Broadway Babies*, director Mervyn LeRoy and cinematographer Sol Polito made *Gold Diggers of 1933*. The same creative partnership returned to backstage musical territory, but the economy had rewritten the genre.

*Gold Diggers of 1933* opens with "We're in the Money."

The women wear coin costumes. Ginger Rogers sings in Pig Latin. The number promises abundance, glamour, and financial resurrection.

Then creditors enter and repossess the costumes and scenery while the performers are still wearing them.

That is the distance between the two films.

In 1929, a gangster's jackpot saves the production.

In 1933, legal creditors dismantle the production during a song about prosperity.

The backstage musical has become an emergency workplace. The women share apartments because they cannot afford separate ones. Producers have talent but no financing. Performers calculate which men possess money, which men control jobs, and which apparent opportunities are scams. The economic anxiety is no longer hiding behind the romance. It is the plot.

Busby Berkeley turns that anxiety into architecture. Bodies become coins, assembly lines, military formations, waterfalls, city blocks, and human machinery. The enormous patterns are beautiful because the people inside them are interchangeable.

*Broadway Babies* catches the studio before that visual system fully forms. Its production numbers remain small and awkward. Its sound technology remains visible. Its performers still seem to be negotiating with the equipment.

That awkwardness gives the film value. It shows the factory while the factory is being installed.

The larger Warner system is mapped in [1933: The Year Warner Bros. Built a World](/departments/hobfarm-presents/3-degrees-of-dick-miller/1933-the-year-warner-bros-built-a-world/). [The Mouse in the Cat Musical](/departments/hobfarm-presents/3-degrees-of-dick-miller/the-mouse-in-the-cat-musical/) follows one tiny performer out of *Footlight Parade* and toward Roger Corman.

## From chorus girls to monsters in three moves

Sally Eilers provides the cleanest exit from *Broadway Babies* into 3 Degrees of Dick Miller.

In 1931, she starred with Bela Lugosi in the Charlie Chan mystery *The Black Camel*. Lugosi later appeared with Boris Karloff in *Son of Frankenstein*. Karloff then appeared with Dick Miller in Roger Corman's *The Terror*, where Miller was credited as Richard Miller and played the servant Stefan.

That is what makes *Broadway Babies* a good 3DM film. It looks isolated because most of its stars no longer occupy the center of popular film history. Follow the credits and the isolation disappears. Marion Byron leads backward to Keaton and Hal Roach. Alice White exposes the early construction of the multimedia sound star. Sally Eilers leads through Depression romance, Lugosi, Karloff, Corman, and Dick Miller.

The little First National musical was already connected to the entire century.

It simply arrived during the last summer when everyone onscreen still believed somebody would find the money.
