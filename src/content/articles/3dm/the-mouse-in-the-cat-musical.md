In 1933, James Cagney's Chester Kent staged a musical about cats. The cats danced on a backyard fence, Ruby Keeler handled the tap work, and a tiny mouse interrupted the feline courtship. Andrew Lloyd Webber's *Cats* would not open in London until 1981.

Nobody stole anything. Hollywood had simply already done it.

The comparison is a visual echo, not an influence claim. Its real value is the mouse. The performer inside that costume was Billy Barty, eight years old when *Footlight Parade* was made. Follow him out of the cat musical and the joke turns into a route through fifty-five years of screen fantasy.

## The route

*Footlight Parade* (1933) → Billy Barty → *The Undead* (1957) → Dick Miller

That is a complete 3DM connection. The later branch keeps going:

Billy Barty → *Legend* (1985) → *Willow* (1988)

The route starts with a studio musical, passes through Roger Corman's bargain-scale fantasy-horror world, and ends inside the practical-fantasy boom of the 1980s. One working actor carries the line.

## Everybody was broke

*Footlight Parade* turns workplace panic into a musical factory. Chester Kent begins as a Broadway producer knocked out of his old business by talking pictures. He adapts by building short live "prologues" that theaters can stage before the feature.

The [AFI Catalog](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/3881-FOOTLIGHT-PARADE) describes Kent and his partners taking on more prologue work than they can comfortably handle while competitors steal ideas and business partners work their own angles. [Turner Classic Movies](https://www.tcm.com/articles/12783/footlight-parade-1933) places the story directly inside the Great Depression and the arrival of sound.

The movie's backstage comedy runs on exhaustion. Ideas become inventory. Performers move between rehearsal rooms and stages while Kent tries to feed an entertainment pipeline that cannot stop. The gigantic numbers grow out of that pressure. Spectacle is the product at the far end of an anxious workplace.

The wider studio system is mapped in [1933: The Year Warner Bros. Built a World](/departments/hobfarm-presents/3-degrees-of-dick-miller/1933-the-year-warner-bros-built-a-world/).

## Chester Kent invents *Cats*

"Sittin' on a Backyard Fence" is a miniature cat musical. Performers wear feline costumes. Courtship becomes choreography. Ruby Keeler takes the center while the ensemble turns an alley fence into a stage.

Then the mouse arrives.

Billy Barty scampers into the number as a disruptive scale change, a child-sized performer puncturing the elegance of the adult chorus. AFI credits him as the "Mouse in 'Sittin' on a Backyard Fence'" and also as the little boy in "Honeymoon Hotel." The same record credits Larry Ceballos with dance direction on those two numbers while Busby Berkeley handled the movie's larger musical design.

To a modern viewer, the sequence looks uncannily like an ancestor of *Cats*: costumed felines, romantic types, ensemble movement, and a whole social order explained through dance. That resemblance is the joke. The documented connection begins when we stop looking at the cats and identify the mouse.

## Follow the mouse

Barty did not stay trapped inside a childhood novelty act. His credits kept moving.

In *Footlight Parade*, he is inside Warner Bros.' early-sound musical machine. Twenty-four years later, he appears in *The Undead*, a Roger Corman production made on an entirely different scale. AFI credits Barty as the Imp and Dick Miller as the leper who sells his soul. They are not connected by genre atmosphere, studio ancestry, or a clever theory. They worked on the same picture.

That shared credit is the hinge.

## The mouse meets Dick Miller

*The Undead* has a hypnotist, a time-travel experiment, medieval France, witches, Satan, an Imp, and Dick Miller selling his soul as a leper. Roger Corman directed and produced it. Mel Welles and Bruno VeSota appear nearby, two more familiar faces in Corman's production network.

The contrast with *Footlight Parade* is almost too clean. Warner Bros. gave Barty a musical alley filled with dancing cats. Corman gave him fog, a medieval costume, and a handmade fantasy world assembled under much tighter limits.

One performer physically connects those production systems. The route exposes something a genre list cannot. Studio spectacle and low-budget fantasy were not sealed historical compartments. Working actors carried experience, habits, and recognizable screen types between them.

That is the 3DM principle. Start with a visible oddity. Identify the worker inside it. Follow the credits until two supposedly separate movie worlds touch.

## The fantasy branch

Barty's career keeps the route open after it reaches Dick Miller.

The [AFI Catalog record for *Legend*](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/57395-LEGEND) credits him as Screwball in Ridley Scott's dark fairy tale. Three years later, he played the High Aldwin in *Willow*. Lucasfilm's own retrospective identifies Barty in the role and describes the much larger company of short actors assembled for the Nelwyn community.

The mouse becomes the Imp. The Imp becomes Screwball. Screwball becomes the High Aldwin.

This is not a line of influence. It is a line of labor. Barty moved through radically different ways of manufacturing fantasy:

- Warner Bros. musical spectacle in the early sound era.
- Corman's compressed fantasy-horror production system.
- Ridley Scott's enormous, heavily designed dark fairy tale.
- George Lucas and Ron Howard's heroic fantasy world.

The costumes, budgets, effects, and audiences changed. The specialized performer remained useful.

## What the connection reveals

Film history usually puts the largest name at the center. The better connections often belong to people working at the edge of the poster.

Barty's career joins the pre-Code musical to the Corman repertory system, then carries that line into creature performance and the practical-effects fantasy boom. He was present when fantasy meant a painted studio alley, when it meant a fog machine and a cave, and when it meant enormous location companies and elaborate physical worlds.

That continuity is the story. The route begins as a joke about dancing cats and ends as a compact history of how Hollywood reused specialized performers across six decades.

Start with the cats. Follow the mouse. Eventually, you reach Dick Miller.
