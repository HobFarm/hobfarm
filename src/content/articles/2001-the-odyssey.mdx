On December 30, 2000, three of us left Maine for New York in a 1994 Nissan Altima. The weather in Maine was manageable. Around Boston it stopped being manageable.

Snow blew across the highway hard enough to erase the easy version of the road. It continued through Connecticut and followed us into New York. I had grown up driving in Maine winters, and the Altima had substantial snow tires, so I knew the basic arrangement: slow down, leave room, do not make any sudden brilliant decisions. Other drivers had a different plan. SUVs kept charging past us as if four-wheel drive had repealed physics. Some of them appeared again later, tilted into snowbanks beside the road.

The trip took much longer than it should have. It was stressful and unpleasant, but nothing cinematic happened to us. The car did not break down. We did not crash. We did not spend the night in a ditch. We kept going.

By the time we reached Manhattan, the highway storm had become a street problem. Snow was deep at the curb. Sanitation trucks and plows were moving through the city. Parking required a search for one improbable car-sized exception in a landscape made of white piles, buried bumpers, and people who had already claimed the possible spaces. We eventually got the Altima parked and hauled ourselves into a Greenwich Village flat.

Central Park recorded twelve inches of snow that day. The Department of Sanitation reported a citywide response involving thousands of workers and a special operation to clear Times Square for New Year’s Eve.[^snow] I did not know any of those numbers while I was driving. I knew the taillights ahead of me, the tires under me, and the fact that New York was still somewhere on the other side of the windshield.

## Twenty-something chances

I brought one disposable camera.

I do not know the exact model or exposure count. It was probably one of the ordinary twenty-something-exposure cameras sold everywhere at the time. That uncertainty is part of the medium now. There was no file information, no cloud account, no camera-roll database, and no screen on the back telling me whether I had covered the lens with my finger.

Each press of the shutter consumed one of a small number of chances. When the roll was finished, it had to be developed. Development cost money. Only then would I learn whether the frame was straight, whether the flash reached, whether anybody blinked, or whether a photograph I remembered taking existed at all.

That sounds like deprivation when compared with a phone, but it also produced a form of editing before the photograph. I had to decide whether something deserved one frame. The decisions were not sophisticated. They were vacation decisions. The city still came through as a short list of what a twenty-year-old from Maine thought looked cool.

The constraint shaped more than the quantity. It shaped the kind of attention I brought to the street. I could not take ten versions of a scene and let software, or future me, choose the useful one. The edit happened while I was standing there. Landmark or friend? Skyline or subway tile? Save a frame in case something better appears around the corner, or spend it on the thing directly in front of me?

That is why the roll can hold the World Trade Center and a field of restaurant signs without treating either as a thesis. Both passed the same simple test in the moment: I wanted to remember seeing this. Twenty-five years of history would assign those frames radically different weights, but the camera did not know that. It charged one exposure for each.

Friends. Snow. Buildings. Signs. A subway station. The Intrepid. The Statue of Liberty. The World Trade Center. A fake Concorde hanging over Times Square. A giant record store. Lunch.

Nothing in the roll announces itself as evidence. That is what made it evidence later.

## New York before the photograph changed

The first pass through the roll is the easiest one. We were in New York for New Year’s weekend and we had fun.

From the Greenwich Village rooftop, the view faced north toward the Empire State Building. The Twin Towers were not in that frame. There was also a photograph of the friends on the roof, people who belonged to this trip and would still be part of my life on September 11. I am leaving that group picture private here. The empty view does the public job without turning friends into historical props.

I bought an NYPD tourist hat. It was not a political act. It was the kind of stupid souvenir that becomes a party joke: haha, I’m a cop. Times Square was a concentration of similar decisions enlarged to building scale. Virgin Megastore was a giant record store. Compaq was a computer company on a billboard. Planet Hollywood was a place people recognized from television. The fake Concorde looked like the future had been parked over a restaurant.

The World Trade Center belonged to the same category. I took pictures of it because I was in New York and it was there. The towers had already returned to ordinary skyline status after the 1993 bombing. I had visited in the summer of 1993 and taken a photograph looking almost straight up one tower. By New Year’s 2001, the buildings were not a warning to me. They were landmarks.

The expected future looked stranger and cooler than the present. Computers were getting interesting. The internet was spreading. Virtual reality, hoverboards, flying cars, video calls, and pocket devices all seemed to be approaching from different directions. Politics existed, but it did not have permanent possession of my attention. Bill Clinton could play saxophone on *Arsenio* and turn up in MTV culture without every part of daily life becoming an argument about him.

The future I expected was mostly a collection of objects. A better computer would arrive. A small camera would arrive. Some kind of wearable screen might arrive. Transportation would get faster, music would become easier to reach, and the internet would make more of the world available. I did not imagine that these improvements would merge into a single device, then join airports, employers, advertisers, platforms, maps, stores, newsrooms, and strangers into one continuous system.

That difference matters. A gadget can be turned off and put in a drawer. Infrastructure becomes the environment. In 2000, I was waiting for better gadgets. I was not imagining how much of the future would arrive as procedures, accounts, subscriptions, checkpoints, notifications, and requests for attention.

Music was the more reliable timeline. In October 1997, I saw Primus in Lewiston with Limp Bizkit and Powerman 5000. That sentence already contains several incompatible futures. At the time it was just a concert bill.[^primus-1997]

Primus will return to this story. They are useful that way.

## The first thing that went wrong that morning

On September 11, 2001, I was twenty years old, living near the Gorham and Standish area, and working full time as a cook at Olive Garden in South Portland.

I had been called in for a meeting. I drove all the way to the restaurant and found out the meeting had been canceled. Then I drove all the way home, furious about the wasted trip. This remained the largest problem in my morning for a few more minutes.

When I walked in, my friends or roommates were watching *Today*. The first plane had already struck the North Tower. I remember watching the second plane hit and then watching the scale of the event keep increasing.

Memory compresses that morning. The official timeline does not. American Airlines Flight 11 struck the North Tower at 8:46 a.m. United Airlines Flight 175 struck the South Tower at 9:03. American Airlines Flight 77 struck the Pentagon at 9:37. The South Tower fell at 9:59. United Airlines Flight 93 crashed near Shanksville at 10:03. The North Tower fell at 10:28.[^commission]

I cannot fit my memory to every minute with confidence. I can place the canceled meeting, the useless drive, the television, the second impact, and the feeling that ordinary time had split open.

Twenty-five years later, the anniversary belongs first to the people killed, their families, the survivors, the responders, and the people who became sick or died from the long aftermath. The National September 11 Memorial names 2,977 people killed in the 2001 attacks and six killed in the 1993 World Trade Center bombing. In 2026, family members will again read the names. Six moments of silence mark the impacts, collapses, the Pentagon attack, and Flight 93. A new seventh silence at the end of the reading honors those lost to 9/11-related health effects.[^memorial]

The photographs are personal. The loss is not mine to turn into scenery.

## Same photograph, different meaning

After September 11, I looked at the New York roll again.

The images had not changed. The person looking at them had. The World Trade Center was no longer one stop in a set containing a subway sign, a museum ship, restaurants, and a giant advertisement. The rooftop picture connected to the friends with whom I watched the aftermath and entered politics. The 1993 photograph became the first point in a personal timeline I had not known I was making.

The NYPD tourist hat changed too. Before the attacks, it was a goofy souvenir. Afterward, wearing it could be read as support for New York, police, firefighters, and first responders. I did not buy a different hat. I did not add a flag to it. I did not make a speech.

I didn’t change the hat. September 11 changed what it said.

The same thing happened to the photographs, especially the skyline image now used at the top of this article.

An archive does not preserve one stable meaning. It preserves material that later meanings can gather around. The skyline frame began as proof that I had gone somewhere. It became a record of buildings that no longer existed. Now it also shows snow, shoreline, distance, and the limitations of the camera that made it. The older the image gets, the more visible its ordinary details become.

That does not make every old photograph prophetic. It makes survival important. Most objects from an ordinary weekend never enter a public history. A hat and a few developed prints happened to last long enough for the surrounding world to keep revising them.

I took this picture because I was in New York, not because I thought I was documenting anything.

## They were in Maine

The event also changed the meaning of a landscape much closer to home.

The Maine Mall area in South Portland is a chain-commercial environment that could be rebuilt almost anywhere in the United States from the same parts: hotels, restaurants, parking lots, drive-up ATMs, gas stations, big-box stores, airport roads, and people arriving for shifts. I worked in that broader geography. It was not symbolic. It was where I went to work.

On the night of September 10, Mohamed Atta and Abdul Aziz al-Omari were there.

The FBI chronology says they checked into the Comfort Inn at 90 Maine Mall Road at about 5:43 p.m. It places them or men matching their description at a Pizza Hut, two ATMs, Jetport Gas, and a Walmart in Scarborough. Early the next morning they checked out, drove a blue 2001 Nissan Altima rental to the Portland Jetport, passed security, and boarded a 6:00 a.m. flight to Boston.[^fbi]

<MaineMallTimeline />

The geography is eerie because it is so ordinary. Nothing in the listed stops looks like a stage for world history. It looks like the stuff surrounding a mall. People were eating dinner, taking out cash, buying gas, stocking shelves, cleaning hotel rooms, cooking, and getting ready for another day.

The official chronology can establish times, places, and movements. It cannot turn everybody sharing the landscape into a participant. Thousands of people passed through those roads, stores, restaurants, hotels, and parking lots without seeing anything that would have distinguished one customer from another. The historical record later draws a line across the map. The people who were already there experienced separate, ordinary nights.

I did not see either man. I am not claiming a near encounter. My connection is that I worked inside the same broad commercial landscape, and the public record later attached those ordinary roads to the attacks.

There is one more object coincidence. Their rental was a Nissan Altima. The car I drove to New York was a Nissan Altima. This means nothing causal. It is simply the kind of detail a brain catches once two timelines have been forced together.

## What if they had been late?

The 9/11 Commission found no convincing explanation for why Atta and al-Omari drove from Boston to Portland only to fly back to Boston the next morning. Portland was the nearest airport with a flight that could reach Logan in time for their connection. They departed at 6:00 a.m., arrived at 6:45, and American 11 was scheduled to leave at 7:45.[^portland-unknown]

That schedule creates an obvious counterfactual. What if the car had failed? What if they had overslept? What if traffic, weather, parking, a long line, or one ordinary argument at a counter had made them miss the commuter flight?

The responsible answer stops quickly. A delay could have prevented those two men from making that specific connection. I do not know whether American 11 would still have been hijacked without one or both of them. I do not know whether the other attacks would have proceeded. I do not know whether the plot would have been delayed, exposed, altered, or replaced.

Pizza Hut did not almost save the country. A Nissan did not decide history.

The point is smaller and more unsettling. History becomes a straight line after it happens. Before it happens, it is still made of ordinary timing, traffic, hotels, meals, parking, alarms, and connections.

The minute-by-minute record makes the temptation stronger because it gives contingency a clock face. Forty-five minutes at Logan can look, from twenty-five years away, like a narrow door history had to pass through. But each question creates more questions. Removing one connection does not reveal the alternate timeline behind it. It only restores uncertainty to a sequence that now feels inevitable.

That is the useful limit of the exercise. The attacks were planned by people making choices. The systems and schedules they used supplied points of friction, not destiny. Noticing those points can correct the illusion that history could only have happened one way. It cannot tell us which other way would have followed.

## Life underneath the news

After September 11, politics and news took up more of my attention. Life did not politely wait for me to understand them.

Nine days after the attacks, Tool played the Cumberland County Civic Center in Portland with Fantômas. I went. The date is documented, but I am not going to retrofit a grand speech or special meaning onto the performance without a recording that supports it.[^tool] What I remember is simpler: the concert happened.

That matters because memorial history can flatten ordinary life into a before and an after. People kept working, cooking, driving, dating, arguing, going to shows, and trying to understand the next week. Music did not stop. Neither did bills.

I had already been to Primus in 1997. I went to Phish’s IT Festival at the former Loring Air Force Base in 2003. I kept seeing shows through the years when Afghanistan, terror alerts, Iraq, and anti-Bush culture were taking up more room in my head. The continuity was not denial. It was life continuing beneath a news layer that rarely went quiet.

I tried politics because I cared. One of the friends from the New York rooftop became a human connection into the League of Pissed Off Voters. I paid attention to the Greens. I studied, participated, made media, and tried to do something useful. I did not quit politics because I had never tried it.

By the 2004 election, I was beginning to question what all that attention was producing in me. The full political and economic branch belongs in [*Fear and Loathing After the American Dream*](/articles/fear-and-loathing-after-the-american-dream/). The important fact here is that the questioning happened while I was also going deeper into the machinery that supplied the news.

## The rundown

I worked in television news production, including camera work for repeated newscasts. I was not a reporter. I was one of the people helping make the program happen again and again.

That distinction changes how the news enters your life. A viewer can turn off the television. A person working a newscast hears the story in rehearsal, in the control room, through cues, through the live show, and again in later shows. The names and graphics repeat until they become part of the room.

A rundown gives the day a structure. Stories occupy slots. Video, copy, graphics, live elements, timing, and commercial breaks have to become a program that starts at a specific second and ends when the next program begins. Real events enter that structure at different sizes, then return in the next show with revised language, a new clip, or a fresh angle. The grid is necessary. It is also hungry.

Working camera meant I experienced some stories as physical repetition. Set the shot. Listen for the cue. Follow the person speaking. Hold steady while the subject returns. The craft demanded attention even when the subject had already passed through my ears several times that day. I did not have to decide that a story was personally important for my body to learn its rhythm.

In October 2004, Ashlee Simpson appeared on *Saturday Night Live*. During her second performance, the vocal track from the first song started again. She did a strange little dance and walked offstage while the band kept playing. A coworker captured the live East Coast feed from production control.[^ashlee]

It had considerably less geopolitical significance, but it was much funnier.

Then there was Terri Schiavo. A family and medical dispute in Florida became national programming and federal legislation. `SCHIAVO` kept appearing on a Maine affiliate’s rundown. I heard the name every newscast, not because each show contained a new discovery, but because the national attention machine had selected the subject and could keep producing segments from it.

Pope John Paul II died on April 2, 2005. NBC breaking coverage took priority over local programming. That was not an editorial mystery. It was an example of the network hierarchy made visible. The national feed could enter the local schedule and own the hour.

BRAC was different. On May 13, 2005, the Department of Defense sent the Base Realignment and Closure Commission 222 recommendations involving 837 actions.[^brac] That federal process became a direct local problem because Brunswick Naval Air Station meant jobs, families, schools, contractors, housing, and identity to the WCSH audience. Local news had a real job to do: translate a remote national process into consequences for people who lived nearby.

Then Hurricane Katrina made Gulf Coast landfall on August 29. It was a genuine catastrophe that deserved sustained reporting. Television also required the story to keep filling time, developing conflict, and returning with new live positions. A few days later, during NBC’s live benefit, Kanye West stood beside Mike Myers and went off script. A coworker caught that East Coast feed too.

Ashlee Simpson, Terri Schiavo, a pope, BRAC, Katrina, and Kanye West had almost nothing in common.

The production system did.

Some of those stories mattered enormously. The lesson was not that news was fake. The lesson was that the machine did not have an off switch. There was always another rundown.

The difference between those claims became important to me. Television could show people something urgent, document consequences, and explain why a federal decision in Washington mattered in Brunswick. It could also take a subject that had already been established and keep it circulating because the format needed another segment. Useful reporting and compulsory repetition could occupy the same show.

From inside production, attention looked less like an abstract moral virtue and more like inventory. A program had a fixed amount of time, but the larger news day had no natural end. When one story fell away, another moved up. When a viewer finished watching, another show began. I had entered news partly because events mattered. I left with a clearer sense that the system's appetite and a subject's importance were not the same thing.

## One federal acronym, three Maine futures

BRAC was also a junction between places I had already known in completely different ways.

Loring Air Force Base had closed in 1994. By the time I went there for Phish’s IT Festival in 2003, the former base had become a vast concert landscape. Griffiss Air Force Base in New York had been realigned and then became the site of Woodstock ’99. In 2005, Brunswick was the active local story, still full of people who did not know exactly what the next version of the place would be.

The point is not that base closure is secretly good because festivals happen. Workers and communities absorb real disruption. It is that institutional land does not remain frozen after the institution changes. Today the former Brunswick station includes Brunswick Landing and Brunswick Executive Airport. The place kept becoming something else.

I had encountered the same process three ways: as a festival site, as a local-news threat that needed explaining, and as a long physical transformation visible years later.

## Leaving the rundown

By 2006, I had spent years giving politics and television news more attention than they gave back in useful understanding. The result was stress, not clarity. I moved to Los Angeles and started doing different creative and media work.

The move involved my first airplane trip since my family flew to Disney World in 1985. On that earlier flight, my father smoked cigarettes on the plane. That detail now feels more futuristic than half the gadgets predicted in old magazines.

The airport in 2006 belonged to the post-September 11 system. Threat-level announcements were part of the atmosphere. Screening had become federal. Shoes, belts, bags, identification, and later that year the new liquid restrictions turned the checkpoint into a sequence of learned gestures. The inconvenience was not the whole story. So was the normalization. A process that would have looked extraordinary in 2000 had become what you did before reaching the gate.

The remarkable part of normalization is how quickly an instruction becomes muscle memory. Read the sign, prepare the identification, separate the required objects, wait for the next direction, collect everything on the other side. The exact procedure changes, but the passenger learns to approach the checkpoint as a problem to solve correctly. By the time I flew west, the checkpoint was not presented as a temporary interruption of ordinary travel. It was ordinary travel.

I moved to Los Angeles and started working on completely different television. One project was apparently called *Hottest Mom in America*.

That’s another story.

Barack Obama later arrived as a national figure who seemed different from Bush. Hope and change sounded plausible to me. My eventual disappointment does not need a presidential scorecard here. It reinforced the same practical conclusion I had begun reaching in the newsroom: I could care about policy without allowing political performance unlimited access to my mind.

## Whatever happened to Threat Level Orange?

For years, the color-coded Homeland Security Advisory System made danger look like a strip of paint. Airports and news programs announced that the country was at an elevated or high risk. “Threat Level Orange” became both an official condition and a cultural phrase.

Then it disappeared.

In 2011, the National Terrorism Advisory System replaced the color scale with alerts intended to contain more specific information.[^ntas] The phrase vanished faster than the machinery around it. TSA remained. The checkpoint remained. Liquid rules remained. The identity layer kept growing, including REAL ID enforcement beginning in 2025.[^tsa]

At the same time, travel became easier in ways that would have looked impossible from the 2001 road trip. A phone can compare flights, buy a ticket, hold the boarding pass, find the terminal, show the traffic on the way there, book the hotel, and tell somebody when the plane lands.

The future did not choose between convenience and friction. It built both and put them in the same trip.

## They keep trying to enter the room

Politics did not need my permission to keep arriving. Trump, anti-Trump commentary, birther material, reactions to the reactions, and arguments about what somebody else had said would enter work and ordinary conversation through other people.

The chain became familiar:

> Something happens. Somebody interprets it. Somebody clips the interpretation. A platform distributes the reaction. Somebody repeats what “they say.”

“They say” is not a source.

Not every claim deserves the time required to disprove it.

That is not the same as saying nothing matters. It means attention has a cost, and somebody else should not be able to spend mine merely by producing a sufficiently irritating clip. The larger argument belongs in [*Brought to You by They, Inc.*](/articles/brought-to-you-by-they-inc/). My useful rule is simpler: when the question matters, find the source. When it does not, it may not deserve a research project at all.

Politics understands attention as a resource. Advertising understands it. Social platforms understand it. Las Vegas understands it so well that the buildings can direct a body from spectacle to spectacle without the visitor feeling like he has followed instructions. I took longer to understand that my attention was a resource too.

Treating it that way is not a declaration of neutrality. It is a refusal to grant automatic priority. A policy that changes people's lives can earn attention. Evidence can earn attention. A politician saying something outrageous in order to occupy the day does not automatically earn a response, a share, an argument, and a permanent room in my head.

## Inspector Gadget was a phone

One large part of the future turned out to be excellent.

I grew up watching *Inspector Gadget* and wondering where the camera was when characters seemed to talk toward a screen, a wrist device, or empty air. The show treated cameras, maps, radios, computers, sensors, and communications as separate miracles distributed across an impossible body.

Now most of that stack sits in a pocket.

A phone can make a video call, take high-resolution photographs and video, show live maps and traffic, book a flight and hotel, hold a boarding pass, manage appointments, edit media, publish an article, open a spreadsheet, connect to an AI system, run marketing work, and coordinate with a watch or a pair of glasses I do not particularly want.

I do not use every capability. Most days I want calls, maps, traffic, reference access, and a camera when something earns a picture. The absurdity is that the rest remains available.

I could sit at an IV bar getting a vitamin B drip, eating tropical fruit, book a flight and hotel, edit a video, publish an article, and leave later that day without opening a laptop. I am not going to do that. The point is that I could.

The gadget future arrived. It just came with terms of service.

It also reversed the disposable camera's sequence. With film, selection came before capture. With a phone, capture is nearly free and selection can be postponed forever. I can make several versions, keep them all, and tell myself I will organize them later. The cost moved from the shutter to the archive.

That shift made more moments recordable, which is genuinely useful. It also created thousands of small decisions waiting in storage. Which image is the record? Which duplicate can go? What date, person, place, or project does it belong to? A camera roll can remember more than a disposable camera and still make a particular memory harder to find.

## The same camera turns everybody into a camera crew

Casinos normalized surveillance long before smartphones. In Las Vegas I assume cameras are present and behave accordingly. I am not worried that a secret agency has selected me for study. I am worried about the much more practical modern possibility of becoming somebody else’s content.

A public breakdown on the Strip no longer ends as a story witnesses tell. Bystanders record it. Somebody uploads it. Somebody adds a caption. Other accounts repost it. Reaction channels react to it. A person’s worst ten minutes become inventory for an engagement factory.

My countermeasure is urban camouflage: look and behave like an average nobody who gives nobody a reason to point a phone at him. The technique works so well that I cannot get anybody to read my articles.

I am not hiding from the government. I am trying not to give some asshole a thumbnail.

The recording is only the first conversion. A person becomes a clip, the clip becomes a claim, and the claim becomes a prompt for strangers who do not know what happened before or after the frame. The camera may be accurate about the visible ten seconds and useless about the event. Distribution supplies confidence faster than context.

This is different from saying public photography is wrong. Cities are visible. News happens in public. People document abuse, beauty, accidents, art, weather, architecture, and each other. My own work depends on being able to use a camera outside. The question is not whether the device is good. The question is what the person holding it chooses to make from somebody else's life.

## What I do instead

I redirect attention toward work that can leave something useful behind.

I write articles. I build and own HobFarm. I work on search and structured discovery so a curious person can find something without waiting for a social platform to select it. I look for primary sources or get as close to them as the subject allows. I try to notice where interpretation has entered the chain. [*Hit the Source Directly*](/articles/hit-the-source-directly/) explains that method in more detail.

I am also learning AI systems as systems, not treating AI as one magic intelligence. Some tools are impressive. Some are frustrating. Some fit one stage of a job and damage another. The useful work is finding where they belong beside photography, editing, video, web development, research, and all the fragments I already accumulated.

HobFarm has become an independent publication, an attention filter, a memory system, and a mesh connecting old material to new work. The unfinished graffiti documentary in [*The Future Was Already There*](/articles/the-future-was-already-there/) became useful twenty years later because the pieces survived. This New York roll is useful for the same reason.

The goal is not to escape the world. It is to decide which parts of the world earn a closer look.

Curiosity usually begins with a better question than political sorting. Not “Which side am I supposed to join?” but “What is that?” What is the unfinished building beside the World Trade Center? Why was a Concorde model hanging above Times Square while the real planes were grounded? How did a sacred waterfall also become the site of an underground generating plant? Why did a former air base become a concert field?

Those questions do not remove conflict, power, money, or politics from the subject. They prevent the conclusion from arriving before the investigation. Sometimes the answer is still political. Sometimes it is engineering, geology, business history, music, or a friend's memory. The filter is not designed to keep reality out. It is designed to let reality arrive before the performance around it.

The result is slower than a feed and more durable than a reaction. A photograph gets a caption. A recollection is labeled as a recollection. A public claim gets a source. An uncertainty stays open instead of being filled with the most satisfying guess. The fragments become work without pretending they were always waiting to become this exact story.

## A job connected to something worth noticing

I always liked concerts and thought venue work would be interesting. Brooklyn Bowl Las Vegas hired me as a video person. The roles changed and expanded. I have worked video, stage management, lighting, and other positions.

I am not comparing myself with touring lighting designers for Goose or the Disco Biscuits. I do not want to tour. Large corporate-event work is not my secret destination. The opportunity has still been unusual, privileged, educational, and worth preserving.

After a few shows a week for months, the shows blur into work. Coworkers retain different fragments: the odd request, the funny mistake, the recurring story, the night something finally worked. My basic goal is to have fun, do a good job, and treat people well.

## What I choose to look at now

The current photographs are not better because the camera is better. They are different because I know I am building a record.

In Las Vegas, I photograph the work city underneath the attraction city. I notice a security camera hanging inside Caesars Palace. I notice street art embedded in the pavement. I return to the Hard Rock construction site and photograph the guitar-shaped hotel from roughly the same place, month after month. A full time-lapse can become its own project later.

The repetition is deliberate in a way the New York roll could not be. I know the building will change, so I keep a position and return. Cranes move. Floors close in. The recognizable outline arrives a piece at a time. Each image is less dramatic than the idea of a completed time-lapse, but the completed record can only be made from those ordinary returns.

That practice has changed how I value a frame. A useful photograph does not need to catch a once-in-history event. It can establish a condition: what was there, what was not there yet, and where I stood when I looked. The unfinished hotel beside the Twin Towers does that accidentally. The Las Vegas construction photographs do it on purpose.

One frame is enough here because the 2001 roll already contains the comparison.

## The future advertised itself

On the third pass through the 2001 roll, the most revealing material is often in the background.

One Times Square frame contains a Compaq billboard reading `TIMES SQUARE WILL STOP BEFORE WE DO` and `24x7x.COMPAQ`. Below it are Virgin, Loews, Planet Hollywood, LG, and advertisements packed into the same field.

The internet did become 24/7. Compaq did not remain Compaq. HP and Compaq announced their merger days before September 11.[^compaq]

Virgin Megastore looked like a permanent physical container for music. The Times Square store had opened in 1996 and closed in 2009.[^virgin] The inventory did not vanish. It escaped the building and became files, streams, subscriptions, search results, and a library reachable from the phone in my pocket.

Planet Hollywood, Loews, LG, and the surrounding signs show Times Square doing what Times Square does: turning the current commercial arrangement into a claim about permanence.

## Return to the camera

In 2000 I had a disposable camera and had to decide which twenty-something things were worth remembering. In 2026 I can photograph almost anything I want. The harder problem now is deciding what deserves my attention.

The abundance does not automatically improve memory. Several shows a week become months of similar calls. A room that once felt extraordinary becomes the workplace. People remember different fragments. The phone fills with thousands of files that can disappear into their own volume.

That is why the articles matter. A photograph can be attached to a date, a source, a place, and a thought. It can connect to other work without pretending every connection was visible at the time. The archive becomes intentional instead of merely large.

Intentional does not mean complete. The rooftop group picture stays private. The tourist hat has no current publication photograph. The unfinished lower Manhattan building remains unnamed until I can verify it. Those are not failures that need to be patched with confident prose. They are boundaries around what this version of the record can support.

In 2000 I took pictures without knowing what I was saving. Now I know better.

The trip contained a friend, travel, a forest, a sacred waterfall, hydroelectric infrastructure, an outdoor crowd, and a modern concert production.
