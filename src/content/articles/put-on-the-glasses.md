I have loved *They Live* since it came out.

Around 2013, the movie started moving again. Google Glass was in public. Phone cameras could recognize printed images. Startups were demonstrating spatial screens and talking about putting digital objects into the room. A pair of cheap black sunglasses from 1988 suddenly looked less like a science-fiction prop and more like a product category waiting for the wrong venture capitalist.

Atheer was the company that caught me. Google Glass got the headlines, but Atheer showed a larger binocular view, Android applications floating in space, and hands reaching into the interface. I encountered it during the crowdfunding period and thought: there it is. That is the thing.

I also tweeted Roddy Piper, “They Live is like fine wine, it gets better with age.” He retweeted it.

I do not have the screenshot or the exact date. This is a personal recollection, not a recovered artifact. The retweet was small, but it connected me to somebody inside a movie that had stayed in my head for decades. Piper died in 2015. The memory has more weight now because it cannot happen again.

[*Too Big for the Box*](/articles/too-big-for-the-box/) asked where the personal computer went as computing spread into accounts, services, networks, remote infrastructure, and the environment. This article asks who the resulting interface was built for. Who owns the layer between the person and the world?

*They Live* already had the answer in a cardboard box full of sunglasses.

## 1. Los Angeles under the billboard

John Carpenter directed *They Live* and wrote the screenplay under the name Frank Armitage, adapting Ray Nelson’s short story “Eight O’Clock in the Morning.” Roddy Piper plays Nada, Keith David plays Frank, Meg Foster plays Holly, Peter Jason plays Gilbert, and George “Buck” Flower plays the drifter who appears friendly until the glasses revise him.

The movie begins with unemployment, construction work, television, and a man carrying everything he owns. Nada walks into Los Angeles under Carpenter and Alan Howarth’s slow blues score. The music is spare enough to leave room for boots, trains, traffic, and the city. It gives him the rhythm of a Western drifter entering a place that has replaced horses with labor agencies and billboards.

Nada finds work by being physically useful. Frank helps him because Frank understands what a day’s labor means. Justiceville offers food, showers, a church basement, television, arguments, families, and cardboard shelters. The place is vulnerable because every object in it is vulnerable. Police and bulldozers can destroy a home in minutes when the home is made from plywood and tarps.

Carpenter described the film in 1988 as an update in which “the Reagan Revolution was run by aliens.” The line is funny because the film is funny. It is also precise. The aliens do not arrive as a military occupation that interrupts normal American life. They arrive through the economy, television, police, advertising, and the promise that collaboration can move a person into the good neighborhood.

The satire has material weight. Nada does not discover the system at a symposium. He follows Gilbert from a soup line into a church, finds boxes and broadcasting equipment, then wanders into commerce wearing a tool nobody in power intended him to have. The city changes because his access changes.

Carpenter and cinematographer Gary B. Kibbe shot dingy locations in anamorphic Panavision, used low light, favored prime lenses and physical movement, and built the glasses view around black and white. The choice makes the reveal feel cheaper and harsher than a fantasy effect. Color is the expensive part. The truth looks like a photocopy.

Carpenter called the film science fiction, action, comedy, and political satire. Piper belongs inside that mixture. Carpenter said Piper looked as if he had lived a life, with a rough face that remained kind. A professional wrestler could carry the movie’s jokes, absorb punishment, sell pain, and look credible sleeping outdoors or walking onto a construction site. Nada’s body is not decoration. His body is his résumé, his shelter, his weapon, and eventually the evidence that seeing costs something.

## 2. Augmented reality in reverse

The sunglasses in *They Live* are augmented reality in reverse.

Commercial augmented reality adds. It places directions, products, assistance, messages, annotations, entertainment, cameras, recording, and services into the visible world. Carpenter’s glasses subtract. They remove the polished layer until the command beneath the image becomes legible.

When Nada first wears them, color drains away. Package design becomes instruction. Billboards become commands. Money reveals an order to recognize it as a god. Television becomes an operating surface. Human faces divide into people and the class that owns the transmitter.

The effect accumulates. One sign could be a joke. A whole block establishes a system.

A user-controlled diagnostic overlay today could expose a different set of information: who owns the service, who paid for the placement, what data leaves the device, whether a price is personalized, which provider supplied the answer, what contract governs the feature, and which buyer controls the roadmap.

That would still be augmented reality. It would simply serve the wearer before the platform.

## 3. The open-source glasses

“Open-source glasses” is my metaphor. The film does not describe a license, publish code, or stage a software-development meeting.

It does show people building, testing, copying, storing, and distributing a counter-tool outside the official system. The resistance has a workshop. It has chalkboards, instruments, glassware, racks, boxes, workbenches, and people who understand parts of the process. The glasses are made objects. Somebody has to solve the problem, produce more than one pair, and get them into other hands.

Their open-source quality comes from inspectability, reproducibility, shared knowledge, and user control. The wearer controls the view. The device exposes the hidden layer. The tool can be copied and circulated. Its output helps people act on what they learn. It does not ask the system being inspected for permission.

That is the part I want from open source and local-first software. A tool becomes more useful when I can inspect the structure around it, keep my own data, replace a provider, and preserve the work after a company changes direction.

I use general-purpose models inside structures I designed for specific jobs. Grimoire organizes knowledge and project memory. StyleFusion turns visual judgment into explicit parts that can be inspected and revised. Wonder Machine separates authored material, deterministic state, and optional language. [*The Card Catalog Started Talking Back*](/articles/the-card-catalog-started-talking-back/) explains the larger direction.

Those systems do not need to defeat every corporation. They need to remain legible enough that I know which machine is allowed to make a fact true.

## 4. The future they showed me

The excitement around augmented reality in 2013 was legitimate.

Google Glass had put a camera, small display, voice interface, and developer platform onto a face. Atheer went wider. At its May 2013 demonstration, the prototype used two displays, sensors, Android compatibility, and hand tracking to create a three-dimensional workspace. The Engadget reporter could reach toward objects in space, close programs with a hand gesture, and spin a cube. The hardware was bulky, and the reporter did not get to use a complete operating system, but the interface was present enough to touch.

That mattered. Atheer was showing spatial screens before “spatial computing” became polished launch-video language. It promised that ordinary Android applications could live inside a larger visual field. A person could manipulate three-dimensional objects at physical scale. A screen no longer had to be a rectangle held below the face.

At the same time, phone-based image recognition was making printed media behave like a marker. While I was working with an art group in Los Angeles, I heard about a film-poster campaign that used an app and an image target to play a trailer inside the poster and animate parts of the design.

I still think that is a genuinely good idea.

I could not identify the exact campaign from the memory alone. The mechanism is well documented. In 2013, AMC added an Aurasma-powered feature to its iPhone app that recognized 25 enabled movie posters. A person could point the phone at a poster, watch a trailer, buy a ticket, or save an upcoming film. That March, *The Croods* used Aurasma-enabled outdoor posters to trigger animated characters.

The problem was the route into the experience. A poster might require the AMC app, the Aurasma app, a film app, Blippar, Zappar, or another scanner. The physical image did not carry a durable public layer. It pointed into one company’s recognition service and campaign hosting. BCG later described first-party AR marketing as fragmented among bespoke applications with limited scale. Aurasma became HP Reveal and shut down in 2020.

The idea survived through QR codes, WebAR, social lenses, phone-native recognition, and theater apps. The shared habit never became ordinary enough to make every poster a door.

## 5. The future found a better customer

Atheer launched its Indiegogo campaign on December 19, 2013.

The consumer model was called Atheer One. Early backers could reserve it for $350; later offers were $500. The developer kit started at $850. The One was supposed to arrive in December 2014, while developer hardware was promised earlier. The campaign raised about $214,000 against a $100,000 goal.

The pitch combined several futures at once: a large binocular display, Android applications, three-dimensional objects, stereo cameras, a phone connection, remote desktop, and direct hand interaction. The One was supposed to weigh about 70 grams. The product images looked far closer to wearable glasses than the hand-built prototype demonstrated in May.

Six months later, Atheer canceled the consumer One.

The June 2014 report is unusually useful because the company’s decision is understandable. Atheer expected the consumer hardware to slip to late 2015, about a year beyond the promised date. Interest was arriving from automotive, medical, oil, CAD, and other enterprise customers. Consumer expectations, hardware polish, awareness, support, comfort, and social adoption formed a larger problem than the startup could carry.

Orders were canceled and refunded. This was a strategic turn, not evidence of a secret plot or a fraud. The economics explain enough.

A consumer product has to work across many settings. It must be affordable, comfortable, attractive, durable, socially acceptable, understandable, supportable, and useful often enough to earn a place on somebody’s face. An enterprise buyer can pay more for a narrower system that improves one measurable workflow. A hospital can value sterile hands. A warehouse can value faster picking. An oil rig can value remote expertise and visible instructions during a repair.

Atheer’s archived site eventually spoke directly to field service, healthcare, warehousing, and oil and gas. Its current site has completed the journey. The company now calls itself an enterprise work-execution platform for frontline operations. It integrates with ERP, MES, and maintenance systems, guides tasks, captures evidence, and produces records.

The future did not disappear. It found a customer with a procurement department.

The pattern I see is narrower than a universal law and more useful than a conspiracy:

> The future is demonstrated to individuals, financed by enthusiasts, refined through their attention, then sold to institutions.

Atheer is a clean example because the turn happened quickly and the company survived. The enthusiasts were not foolish. They correctly saw a useful interface. The startup correctly saw a customer who could pay for a specific outcome. The person who wanted a spatial computer lost the roadmap because the person was never the strongest buyer.

## 6. Who owns the overlay?

Institutions follow incentives. The presentation is an interface over the system.

Launch videos, mission statements, political messaging, friendly copy, branding, and lifestyle photography describe the decorative layer. Contracts, buyers, procurement, ownership, data, support tiers, enforcement, and maintenance describe the operational layer.

Coordinated-looking behavior can emerge from shared incentives without a secret room of conspirators. The glasses help separate the public story from the purchasing structure beneath it, not prove that every institution is run by a secret cabal.

[*Brought to You by They, Inc.*](/articles/brought-to-you-by-they-inc/) followed the way complex events become compressed public packages. The same inspection works on technology:

- Who pays?
- Who receives support and guarantees?
- Who can negotiate the terms?
- Who owns the data?
- Which version receives long-term maintenance?
- Which user can be abandoned without consequence?
- What does the company advertise to the public while building for a different buyer?

Those questions apply to corporations, government systems, media platforms, professional software, artificial intelligence, and the cheerful service that promises to simplify your life. They produce documents, names, and decisions. They also leave room for ordinary tradeoffs, mistakes, and good-faith work.

The alien face is fun. The contract is better evidence.

## 7. Enterprise of one

I do not want camera glasses recording my walk. I do not want a voice assistant that makes me talk to myself in public. I do not want gesture controls that make me look like I am fighting invisible bees.

Current smart glasses split into different categories, and the distinction matters. Display-less Ray-Ban Meta glasses emphasize cameras, calls, audio, sharing, and a voice-controlled assistant. Meta Ray-Ban Display adds a small full-color view at the side of one lens, plus a wristband that reads subtle muscle signals for finger control. Orion is a limited product prototype with a wider holographic display and spatial content placed into the environment. It is not a consumer product.

Those are different interfaces with different social costs and different jobs. Calling all of them “AR glasses” hides the choice that matters most: what kind of layer is being built, and for whom?

I want the deeper capabilities behind the category:

- private overlays for a physical workspace;
- technical information during a production or repair task;
- full-scale three-dimensional previews;
- archive and location-history layers;
- custom interfaces connected to data I control;
- local operation where possible;
- continued usefulness without a mandatory social platform;
- narrow systems that solve one real problem well.

I do not need a universal platform attached to my face. I need enterprise software for an enterprise of one.

An individual should be able to assemble some of the capabilities institutions buy: structured records, task-specific models, private search, reproducible workflows, versioned sources, local data, replaceable providers, and interfaces shaped around the work. Open-source software can supply parts. Local tools can preserve control. Schemas can keep one system from quietly taking over another system’s job.

This is not a fantasy of complete independence. I still use networks, vendors, models, platforms, and machines made by other people. The practical goal is leverage without surrendering the map.

The glasses should help me see the work. They should not convert the work into inventory for somebody else’s lifestyle platform.

## 8. The actors carry the signal

The movie survives because the people carry it.

Roddy Piper plays Nada directly. He does not wink at the material or polish the character into a conventional action hero. His wrestling career gave him the physical vocabulary for a man who gets hired with his body and solves danger through impact. It also gave the film a performer who understood that a fight can be story, joke, endurance test, and audience contract at the same time.

Keith David gives Frank authority, warmth, exhaustion, and anger. Frank has a job to protect and a family to think about. He has already learned how expensive trouble can be. His refusal to wear the glasses is not stupidity. It is self-defense against a truth that would demand a new life.

The alley fight was rehearsed for weeks, shot over four days, and scripted move by move. Carpenter compared its broad undercurrents to *The Quiet Man*. The scene keeps going until the joke becomes pain, then keeps going until pain becomes intimacy. Nada cannot explain the system well enough to make Frank look. Frank cannot accept the invitation without risking everything his practical intelligence has kept intact.

Meg Foster’s Holly belongs to another register. Her eyes already make ordinary looking feel like surveillance. Carpenter uses her stillness against Piper and David’s battered physicality. Peter Jason’s Gilbert carries the resistance with the recognizable competence of a working character actor. George “Buck” Flower turns a familiar scruffy warmth into a class betrayal with one look through the glasses.

Flower was part of Carpenter’s recurring company, appearing across *The Fog*, *Escape from New York*, *Starman*, *They Live*, *Body Bags*, and *Village of the Damned*. Peter Jason also belonged to the larger network of actors who could walk into a genre film and make the room feel occupied before the plot explained them.

Jason played Detective Drooler in Cheech and Chong’s *Nice Dreams*. Timothy Leary appears in that film as himself. That creates a small trail from Gilbert’s hidden workshop to Leary’s durable instruction: “Think for yourself. Question authority.”

The trail is associative, not a claim that *Nice Dreams* secretly explains *They Live*. It is the kind of connection that makes character actors worth following. Keith David deserves his own feature. So do the recurring faces around Carpenter, Roger Corman, Joe Dante, and the larger ecosystem that includes Dick Miller. I found no precise Buck Flower and Dick Miller collaboration worth claiming here. The honest connection is the kind of work they did: faces that made a movie world feel larger than its leads.

Carpenter and Sandy King kept building too, through films, music, and Storm King Comics. The current catalog contains science fiction, horror, children’s work, and new releases. The signal did not remain trapped in one 1988 movie.

## 9. He lives in the simulation

*Saints Row IV* takes the afterlife literally.

Much of the game occurs inside a simulated Steelport. Keith David appears as Keith David, serving as vice president. Roddy Piper appears as Roddy Piper. The mission “He Lives” pulls their history into the simulation, revisits the fight, and turns *They Live* into playable language.

The joke works because David had already become a major game voice and because the movie’s interface had become familiar. By 2013, hidden systems, simulated cities, corrupted visual layers, and realities revealed through a device belonged to games as naturally as they belonged to post-*Matrix* science fiction.

The film did not need to predict every later work to travel through them. Piper and David carried the signal themselves.

## 10. Put on the glasses, then make something

Frank resists the glasses because seeing is not merely a technical problem.

The tool works. Nada can put it on Frank’s face. The hard part is consent. Accepting the view would reorganize Frank’s understanding of television, work, money, neighbors, police, success, and safety. He fights for the world he knows because the replacement is a war.

That is why the long fight matters. Evidence does not enter a person as neutral information. It threatens relationships, habits, plans, and the story that made yesterday survivable.

I have spent enough time explaining that the billboard has an owner and the system has incentives. The point of seeing is to choose tools more intelligently, preserve useful control, and return to work.

Put on the glasses. Read the roadmap. Read the contract. Find the buyer. Check who owns the data and who receives the guarantee. Think for yourself. Question authority, including the authority of anybody selling permanent suspicion as wisdom.

Then take the glasses off.

Give Carpenter, Piper, David, Foster, Jason, Flower, King, Howarth, Kibbe, and the people who built that rough Los Angeles their credit. Follow the actors into the next movie. Follow the score into music. Follow the posters into design, the comics into magazines, the simulation into games, the workshop into better tools, and the cardboard box into whatever somebody decided to make by hand.

The diagnostic work should end somewhere.

I want to get back to movies, music, magazines, history, cartoons, character actors, strange production systems, Other Alice, and worlds that do more than explain what is wrong with this one.

Put on the glasses long enough to see who the product was made for. Then make something of your own.
