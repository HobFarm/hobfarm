A coworker of mine plays chess on his phone whenever work gives him a spare minute. Not just the occasional game, either. He has the look of somebody who has opened a door and found an entire building behind it.

He told me about a Bobby Fischer documentary. The title did not stick. The part that stuck was him explaining Bobby Fischer to one of our younger employees.

I can imagine the answer before anybody says it:

**Who the hell is Bobby Fischer?**

That is not an insult. It is the correct question if you were born in 2008 and nobody has routed Fischer into your life.

I was not born when Fischer played Boris Spassky in Reykjavík in 1972. I am not *that* old. I am old enough, though, that Bobby Fischer arrived as part of the general cultural weather. I knew the name before I knew much about chess. I knew he had beaten the Russians, had become a difficult recluse, and had gone off somewhere dark. The details were foggy, but the fame was assumed.

For a younger person, there is no reason to assume any of that. Fischer is not a current champion, a streamer, a face from a short video, or somebody the algorithm needs them to know. His name has to enter through a particular door: a documentary, a chess game, a coworker telling a story, an old television clip that lands in the recommendations.

A few days after that conversation, YouTube gave me two Bobby Fischer appearances from *The Dick Cavett Show*.

I had not searched for them.

Maybe YouTube had listened to us. More likely, I had recently watched [Salvador Dalí bring an anteater onto Cavett's stage](/articles/mr-paige-theres-an-anteater-behind-you/), and the system followed the typed edge from Cavett to another old guest. That explanation is less sinister and more useful. Recommendation systems do not need a microphone when our viewing histories already describe what belongs next to what.

The clips did more than remind me who Fischer was. They showed me a kind of celebrity that barely exists now: a chess player sitting on a general-interest talk show because the country was expected to care.

## How far ahead can you see?

The question Cavett asks Fischer is the question almost everybody asks a strong chess player: How many moves can you see in advance?

It sounds as if the expert has a longer flashlight. I see two moves down a tunnel; Fischer sees twenty. The romance of chess depends heavily on this image. A grandmaster looks at sixty-four squares and watches the future unspool.

Fischer gives the less magical answer: “It depends on the position.” In a complicated position he might calculate one or two moves while considering many subvariations. In a forced, simplified line he might see ten or twenty moves. The distance changes because the number of plausible branches changes.

Chess is finite, but finite is not small. In 1950, Claude Shannon estimated a typical position at roughly thirty legal moves and a game tree on the order of 10<sup>120</sup> possible variations. He was not trying to produce a sacred final count. He was explaining why a machine could not just walk every branch and return with the answer.[^shannon]

Humans cannot do that either.

The useful skill is not seeing every future. It is deleting futures.

Strong players recognize structures they have seen before. They generate better candidate moves sooner. They devote calculation to checks, captures, threats, tactical necessities, and strategically credible plans. Then they stop wasting time on lines that no longer deserve attention. Experiments on chess expertise support both parts of that process: pattern recognition matters, and so does search, but experts are not simply searching every position more deeply than everyone else.[^expert-search] Studies of memory find the same distinction. Masters have a large advantage when pieces form meaningful chess positions; scramble the board, and much of that advantage shrinks.[^templates]

<FischerFigure kind="future" />

This is why a grandmaster can appear to see the future. The board is not equally mysterious in every direction. Experience compresses parts of it into familiar shapes. Calculation begins after recognition has already removed a great deal of nonsense.

That makes Fischer's answer on Cavett better than the legend. Sometimes the position demands a long line. Sometimes it demands one move examined across a forest of replies. Expertise is knowing what kind of future the current position contains.

## One player against a system

Cavett's 1971 introduction sounds almost like the opening of a spy picture. Soviet players dominate international chess. Fischer is preparing to take them on. He has just beaten Mark Taimanov 6-0 and Bent Larsen 6-0. Tigran Petrosian is next. If Fischer gets through him, Boris Spassky and the world championship wait on the other side.[^fide-fischer]

The language was dramatic, but the structure was real.

On the July 1972 FIDE rating list, Fischer was number one at 2785. Spassky was second at 2660, a 125-point gap. Seven of the other nine players in the top ten represented the Soviet Union.[^ratings-1972]

<FischerFigure kind="ratings" />

The Soviet chess establishment had coaches, analysts, tournaments, institutional support, and generations of elite competition. Fischer had seconds and allies at different points, but the public story turned him into the singular American mind facing a machine made of people.

That story was ready-made for 1972. The United States and Soviet Union had spent decades converting almost every arena into evidence: missiles, spaceflight, Olympic medals, ballet, defectors, wheat production, kitchen appliances. Chess offered a perfect Cold War stage because intelligence itself seemed to be sitting in plain view. Nobody had to explain why winning mattered. The winner would appear to have thought better.

Fischer beat Petrosian 6½-2½, then defeated Spassky 12½-8½ in Reykjavík.[^fide-fischer] He became the first American-born world champion and broke a Soviet title run that had lasted since 1948.

The match was full of material television understands: threatened withdrawals, disputes over money and cameras, a forfeited game, diplomats and organizers trying to keep the event alive, and an opponent from each superpower. Henry Kissinger reportedly called Fischer before the match and urged him to go to Iceland.[^kissinger]

The public could follow even if it barely understood the board.

## Chess on television without the chess players

The strangest part of the 1972 television story is that much of the American audience did not watch a clean live shot of Fischer and Spassky leaning over the pieces.

They watched Shelby Lyman in a studio.

Moves arrived from Reykjavík and Lyman recreated them on a demonstration board. He analyzed. He waited. He filled dead air. New York public television carried the program, other stations picked it up, and the audience grew far beyond what anyone expected a chess broadcast to hold. Contemporary accounts place the match in general news, magazines, bars, and public television. The precise national audience totals are messy, but the scale of attention is not in doubt.[^congress][^lyman]

Chess became compelling television by making the invisible visible. A man stood beside a board and explained the pressure building inside a position nobody at home could see directly.

Fischer's Cavett appearances did something similar. The show did not treat him as a curiosity from a subculture. Cavett introduced the competitive situation, asked about preparation and mental stamina, and put a board between them. Fischer could be awkward, severe, funny, and patient in the space of the same interview. He was there long enough to become a person instead of a headline.

The second official clip now on YouTube carries an August 4, 1972 broadcast date. Cavett opens with the current score from Reykjavík, then rolls an interview recorded January 4. By the time the segment aired, Fischer and Spassky were not an abstract future booking. They were the story already in progress.

Near the end, Cavett asks whether the possible moves are finite. Fischer says they are, then adds that the number is “more than a mind can fathom.” A few seconds later he predicts that growing interest could carry international chess onto network television.

For a moment, that is exactly where history appears to be going.

## The celebrity did not end cleanly

Fischer did not defend his title in 1975 after he and FIDE could not agree on match conditions. Anatoly Karpov became champion by default. Fischer then disappeared from serious competition for nearly twenty years.

He returned in 1992 for an unofficial rematch with Spassky in Yugoslavia and won 10-5, but the event violated U.S. sanctions. He became a fugitive from the United States. His later public life was filled with antisemitic, anti-American, and conspiratorial statements. Japanese authorities detained him in 2004 over his revoked U.S. passport. Iceland granted him citizenship in 2005, and he died in Reykjavík in 2008.[^fischer-obit]

This cannot be filed under eccentric genius and left there. The hatred was real. It is part of who Fischer became in public.

It also complicates the inherited name. Older people may remember several Fischers layered together: the Brooklyn prodigy, the American who broke the Soviet championship, the missing recluse, the man returning for a strange 1992 rematch, and the bitter voice emerging through radio interviews. A younger person who encounters him through a documentary may receive all of those versions in two hours.

Celebrity preserved his achievement and his damage. It did not settle the relationship between them.

## The next public metaphor

Twenty-five years after Reykjavík, chess became a mass-media symbol again. This time the opponent was not another country. It was IBM.

Garry Kasparov beat Deep Blue in a six-game match in 1996. In the May 1997 rematch, the upgraded computer won 3½-2½, the first time a reigning world champion had lost a match to a computer under standard tournament conditions.[^deep-blue]

Deep Blue could evaluate roughly 200 million positions per second. In certain lines it could search as far as thirty moves, although that was not a fixed depth it reached in every position.[^deep-blue-hardware] The machine's speed was astonishing, but it still depended on human-built evaluation functions, opening material, endgame databases, custom chips, and choices about which lines deserved additional search.

In other words, the public metaphor changed before the underlying problem did.

Fischer had represented an American individual against a Soviet institution. Kasparov represented humanity against computation. Both stories turned chess into a clean board on which a much larger anxiety could be played.

<FischerFigure kind="meaning" />

The Deep Blue match also marked a change in the kind of cultural knowledge chess carried. In 1972, the question was whether one American could take the title from the Soviet system. In 1997, the question was whether the machine could take the intellectual game from us. Today, using an engine to analyze a game is ordinary. The crisis became infrastructure.

## Chess did not get smaller

The disappearance of a universally recognized champion would be easy to mistake for a decline in chess. The available evidence points somewhere else.

Chess.com reported 9.7 million daily active users, 14.9 million new accounts, and 2.6 billion games during the first quarter of 2026. Those are company figures, not an independent census, and accounts are not the same as unique people. They still describe activity at a scale the television producers of 1972 could not have imagined.[^chesscom]

The championship also escaped the old U.S.-Soviet frame. Gukesh D of India is the reigning world champion. Uzbekistan's Javokhir Sindarov won the 2026 Candidates Tournament and is next in line to challenge him. Magnus Carlsen of Norway remains first on the August 2026 open rating list.[^fide-cycle][^fide-current]

Carlsen has crossed into general-interest culture. *Time* named him one of its 100 most influential people in 2013.[^time-carlsen] Millions of people know him. Millions more can play chess every day without knowing who currently holds the title.

That is the change.

Modern chess has championship broadcasts, live commentary, streamers, instructional channels, puzzle streaks, cheating scandals, engines, memes, speed formats, school programs, celebrity tournaments, and a permanent archive of old games. It is not waiting for three networks to decide whether chess belongs on television.

It is also no longer guaranteed that a person who follows politics, movies, and general news will pass through the same cultural room as the world chess champion.

Network television still exists, but its old concentrating power has weakened. CBS ended *The Late Show* in 2026 after a thirty-three-year franchise run, while other late-night programs reduced some of the performance space that once placed specialists and emerging artists in front of audiences who had not requested them.[^late-night] The programs that remain compete with every archive, stream, game, podcast, and feed available at the same hour.

When Bobby Fischer sat across from Dick Cavett, scarcity did part of the publicity work. There were fewer rooms, so entering one large room mattered more.

Today there are more doors than walls.

<FischerFigure kind="routing" />

This is the same delivery problem I found when [Britain's record-breaking number-one song had never entered my listening world](/articles/the-number-one-song-ive-never-heard/). A chart can accurately count an enormous audience without forcing the song into everybody else's life. A chess platform can host billions of games without making the current champion a household name. [MTV once concentrated music, promotion, style, and argument inside one scheduled channel](/articles/i-want-my-mtv/). YouTube now contains the whole wreckage and the whole replacement: official archives, unauthorized fragments, new analysis, live streams, and whatever it decides belongs next.

The audience is not absent. It has been routed.

## Who gets carried forward?

My coworker found Fischer through chess. I found him again through Cavett. A younger employee might find him through that coworker, a documentary, a speedrun, a cheating debate, an engine lesson, or never.

The title of this article depends on the gap between those routes.

“Who the hell is Bobby Fischer?” sounds blunt because inherited fame always mistakes itself for basic knowledge. If everybody around you knew a name, the name feels permanent. But celebrity is not a property stored inside a person. It is a distribution system built around them.

Fischer had the board, the Cold War, the Soviet rating list, the threatened match, public television, magazine covers, and Dick Cavett. Modern champions have a larger chess network and a smaller claim on any one shared national room.

That does not make their fame lesser. It makes it specialized, international, measurable in games and subscribers, and easy for the next feed over to miss.

I know who Bobby Fischer was because I am old enough to remember the residue of the room he once occupied. Somebody born in 2008 received a different map.

The board stayed the same. Everybody else got a different television.
