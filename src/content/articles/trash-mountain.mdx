## Something smoking on Google Earth

I found the garbage mountain because I was looking at something else.

The Îles de Los sit off the coast of Conakry, Guinea, like pieces of a broken ring. Behind them, the shoreline narrows into a long peninsula covered with roofs, roads, neighborhoods and airport pavement. I was moving around Google Earth, trying to understand the shape of the place, when I noticed smoke in the middle of the city.

I zoomed in.

The smoke was rising from garbage.

The gray and brown mass filled a former quarry inside dense development. It was not one burning bin or a roadside pile. Historical images showed a long artificial landform changing shape, expanding, turning green in places and burning again. Houses and roads pressed close around it.

I have never been to Africa. Everything here comes from the map, dated imagery, local reporting, official records and technical research. The useful part of my discovery was not authority. It was the order in which the place revealed itself: coast, peninsula, smoke, dump, history.

## The bin is a promise

A trash can is a simple machine as long as somebody empties it.

I have photographed overflowing cans and discarded objects for years. I was not building an environmental archive. I saw trash and took a photograph.

The pictures record the same temporary condition. An object has left one owner, but it has not reached whatever the system intends to make of it next. Once the bin is full, the system becomes visible. Bags stand above the rim. A comforter takes the sidewalk. Cups move onto a restaurant counter. Pizza boxes claim the pavement beside the container.

<TrashPhotoGrid photos={overflowPhotos} label="Three ordinary waste-capacity failures" columns={3} />

At my house in Las Vegas, disappearance is reliable. I put bags in a wheeled bin. Once a week, a truck empties it. The fee is small enough that I rarely have to think about the labor, routes, transfer stations, cells, gas, water and monitoring that keep the kitchen clean.

Nobody wants rotting garbage in the kitchen. Reliable collection is an infrastructure convenience and a form of distance. It is not virtue.

Conakry has had organized collection and transfer systems. World Bank records describe small operators moving waste to grouping points and larger systems hauling it to La Minière. Coverage and performance changed as funding, contractors and institutions changed. The final site kept receiving material while replacement and closure plans lagged behind it.[^conakry-chain]

The bin is the smallest capacity boundary in that chain. A city adds storage, collection, transfer, sorting, treatment, drainage, gas control, land and money. Each stage has to move material before the next load arrives.

Every garbage mountain begins with a container someone keeps filling.

<TrashPhotoGrid photos={transferPhotos} label="Waste between use, sorting and collection" columns={3} />

Metal cans may become a commodity when they are separated and worth hauling. Cups may remain mixed waste. A full restaurant cabinet can spill while the rest of the business keeps operating. The object called garbage is partly a material and partly a decision about who moves it next.

<TrashPhotoGrid photos={curbsidePhotos} label="Objects at the curbside boundary" columns={2} />

The shopping carts are the hardest image in the group. Cardboard, clothing and bags may be possessions, salvage, stored material or trash. The photograph cannot settle that classification. A waste system often has to.

## Maine after the last truck

I grew up in Hampden, Maine, with Sawyer’s landfill in the local mental map. I lived in town, not beside it. I remember a place that kept getting higher and a flame that appeared above it.

The records supply the structure beneath that memory. Disposal at the site began in the early 1970s. The original conventional landfill was unlined. Later secure cells were built around it and piggybacked over earlier waste. The renamed Pine Tree Landfill closed in phases from 2008 through 2010 and reached a reported top elevation of 320 feet. Collected landfill gas still burns in a flare. Water quality, leachate, gas and corrective work continue after closure.[^pine-tree]

Maine’s Department of Environmental Protection oversees more than 400 closed municipally owned or operated landfill sites. Cover has to be mowed and repaired. Water may need monitoring. Gas and leachate still need somewhere to go. Some closed sites have ordinary afterlives. Falmouth identifies its Hardy Road Conservation Area as former landfill property with public trails.[^maine-closure]

A closed landfill is garbage wearing a landscape.

Maine and Guinea are different waste systems with different land, budgets, histories and exposure. The connection is the material. It does not vanish in either place. One system can cap a mound, collect gas and fund decades of monitoring. Another can keep sending waste into an overloaded quarry while new plans remain plans.

## The dump that outlived its expiration date

I opened the historical timeline.

In October 2000, much of the Dar-es-Salam basin appeared green or wet. Disturbed ground was visible in the southern half, and dense housing already surrounded the site. By 2007, broad brown ground and smoke were prominent. In 2015, exposed waste extended farther through the center. In May 2019, the dump occupied most of the basin and structures remained pressed against its southern edge.

Then the edge changed.

<SatelliteTimeline />

The green northern section in the 2020 frame does not prove that the site closed. It may be vegetation, cover, seasonal difference, reduced activity in one part of the basin, or several of those at once. The February 2025 frame removes any temptation to write a clean ending. Broad exposed waste and multiple smoke plumes are visible again.

As of July 1, 2026, Guinea’s government still described Dar-es-Salam’s closure as progressive while reporting work on a replacement technical landfill at Baritodhé.[^guinea-status] The dump had not become a finished historical object.

## The homes were removed

A close image from May 6, 2019 shows roofs along the southern foot of the waste mass. A second image from December 6 shows open ground across a broad strip.

Human Rights Watch documented forced demolitions beginning later that May and counted at least 386 destroyed buildings in Dar-es-Salam through satellite analysis. The supplied pair is not the same exact before-and-after set used by Human Rights Watch, so it cannot assign every missing roof to one operation. It establishes the visible sequence.[^hrw]

The dump remained.

The removal followed a separate disaster. In August 2017, part of the Dar-es-Salam waste mass collapsed during heavy rain. Early reports gave lower totals; Amnesty International later recorded at least ten deaths, including two children.[^amnesty]

The 2017 collapse and the 2019 demolitions are not one event. Together they show how the distance between a dump and a neighborhood can be created. In this case, the city removed buildings from the edge. The waste stayed.

## When garbage becomes geology

Mixed waste is an ugly engineering material. It contains food, plastic, textiles, wood, metal, rubble, voids, gas and water. Its strength changes with age and depth. Parts decay. Parts stretch. Water moves through some layers and stalls above others.

The basic failure sequence is plain. A pile grows high or steep. Rain or poorly controlled leachate raises the internal water level. Water pressure rises and useful friction falls. A weak surface forms inside the waste, through the foundation, or along an interface. The mass begins as a slump or slide. Fragmentation and water can turn it into a fast flow with a much longer runout.

<WasteSlopeMechanism />

Across a study of 62 landfill slope failures, high leachate level was the leading coded cause, followed by inadequate compaction and weak foundation capacity.[^slope-study] Rain is a common trigger. It is not the complete mechanism.

Fire can join the process. It reduces visible bulk, creates voids, damages drainage and gas systems, and puts workers or responders onto unstable ground. At Leuwigajah in Indonesia, a detailed field study connected heavy rain, deep internal fire and biogas release to the February 2005 avalanche. The waste traveled about a kilometre, buried 71 houses and killed 143 people.[^leuwigajah]

Other slides are mainly water-and-slope failures. Fire is not required.

## The known record

The failure at Conakry belongs to a record that is larger than I expected and less complete than a clean statistic would suggest.

In July 2000, saturated waste at Payatas in the Philippines became a fast flow after ten days of extreme rain. The commonly repeated official death toll is 218. An engineering reconnaissance recorded more than 330 people buried, 58 rescued and 278 bodies recovered. Those figures cannot be merged into one falsely precise number.[^payatas]

Koshe in Addis Ababa killed 116 people in 2017 according to UN-Habitat. Hulene in Maputo killed 17 in 2018 when waste buried seven houses at night. Kiteezi in Uganda killed 35 in August 2024; a March 2026 UN update still used that confirmed count.[^koshe-kiteezi]

<CaseImageStrip />

The audit assembled for this article located 22 fatal waste-mass failures between September 1996 and July 2026. Its conservative confirmed total is 691. It rises to 751 if the Payatas engineering account’s 278 recovered bodies replaces the usual 218 figure. Ten events reached residential areas. Eight killed at least six residents.

The documented record becomes much denser after 2000. No complete international registry exists. Better reporting, digital archives and language access may explain part of that pattern. Growth in urban waste, steep vertical dumping and more people living or working inside runout zones may explain part of it too. The audit shows a recurring failure mode. It does not prove a clean global rate.

## Other destinations

Waste can concentrate until it becomes a mountain. It can also escape the chain and spread through drains, rivers and coastlines.

The Great Pacific Garbage Patch is not a solid island. It is an enormous shifting accumulation zone with debris distributed through the water. A 2018 study estimated about 79,000 tonnes across 1.6 million square kilometres. Microplastics dominated the number of pieces while larger objects carried most of the mass; fishing nets accounted for at least 46 percent of the estimated mass.[^ocean]

A 2021 model found that more than a thousand rivers account for 80 percent of modeled river-borne plastic emissions to the ocean. The old ten-river shorthand was too narrow.[^rivers]

On land, garbage can concentrate until it becomes a mountain. At sea, it can disperse until it becomes part of the environment.

## Somewhere else

Once a week, a truck empties my bin in Las Vegas.

The service works because the visible container is connected to a much larger chain. For household purposes, the job is complete when the garbage is no longer in the kitchen. I do not have to see the final cell, watch the leachate level, repair the cap or stand beside the flare.

The machinery creates a useful illusion. The garbage is gone.

Google Earth collapses the distance. The place called “away” becomes visible. Sometimes it is a capped hill with a trail. Sometimes it is a working landfill with engineered cells. Sometimes it is foam mixed into a shoreline. Sometimes it is a smoking artificial mountain inside a capital city.

Sometimes the mountain moves.
