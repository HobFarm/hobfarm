After sitting down to watch *Tales from the Crypt* because I wanted something shorter than a movie, I ended up in the usual rabbit hole. Old EC horror comics led to *Creepshow*. *Creepshow* led to Stephen King. Stephen King led me back to a story I have been telling coworkers for years:

I may have inspired *It*.

I have no proof. That is what makes it one of my better stories.

This is not a claim I plan to defend in court. It is a Bangor story. Bangor stories work best when the evidence is suspiciously convenient and the final answer remains unavailable.

## The birdbath changed every week

I was born in Bangor, Maine, in 1980. My parents lived in a little blue house below Thomas Hill. They planted a maple tree when I was born. The tree is enormous now because I am old.

Across the neighborhood, Stephen King was living in the big red Victorian on West Broadway and preparing to turn Bangor into Derry.

The author’s note at the end of *It* says the book was begun in Bangor on September 9, 1981, and completed there on December 28, 1985. I was one when he started and five when he finished. Those were also the years when my father regularly took me to the little park at the Thomas Hill Standpipe.

We would stop for candy along the way. At the park, my father lifted me up so I could inspect the birdbath.

This was important work.

The birdbath was never the same twice. The water level went up and down. Leaves appeared. Bugs arrived. Things floated, sank, hatched, vanished, or rotted. Every visit revealed a completely new environment. It was a tiny world running its own weekly simulation, and I needed to know what had changed.

Behind it stood the Thomas Hill Standpipe, an enormous white water tower with a hidden iron tank, a spiral stairway, and a viewing deck around the top.

I was convinced Spider-Man lived inside it.

Years later, I read *It* and found Stan Uris watching birds around the Derry standpipe. The birdbath and water tower belonged to the same landscape I remembered. The monster eventually produced a giant spider.

So while Stephen King was writing a book about a child interested in birds near a water tower containing supernatural horrors, I was an actual child obsessively inspecting the birdbath beside that water tower while insisting that a spider-man lived inside it.

Coincidence is certainly one explanation.

The other explanation is that Stephen King observed me, recognized the raw material of genius, and built one of the biggest horror novels ever written around my weekend park routine.

I prefer the second explanation because I contributed nothing else to the project and would still like partial credit.

## The neighborhood was already Derry

Could King and I actually have crossed paths? I have no idea.

We occupied the same small section of Bangor during the exact years he was writing the book. He lived up the hill on the fancy street with the Victorian mansions. I lived down the hill with the regular folk. He was a famous writer, but he was also a person who lived in town and went places like everyone else.

The possibility is enough for the story.

King has described Derry as Bangor wearing another name. The disguise was never especially elaborate. Bangor already had the correct machinery: the standpipe, old cemeteries, storm drains, wealthy streets, ordinary neighborhoods, brick downtown blocks, river traffic, woods, industrial sites, and the Kenduskeag Stream cutting through the center of town.

Downtown, the Kenduskeag runs through stone walls and concrete channels, squeezed between old buildings and bridges. Farther out, it becomes rocks, trees, pools, culverts, and places where a kid could disappear from one version of the city into another.

That is the part outsiders can miss when they treat Bangor as a Stephen King theme park. King did not have to import much. He recognized how the place already worked.

The local storm drain now folded into the Georgie mythology was near the neighborhood where I lived. I could have been that kid.

I was not, which was a good early career decision.

## I escaped Derry and entered *Carrie*

We moved away from that house around 1985. Later, I attended Hampden Academy, which turned out to be another building inside the extended Stephen King production.

Hampden Academy was chartered in 1803, when Maine was still part of Massachusetts. The original wooden academy is gone. It burned in 1842, and the brick Old Academy in my photograph was built in 1843.

The school grounds were already historic before that building existed. The Battle of Hampden was fought there in 1814, when British forces advanced up the Penobscot during the War of 1812. For a brief period, the British controlled eastern Maine and revived the idea of a colony called New Ireland.

That means I went to high school on a War of 1812 battlefield.

This is what Maine considers a normal school district.

Stephen King began teaching English at Hampden Academy in 1971. He was working there while writing the novel that became *Carrie*. The school’s own history points to its old gym as a room similar to the gymnasium where Carrie’s prom becomes a bloodbath.

I photographed the gym after the old campus had found new uses. The stage, basketball hoops, institutional lights, and polished floor were still there.

It looked perfectly capable of hosting a normal community event or a catastrophic supernatural prom.

I am not claiming King copied this exact room board for board. The school makes the connection, and the room supplies its own argument. It is one more ordinary place from my life that acquired a second identity after I learned how King had used the area around him.

## Downriver, the trucks kept moving

The pattern continues down the Penobscot.

In 1979, King was living in a rented house in Orrington while working as a writer-in-residence at the University of Maine. The house bordered a busy truck route. Local children had made a pet cemetery in the woods. After his daughter’s cat was killed on the road, the pieces came together and became *Pet Sematary*.

That one does not need my unprovable contribution. King has explained the chain himself.

The 1989 movie kept the story in Maine. Mary Lambert directed it after building a major career in music videos, including work with Madonna. The movie uses “Sheena Is a Punk Rocker,” then ends with the Ramones song written for the film. King appears as the minister because apparently the entire state was available for casting.

The trucks in *Pet Sematary* belong to the same industrial landscape that surrounded the Penobscot. Mills in Bucksport, Brewer, Lincoln, Millinocket, Old Town, Milford, and other towns supplied jobs, tax bases, smells, traffic, skylines, and a sense that the machinery would always be there.

It was not always there.

I photographed the Bucksport mill at night in 2014, shortly before it shut down. The stacks were lit. Steam and smoke moved through the orange industrial glow. Reflections stretched across the river. It looked permanent.

By the end of that year, the paper mill was closed. About 500 jobs disappeared with it. The closure became part of a much larger collapse and restructuring of Maine’s paper industry.

Eastern Fine Paper in Brewer closed. Lincoln and Millinocket were hit. The Old Town mill closed, reopened under new ownership, and changed again. The exact corporate timeline differs from town to town, but the regional story is familiar: places built around enormous industrial systems had to figure out what remained when those systems stopped.

The Old Town or Milford mill always smelled like shit when I was growing up.

That is a childhood memory, not a current environmental reading. Anyone who grew up around a paper mill understands that distinction.

## The witch outlasted the mill

Across from the industrial history of Bucksport sits a much older local story.

A large granite monument honors Jonathan Buck, the town’s founder. A dark stain appeared on the stone in a shape resembling a woman’s leg and foot. The legend says Buck condemned a witch, who cursed him and promised that her mark would appear on his grave.

Historical research gives the legend very little support. Buck lacked the authority to impose a death sentence, and there is no record of anyone being executed by burning for witchcraft in Maine.

The stain came first. The witch grew around it.

That is the mechanism behind most good local legends. A physical object exists. Time adds ambiguity. Somebody supplies a story. The story gets repeated until it becomes part of the place.

Which brings us back to my birdbath.

Did Stephen King actually see me at Thomas Hill and borrow my Spider-Man theory?

Probably not.

Was I a child living near his house during the exact years he wrote *It*, repeatedly visiting the exact park he transformed into fiction, studying the birdbath and imagining a spider-person inside the standpipe?

Yes.

That is enough to keep the story alive.

Bangor supplied the hardware: the standpipe, drains, stream, old houses, battlefield school, mills, truck routes, cemeteries, and cursed stone. Stephen King wrote the software.

I may have submitted one bug report from the birdbath.
