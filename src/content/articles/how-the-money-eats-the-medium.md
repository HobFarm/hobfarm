In 1980, Kurt Vonnegut told a room of writing students that they were learning a living craft inside a dying business.

Teaching fiction, he said, had become like teaching someone to play the sackbut, the virginal, or the lute. The student might become excellent. The teacher would eventually have to admit that there was probably no outlet for the skill.

Vonnegut blamed “practical television.” Advertising agencies decided television was a better purchase, advertising money left the magazines, and the publications that had paid writers for short fiction disappeared. He was careful about the diagnosis. The change was technological and economic, rather than evidence that readers had undergone some mysterious spiritual decline.

By 1999, he described pre-television America as “short-story-crazy.” *The Saturday Evening Post* and *Collier’s* each published five stories every week. *Cosmopolitan* published five every month. Stories circulated as popular entertainment, and an ordinary writer could turn one successful sale into serious household money.

Television did not kill the short story. It helped destroy the mass-market business that bought short stories.

The wider pulp and magazine collapse had several causes. Wartime paper shortages, rising production costs, distribution trouble, paperbacks, comics, changing tastes, and television all contributed. Vonnegut was speaking mainly about the slick general-interest magazines, while writers such as Philip K. Dick, Ray Bradbury, Isaac Asimov, Robert E. Howard, and H.P. Lovecraft moved through overlapping pulp, digest, fantasy, horror, and science-fiction markets. The important fact is that the entire ecology contracted.

The living part of the craft had been removed.

## The living part

A market provides more than money.

Those magazines supplied editors, deadlines, assignments, readers, criticism, competition, and repeated public practice. Vonnegut remembered selling a short story for more than General Electric was paying him, which gave him a path out of corporate employment. He also praised the supposedly lowbrow advice supplied by magazine editors: remove anything that neither reveals character nor advances the action. It sounded like hack work until he recognized Aristotle underneath it.

The magazines were an apprenticeship system disguised as entertainment.

A writer could complete a story, receive editorial feedback, sell it, see it published, and begin another one. Every story was both a finished product and another round of training. The system could tolerate minor work, experiments, failures, genre exercises, and strange little premises because another issue had to be filled next week.

Modern media companies still want breakthrough writers and valuable intellectual property. They are less interested in funding the repetitive, uncertain work that allows those things to develop.

They want the writer after somebody else has paid for the writer to become useful.

## Philip K. Dick’s prototype factory

Philip K. Dick is one of the clearest products of that lost machine.

According to his official bibliography, Dick wrote and sold 67 stories during 1952 and 1953. He eventually produced approximately 121 short stories, most of them for science-fiction magazines. That early burst was where he began finding his voice.

Sixty-seven stories in two years is more than hustle. It is research and development.

Every story gave Dick somewhere to test a machine, a social arrangement, a paranoid possibility, a philosophical problem, or a break in reality. He could run the experiment for twenty pages, reach an ending, put it into circulation, and clear the table for the next one.

Several of those compact magazine experiments later became films, including *Minority Report*, *Paycheck*, *The Adjustment Bureau*, *Screamers*, and *Total Recall*. The original ideas were developed as inexpensive short fiction before Hollywood rebuilt them with movie stars, effects departments, global marketing, and corporate intellectual-property plans.

The industry preserved the valuable ore while allowing the mine to close.

It wants Philip K. Dick properties. It no longer maintains a large paid market where the next Philip K. Dick can write 67 strange stories in two years.

Culture advances through cheap experiments. Institutions remember the hits and eliminate the conditions that produced them.

## Never lose a dime

Roger Corman built the film version of the same system.

His autobiography was called *How I Made a Hundred Movies in Hollywood and Never Lost a Dime*. The title was a boast, a joke, and a production manual compressed into one sentence.

Corman produced more than 300 films and directed around 50. They were made quickly, cheaply, and across every profitable low-budget genre he could reach. Only a handful lost money. His rule for young filmmakers was simple: creative freedom was available as long as they stayed within the budget.

That low-budget operation became one of the most productive training grounds in American film.

Corman gave early opportunities to Francis Ford Coppola, Martin Scorsese, Jonathan Demme, Ron Howard, Peter Bogdanovich, James Cameron, Jack Nicholson, and many others. Howard directed *Grand Theft Auto* for him. Scorsese made *Boxcar Bertha*. Coppola made *Dementia 13*. Cameron worked as an art director on *Battle Beyond the Stars*.

Corman did not manufacture certainty. He manufactured opportunities.

A young filmmaker could receive a marketable premise, a small budget, a short schedule, and enough authority to make actual decisions. Failure remained survivable because the film did not cost enough to destroy the company. Success paid for another movie. Either result produced experience.

The science-fiction magazines trained writers by publishing them repeatedly.

Corman trained filmmakers by putting them to work repeatedly.

Both systems understood something corporate production often forgets: complete work teaches lessons that planning, meetings, courses, focus groups, and development documents cannot.

## The outsiders become the standard

People now look back at American movies from the late 1960s and 1970s and ask where all those inventive young filmmakers came from.

They came from cheap systems.

They came from exploitation pictures, regional productions, television assignments, independent distributors, film schools, drive-in movies, and producers such as Corman who could risk giving an unfinished filmmaker a finished movie to make.

Hollywood hired the successful outsiders. For a while, studio money and outsider instincts produced extraordinary commercial cinema. Then the exception became the model.

The surprising hit became the blockbuster. The blockbuster became the tentpole. The tentpole became the franchise. The franchise became the cinematic universe. The cinematic universe became an intellectual-property maintenance program expected to support theatrical releases, streaming subscriptions, licensing, games, merchandise, and the next ten years of corporate forecasts.

The talent did not disappear. The conditions changed.

A Corman production had little money and therefore little room for supervision. A modern franchise production can have so much money exposed that every decision attracts executives, producers, brand managers, visual-effects vendors, test audiences, continuity rules, licensing plans, and future-installment obligations.

The old system was dangerous because nobody had much to lose.

The current system becomes conservative because everybody has too much money at risk.

A $200 million movie does not contain $200 million worth of creative freedom. It contains $200 million worth of fear.

Hollywood absorbed the Corman generation, turned its successful members into the establishment, copied the visible results, raised the costs, and gradually removed the cheap apprenticeship system that had produced them.

It kept the explosion and threw away the laboratory.

## I watched television recognize its replacement

I saw the next handoff from inside broadcast production around 2005.

Television still carried the equipment, technical standards, professional hierarchy, vocabulary, and physical weight of the dominant system. The internet looked cheap, unreliable, amateur, and full of garbage.

It also had the trajectory.

At production meetings, executives talked about finding ways to connect with “Gen Y,” the name still commonly used for millennials at the time. One proposal was to involve me and a few other younger production people in a project built around “backpack journalism.”

The idea was that one person, or a very small crew, could carry lightweight equipment into the field, shoot material, record sound, conduct interviews, and assemble the story without dragging the entire broadcast machine behind them.

That is now normal.

It became YouTube reporting, livestreaming, phone journalism, creator documentaries, video podcasts, field channels, and independent correspondents broadcasting directly from wherever the story happened. The backpack became a phone, a laptop, a small camera, a wireless microphone, and an internet connection.

Broadcast television could already see the shape of its replacement. It understood that younger audiences wanted something faster, closer, less polished, and less institutional. What it struggled to understand was that this could not be solved by placing a few younger employees inside the existing production hierarchy.

The new form was powerful because it removed much of that hierarchy.

A traditional television operation required studios, transmitters, trucks, departments, approvals, schedules, executives, advertising blocks, and expensive technical infrastructure. Backpack production collapsed several of those jobs into one person and allowed the finished work to reach an audience without passing through a station.

The institution treated this as a new format it might add to the schedule.

It was actually a new production economy.

That is the recurring mistake. An established medium recognizes the surface of the next one, then attempts to contain it as a department, a special project, or a strategy for reaching younger viewers. It copies the camera style and hires a younger host while preserving the overhead, management, and commercial assumptions that made the old system slow.

The audience was not merely asking television to look more like the internet.

The audience was moving to the internet.

The same thing that happened to magazine fiction was beginning to happen to broadcast. Audiences moved. Advertising followed. The old jobs, facilities, and skills did not instantly vanish, but the economic structure supporting them began to contract.

Television had become the student holding the lute.

The machine that redirected advertising away from magazines was now watching advertising and attention move somewhere else.

The sharpest idea here is:

**The institution treated it as a new format. It was actually a new production economy.**

That line also prepares the AI section. Companies currently treat AI as a feature, labor-saving device, or content department. The larger change is that individuals and tiny teams can now perform combinations of work that previously required an institution.

## The audience goes around the gate

The internet also gave filmmakers, writers, musicians, critics, comedians, and assorted maniacs a way around the old permission system.

A creator could make work, publish it, find an audience, and demonstrate demand before a broadcaster or studio agreed that the person belonged in the business.

Curry Barker built an audience through YouTube sketches and short films. When he could not find a distributor for the $800 horror feature *Milk & Serial*, he uploaded it to YouTube, where it went viral and helped him secure representation. He then made *Obsession* for $750,000. By June 16, 2026, the film had grossed $286 million worldwide and become the biggest hit in the history of Focus Features.

Kane Parsons developed *Backrooms* from an internet horror concept into a viral YouTube series using Blender. Its feature adaptation cost approximately $10 million and opened with $81.5 million in North America, reaching $118 million worldwide during its opening run.

Cheapness did not automatically make those films good. Thousands of cheap films disappear without a trace.

Cheapness allowed specific ideas to survive.

The filmmakers could develop in public, test their instincts, build relationships with viewers, and prove that an audience existed. They arrived without having passed through the accepted career funnel, which was precisely why they still possessed something the funnel had not flattened.

Their success does not mean audiences have rejected every franchise or corporate production. Large studio films still make enormous amounts of money. It means budget, institutional approval, and established intellectual property no longer guarantee attention.

The audience is willing to go around the gate when something outside it looks more alive.

## The gatekeepers ask for a protected shelf

This produces a historical joke Vonnegut probably would have enjoyed.

Television weakened the magazines because advertisers and audiences found television more attractive. Online creators are now weakening television because audiences and advertisers increasingly find them more attractive. The institutions that benefited from the first disruption now want protection from the second.

A 2026 British government green paper proposes exploring legislation that would require social-media and video-sharing platforms to make news from designated public-service and potentially other approved publishers prominent and easily discoverable. The government presents the proposal as protection for trustworthy information, public-service media, and democratic stability. It also acknowledges that digital platforms let producers bypass traditional broadcasters and monetize their work directly.

There are real public interests involved. Local reporting, emergency information, investigative journalism, children’s programming, regional production, and genuinely public-service work cannot always be funded by immediate popularity. Supporting those functions can be defensible.

A separate problem appears when inherited institutional status becomes a permanent claim on audience attention.

The current British proposal has not yet settled its definition of a “trustworthy” provider, how prominence would work, whether it would always be active, or whether users could switch it off. Those unresolved questions matter because recommendation space is finite. Giving selected institutions guaranteed prominence can reduce the room available to independent voices that audiences deliberately chose.

Australia has already adopted a narrower prominence framework requiring local television services to remain easy to find on connected-TV devices. That policy governs television interfaces rather than YouTube’s general recommendation engine, but it belongs to the same wider argument about whether established broadcasters deserve protected placement as audiences migrate elsewhere.

The declining institution describes its commercial problem as a cultural emergency.

Sometimes it is a cultural emergency. Sometimes it is an executive demanding that the shelf be moved closer to the door.

## The funnel-bucket factory

Independent creators escaped the television executive and found another executive hiding inside the analytics dashboard.

YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, Facebook, and similar platforms offer access to audiences, but they also sort those audiences into advertiser-friendly behavioral categories. The creator who makes one successful horror clip is encouraged to make another horror clip. Then another. The audience becomes a horror bucket, the creator becomes a horror supplier, and the recommendation system becomes an unpaid programming director.

Creators learn what the algorithm rewards and repeat it. The result is a growing field of work that shares the same hooks, pacing, framing, captions, topics, faces, products, and emotional triggers. As *The Verge* summarized the problem, the algorithm rewards what keeps people attached to the platform rather than what is most thoughtful or original.

The funnel begins as discovery and becomes creative management.

Slop is content that knows its category better than it knows what it wants to say.

Corporate media already worked this way through demographics, schedules, comparable titles, test screenings, genre formulas, advertising profiles, and intellectual-property plans. Social media distributed the same behavior to individuals. Every creator can now become a tiny network executive, anxiously studying the numbers and canceling anything that might confuse the bucket.

The internet opened the gate. Advertising built another fence twenty feet behind it.

## AI slop is old slop with the labor bottleneck removed

Now AI has entered the cycle, and everyone is announcing that it will take the jobs.

Some jobs and tasks will be eliminated. Pretending otherwise is useless. The International Labour Organization’s current assessment says job transformation is the most likely broad effect because most occupations combine automatable tasks with work that still requires human input.

AI is the newest machine in a much older process.

Television removed some magazine work. The internet removed some broadcast work. Automation removed technical work. Consolidation removed local work. Outsourcing removed production work. Streaming altered residuals, schedules, and release models. Every economic transition destroys some jobs, changes others, and creates opportunities that the previous system could barely recognize.

People adapt because the alternative is waiting for a dead industry to reassemble itself.

The useful question is not whether AI is morally pure. No production technology has ever been morally pure. The useful questions are who owns it, who captures the savings, who loses bargaining power, who controls distribution, and what becomes possible when the cost of making something falls.

Lazy people will use AI to make more lazy material.

Corporations will use it to reduce labor, clone successful formats, generate endless inventory, and pump existing properties through every available market.

Other people will use the same tools the way Corman used cheap film stock, short schedules, borrowed locations, hungry actors, and unfinished young directors. They will lower the cost of experimentation. They will make projects that could not have survived the old approval process. They will finish things, learn, and make another one.

Cheap tools expose the producer.

The corporate move is to make fifty safer variations of whatever already converted.

The Corman move is to make the strange idea affordable enough to risk.

## Build another rack

Vonnegut did offer an answer.

He said storytellers might have to rescue themselves through “autonomous local collectives.” He wanted culture to become local again because communities needed writers, artists, musicians, and storytellers who could hold up mirrors to lives ignored by national conglomerates.

The web now provides much of the equipment such a collective would have needed.

A small publication can distribute fiction without owning a printing press. It can combine prose, illustration, animation, sound, photography, video, correspondence, and archives. It can publish recurring characters without first converting them into a franchise proposal. It can build its own rack.

That is what **Other Alice Adventures** is for.

Other Alice is a persistent story game built inside an authored Wonderland. Each campaign can draw on prose, illustration, moving scenes, sound, field notes, and artifacts recovered from Wonderland, Wasteland, or the holes connecting them.

The campaign becomes the central object. Choices, relationships, time, and consequences accumulate into a long-form account that no other visit has to repeat. It is a book that makes its own record as you go, while the authored world keeps its characters, places, rules, and history intact.

There is something appropriately circular about using moving images, online publishing, and AI-assisted production to rebuild a home for long-form storytelling. The descendants of the machines that helped dismantle the old market become components of the new publication.

This will not restore Vonnegut’s magazine economy. It does restore the ability to make the work, publish it, own the archive, and invite readers into it without waiting for a conglomerate to decide that short fiction has become profitable again.

The money will move again.

The platform will change again.

The current category will eventually fill with shit.

The durable move is to keep the production cheap enough that the work can lead, good enough that somebody cares, and independent enough that the next advertising migration does not take the whole publication with it.
