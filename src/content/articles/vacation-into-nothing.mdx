On February 15, 1898, reality produced an explosion.

The USS *Maine*, an American battleship stationed in Havana Harbor, blew apart and sank. More than 260 members of its crew died. The cause was uncertain. Tensions between Spain and the United States were already high. Cuban revolutionaries were fighting Spanish colonial rule, reports of Spanish brutality had built sympathy for Cuba, and American expansionists could see opportunities beyond the wreckage.

There were facts, suspicions, interests, eyewitnesses, dead sailors, incomplete investigations, and several governments trying to determine what had happened.

Then there were the newspapers.

William Randolph Hearst’s *New York Journal* and Joseph Pulitzer’s *New York World* had spent years competing for readers through enormous headlines, lurid illustrations, crime, scandal, manufactured urgency, and increasingly dramatic coverage of Cuba. The explosion handed them the perfect story: a real catastrophe with no settled explanation.

They supplied the explanation.

Rumors of a Spanish mine or attack became headlines. Possibilities became accusations. Accusations became public certainty. The destruction of the *Maine* became something Spain had done to America before the evidence could establish that Spain had done it.

Yellow journalism did not single-handedly cause the Spanish-American War. The rebellion in Cuba, Spanish repression, American strategic interests, expansionism, diplomacy, and public sympathy for Cuban independence all mattered. But sensational coverage helped create a climate where war appeared justified, urgent, and increasingly inevitable. The press did not invent the entire situation. It selected the most useful pieces, attached a villain, repeated the story, and sold the resulting certainty by the copy.

The camera did not exist at every street corner. There was no bodycam footage, viral video, Community Note, livestream, Grok reply, translated caption, or coworker walking in the next morning to ask whether everyone had seen what those bastards did.

The machinery was already recognizable.

Something happened.

Evidence was incomplete.

A publisher supplied a motive and a villain.

Emotion increased circulation.

Circulation made the interpretation feel authoritative.

Public reaction became part of the event.

By breakfast, reality had a screenplay.

> This is a brief overview of one recurring media mechanism. The larger story includes whole histories of journalism, propaganda, ownership, censorship, advertising, war reporting, public relations, and platform power. Each could fill its own book. This article follows only the small part needed to see how an event becomes a story, a product, and eventually a memory.

## The Press Was Never Neutral

There was no original golden age when the news arrived clean, objective, and untouched by human intention.

The American press was openly partisan long before Hearst and Pulitzer. Federalists supported newspapers that advanced Federalist policies and attacked their opponents. Jeffersonian Republicans encouraged and financed papers that supported their side. The Library of Congress records that partisan politics helped increase the number of American newspapers from 92 in 1790 to 329 by the end of Thomas Jefferson’s presidency, and all but 56 were identified with a political party.

The election of 1800 was vicious enough to make a modern campaign consultant put down his phone and whisper, “Jesus Christ.” Political papers accused candidates and factions of corruption, aristocracy, atheism, tyranny, national betrayal, and every other available form of wickedness. The newspapers were not impartial referees observing a contest. They were players.

Jefferson defended freedom of the press and used partisan journalism when it served his political project. He also eventually wrote that “nothing can now be believed which is seen in a newspaper.”

That was in 1807.

We have apparently been five minutes from total media collapse for more than two centuries.

The same history reaches farther back through European pamphlets, religious tracts, royal proclamations, revolutionary literature, party papers, censorship systems, and propaganda. Print allowed ideas to travel beyond the speaker’s body. It also allowed accusations, exaggerations, doctrine, rumors, and political narratives to be copied in stable form and distributed to people who had never witnessed the events being described.

This does not mean every claim is equally false, every publisher is equally dishonest, or all journalism is propaganda.

It means information has always arrived through people and institutions making choices.

What deserves attention?

Which witness is credible?

Where does the story begin?

What belongs in the headline?

Which facts fit on the page?

Which facts can wait until tomorrow?

Who owns the press?

Who buys the advertisements?

Who is allowed to speak?

Who will cancel a subscription?

Who might put the editor in jail?

Every version of reality has an editor, even when the editor insists there is no editor.

## Faster Than the Facts

New communications technology repeatedly promised to bring people closer to events.

It usually did.

It also accelerated the circulation of whatever story had been attached to them.

The telegraph separated information from physical transportation. A report no longer had to cross the country at the speed of a horse, train, or ship. By 1866, a durable transatlantic cable connected North America and Europe, allowing information to cross the ocean far faster than any person could travel.

<VacationFigure src="https://cdn.hob.farm/articles/vacation-to-nowhere/telegraph-office-1902.jpg" alt="Black-and-white photograph of three men working among telegraph machines, desks, wires, and communication equipment in the White House." caption="The White House telegraph office around 1902. New media repeatedly reduced the delay between an event and the story attached to it." credit="Barnett McFee Clinedinst, Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division, LC-DIG-ppmsca-37707." sourceUrl="https://www.loc.gov/item/2013651481/" width={1024} height={849} />

Wire services gathered reports and sold them to multiple newspapers. Telephone calls accelerated interviews and newsroom coordination. Radio allowed one voice to address scattered listeners at the same time. The audience no longer had to buy tomorrow’s paper to learn what had supposedly happened today.

Every improvement reduced delay.

None removed interpretation.

A mistaken or dishonest story could now travel faster. A government could address an entire nation. A company could sponsor a program and gain access to its audience. A broadcaster could decide which voice sounded responsible, which voice sounded dangerous, and which voice never reached the microphone.

The history of communication is often presented as a march toward greater access to truth.

It is also a history of reduced cooling time.

The interval between event, interpretation, reaction, and retaliation kept shrinking.

There was less time to investigate before publishing, less time to think before responding, and less distance between a rumor and the public behavior it produced.

The facts got faster.

The bullshit bought a faster car.

## Protect the Children

Not every media campaign begins with a warship.

Sometimes the emergency is a comic book.

In 1954, psychiatrist Fredric Wertham published *Seduction of the Innocent*, arguing that comic books contributed to juvenile delinquency and psychological harm. The United States Senate Subcommittee on Juvenile Delinquency held televised hearings examining crime and horror comics. Publishers faced public pressure, hostile coverage, political scrutiny, and the possibility of government intervention.

The comics industry responded by imposing the Comics Code Authority on itself.

The code restricted violence, crime, sexuality, disrespect toward authority, horror imagery, and even certain words in titles. Vampires, werewolves, walking corpses, torture, cannibalism, and other extremely useful cultural materials were pushed out of mainstream comic distribution. EC Comics was devastated. William Gaines converted *MAD* from a comic book into a magazine partly because magazines did not fall under the code’s authority.

The Senate’s final report did not establish that comic books were causing juvenile crime. The campaign still changed what could be created, distributed, displayed, and sold.

The formula was durable:

Find a cultural object adults do not understand.

Connect it to children.

Select the most lurid examples.

Describe those examples as symptoms of a larger collapse.

Hold hearings.

Invite cameras.

Demand that somebody do something.

Thirty years later, the Parents Music Resource Center applied a similar structure to popular music.

The PMRC assembled its “Filthy Fifteen,” a list of songs associated with sex, violence, drugs, or occult themes. A Senate hearing on record labeling took place on September 19, 1985. Frank Zappa, Dee Snider, and John Denver testified against the proposals. The recording industry eventually adopted parental-advisory labels.

Comics and records were not bodycam videos falsely captioned on Instagram. These moral panics belong in the same history because they demonstrate how an agenda can create a public emergency through framing, repetition, authority, and selective examples.

The threat did not have to be invented from nothing. Horror comics contained horror. Prince sang about sex. Heavy-metal bands deliberately used violent and satanic imagery because violent and satanic imagery is rad.

The persuasive move was to take something real and build a much larger causal story around it.

The comic is not merely a comic. It is producing criminals.

The record is not merely a record. It is corrupting children.

The cultural preference of one group becomes a threat requiring everyone else’s compliance.

The children have discovered culture.

Something must be done.

## The Approved Version Goes on the Air

In 1965, Adrian Cronauer began working as a disc jockey for Armed Forces Radio in Saigon.

The 1987 film *Good Morning, Vietnam* turned that assignment into a comedy about a man who keeps colliding with the distance between official messaging and lived reality. Robin Williams's version of Cronauer brings faster jokes, stranger records, and an actual human voice to military radio. His superiors want the energy. They do not want him deciding for himself which facts belong on the air.

The station has more than one job.

It carries news.

It entertains troops.

It provides a familiar sound from home.

It maintains morale.

Those jobs can coexist until the news threatens the morale.

The useful point is not that every scene happened. Cronauer said the film used substantial exaggeration and invention. He was not expelled from Vietnam, and he did not openly defy a direct order to broadcast a censored story. He joked that doing half of what Williams did on screen would have left him in military prison.

The mechanism underneath the movie was real enough. Cronauer described the station's stateside sound as a morale tool. He also recalled witnessing the aftermath of the bombing of the My Canh restaurant, trying to report it, and being stopped because military censors had not cleared the story.

That makes the film a useful cultural mirror rather than a piece of historical proof.

Something real happens.

An institution decides which version may be heard.

Entertainment makes the package easier to receive.

Uncomfortable information is reduced, delayed, or excluded.

The audience receives a managed reality.

A military radio station is not a social feed. The mechanism still rhymes. The broadcast does not have to be false to be selective. Media does not only inform. It also manages mood.

Propaganda is not always a lie. Often it is a playlist with the bad parts removed.

Reality happens outside.

The approved version goes on the air.

## Conflict Requires Two Chairs

By 1968, television had found another efficient way to turn political disagreement into programming.

ABC News was running behind CBS and NBC. It did not have the resources to match their extensive coverage of the Republican and Democratic national conventions. ABC condensed the day’s events into shorter evening packages and added a recurring attraction: debates between conservative writer William F. Buckley Jr. and liberal writer Gore Vidal.

They were intelligent, articulate, politically opposed, personally contemptuous, and increasingly willing to make that contempt visible.

The debates began as political analysis. They became a feud.

Vidal called Buckley a “crypto-Nazi.” Buckley responded with an anti-gay slur and threatened to punch him. The conventions remained the official subject, but the hostility became the reason people remembered the program. Later accounts, including the documentary *Best of Enemies*, treated the broadcasts as an important prototype for modern pundit television. ABC did not invent televised argument, but it demonstrated that ideological combat could compensate for smaller reporting resources and produce an event of its own.

Reporting requires reporters, cameras, travel, documents, sources, editing, and time.

Conflict requires two chairs.

Once television discovered the formula, the guest no longer needed to appear only as someone who knew something. The guest could represent a side.

Another guest could represent the other side.

The host introduced a subject, established the teams, and waited for the collision.

The argument became the program.

This format has one enormous practical advantage: it never has to resolve anything.

A factual investigation can end. A document can be found. A claim can be disproved. A court can issue a ruling.

An argument between symbolic representatives of two hostile populations can run forever.

The subject changes.

The teams remain.

## The Receipts Still Matter

Six years after Buckley and Vidal, the American president resigned because facts still had consequences.

The Watergate story is often compressed into two heroic reporters, Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, following a secret source until Richard Nixon fell out of the White House. Journalism was essential, but the real mechanism was larger.

Reporters investigated the 1972 break-in at Democratic National Committee headquarters and traced connections toward the Nixon administration. Courts, prosecutors, the FBI, congressional committees, witnesses, grand juries, judges, and other news organizations kept working. The Senate held public hearings. Investigators uncovered the White House taping system. The Supreme Court ordered Nixon to surrender the recordings. The tapes revealed his participation in the cover-up. The House Judiciary Committee adopted articles of impeachment. Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974, before the full House could vote.

Watergate matters here because it keeps this article from collapsing into the easiest possible conclusion:

Everybody lies.

Everything is propaganda.

Nothing can be known.

We are all living inside a simulation operated by lizards.

Good night.

Watergate demonstrates the difference between having an agenda and building a case.

Journalists wanted the story. Newspapers wanted readers. Politicians wanted power. Investigators wanted convictions. Committee members had careers, parties, ambitions, grudges, constituencies, and television cameras.

None of that made the evidence meaningless.

The work produced documents, testimony, recordings, dates, names, contradictions, and a source trail other institutions could inspect.

An agenda is unavoidable.

A method can be audited.

The relevant question is not whether a person or institution has an agenda. Everyone has one.

My agenda is to publish this article and have it make sense.

The question is what constrains that agenda.

Does the writer provide receipts?

Does the publication distinguish fact from inference?

Can another person inspect the source?

Does the outlet correct errors?

Does the conclusion change when the evidence changes?

Is uncertainty presented as uncertainty, or has it already become a 72-point headline declaring Spain guilty?

Watergate is the useful counterweight to yellow journalism.

One manufactured certainty from incomplete evidence.

The other accumulated evidence until a powerful story could no longer be dismissed as partisan noise.

## The Rage Gets a Time Slot

Two years after Nixon resigned, *Network* showed what could happen when television encountered authentic anger and recognized a format.

Howard Beale is a veteran news anchor being fired for poor ratings. He announces on the air that he will kill himself during a future broadcast. The network briefly treats this as a crisis.

Then the ratings arrive.

Beale's collapse becomes a hit. The network gives it lights, music, supporting performers, a title, and a regular schedule. A vulnerable man having a public breakdown becomes the Mad Prophet of the Airwaves because the audience will not look away.

His most famous speech gives the audience something to do with its exhaustion:

> **I'm as mad as hell, and I'm not going to take this anymore.**

People open their windows and yell it into the night.

The anger is not invented. The audience feels ignored, frightened, economically insecure, and surrounded by institutions that no longer seem to answer to them. Beale gives that feeling a sentence.

The network gives the sentence a time slot.

That is what makes *Network* belong here. The film does not argue that public anger is fake. It shows how easily real anger becomes a commercial resource. A sincere outburst can be branded, scheduled, repeated, surrounded by advertising, and measured against last week's audience share.

The institution does not need to believe the speaker.

It needs the speaker to perform.

Beale is allowed to violate broadcasting norms while the violation makes money. When his message threatens the network's corporate interests, the boundary appears. Corporate chairman Arthur Jensen summons him into a dark boardroom and explains that nations and democratic ideals are subordinate to an international system of money and corporate power. Beale returns to the air carrying the approved revelation.

The ratings fall.

The network has a new problem. It no longer wants the angry prophet, but Jensen orders the show to remain on the air regardless of its ratings.

So the executives hire the same revolutionary group they have already turned into entertainment to assassinate Beale during a live broadcast. His death becomes the final episode.

The movie takes the mechanism to a murderous extreme because it is a satire. The underlying rule is ordinary: deviation is welcome while deviation performs. The rebel may attack politicians, culture, viewers, or the general condition of life. Trouble begins when the rebel threatens the machinery that owns the microphone.

The famous speech now circulates online as an inspirational fragment, often detached from the rest of the film. A warning about the manufacture of televised outrage has become reusable outrage content.

That may be the most *Network* thing that could have happened to *Network*.

Today the studio is distributed across millions of phones. The user supplies the breakdown. The ranking system supplies the time slot. The audience supplies the chorus. Advertisers buy the interruption.

The anger may be completely real.

That makes the product better.

## Twenty-Four Hours of Rectangle

CNN launched on June 1, 1980, as the first 24-hour television news network.

This created a practical problem that would eventually spread across the entire information system.

The rectangle had to remain occupied.

A daily newspaper could close an edition. A nightly broadcast could end. A 24-hour channel had to continue whether or not 24 hours of meaningful, verified, visually interesting news had occurred.

A television network cannot display a card reading:

> Nothing sufficiently important has happened. Please go outside.

The schedule must be filled.

Breaking news becomes developing news. Developing news becomes analysis. Analysis becomes reaction. Reaction becomes a panel. The panel produces remarks that become tomorrow’s breaking news.

A small amount of reporting can be expanded through repetition, previews, recaps, speculation, personality, argument, graphics, and commercials.

The phrase *Manufacturing Consent* entered popular culture through Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky’s analysis of mass media and the later documentary built partly from Chomsky’s lectures and appearances. Their propaganda model examined how ownership, advertising, institutional sources, organized pressure, and accepted political assumptions shape what large news systems produce. It did not require every journalist to receive secret instructions in a smoke-filled room. Institutional filters could produce narrow boundaries without a single central conspiracy.

In the documentary, Chomsky also discusses sports as a powerful destination for public intelligence and emotional investment. People memorize statistics, histories, rules, trades, strategies, and entire fantasy systems surrounding games that have little direct bearing on political power.

The useful point is not that sports are evil or that anyone enjoying football has failed democracy.

The point is that attention is finite.

A person can deeply understand a complicated system. The surrounding culture helps determine which systems receive that attention.

Sports provide identities, conflict, heroes, villains, history, statistics, ritual, suspense, and something safe for advertisers to support. Political television increasingly borrowed the same structure.

There were teams.

There were colors.

There were rivalries.

There were commentators explaining why your side had won even when the visible score looked inconvenient.

Entertainment gave people somewhere to place their attention. News organizations sold advertisers predictable access to that attention. Political media learned to attach identity to the package.

Then the internet combined all three.

## 1996: Choose a Category

Fox News and MSNBC both launched in 1996, joining CNN in the expanding 24-hour cable-news market. Fox News went on air on October 7 with Roger Ailes, a former Republican political consultant, leading the operation. MSNBC had launched that July as a partnership between NBC and Microsoft.

On one screen, television companies were discovering how many panels, updates, personalities, scandals, crises, and advertisements could be fitted into a permanent news cycle.

On another screen, I had Yahoo.

Yahoo organized the web into visible categories. Human editors classified websites into a directory because search engines were still primitive and the number of websites was still small enough for people to attempt the job manually. By 1996, Yahoo’s human categorization system was already struggling to keep up with a rapidly expanding web, but the basic interaction remained legible.

I chose where I wanted to go.

Arts.

Computers.

Entertainment.

Games.

Music.

News.

Politics.

Sports.

Yahoo’s categories were imperfect and editorial. Someone had decided where each site belonged. Commercial relationships existed. Popular destinations gained visibility. The system was not politically innocent or mechanically neutral.

The categories were visible.

I could choose Music and leave News sitting exactly where I found it.

From Music, I could find bands, venues, fan pages, record labels, concerts, and festivals. I could discover Warped Tour without already knowing enough to search for Warped Tour. I could probably pay around twenty dollars, enter a large parking lot, and spend a day watching rad bands on small stages with other people who had deliberately gone there to hear them.

Nobody around me was holding a phone above their head because the modern phone-camera-social-network apparatus did not exist.

People brought disposable cameras sometimes. Photographers had passes. Fans could be obnoxious because humanity had already been invented.

The event had not yet become an open-air content mine where every attendee was potentially producing a personal broadcast, filming strangers, blocking views, staging reactions, or gathering proof that they had experienced the experience.

More importantly, I had gone there intentionally.

Yahoo had helped me locate a destination.

It did not need to keep me on Yahoo.

The primitive internet allowed a strange kind of freedom that became less visible as the systems improved.

The intention belonged to me.

## 2000: Reality Gets Called Before It Finishes Happening

The 2000 presidential election showed what happened when live media, institutional competition, political division, and incomplete evidence collided.

On election night, television networks called Florida for Al Gore.

They withdrew the call.

Later they called Florida, and therefore the presidency, for George W. Bush.

They withdrew that call too.

Votes remained uncounted. Returns were incomplete. Ballots were disputed. Legal challenges followed. The actual event was still happening, but the medium required an answer.

Congressional hearings and internal reviews later examined the decision-desk failures. CNN’s independent review described serious errors in the process.

The problem was larger than one incorrect projection.

Once a television network declares a winner, uncertainty begins to look like reversal.

The first completed narrative establishes reality. Everything afterward must fight its way back through that frame.

The election did not create partisan media. It arrived when cable news was developing more distinct identities and audiences. Through the Bush presidency, September 11, Afghanistan, Iraq, talk radio, and the expanding culture wars, the audience sorting became easier to see.

By 2004, Pew found that Fox News had a more Republican and conservative audience, while CNN’s audience leaned more Democratic. By 2009, the party differences had become more pronounced.

This did not mean every Fox viewer was Republican or every CNN viewer was Democratic.

It meant political identity had become an increasingly useful market segment.

A network could select hosts, stories, vocabulary, villains, experts, and emotional tones that appealed to an audience it understood.

The audience could tune in to learn what had happened.

It could also tune in to learn why its side was right about what had happened.

## Everyone Tries to Outfox Everyone

In 2004, Robert Greenwald released *Outfoxed: Rupert Murdoch’s War on Journalism*.

The documentary attacked Fox News’s claim to be “Fair and Balanced.” It used interviews with former employees, internal editorial memos, repeated phrases, guest selection, and examples of framing to argue that Fox operated as a conservative political project disguised as neutral news.

The documentary exposed an agenda.

It also had one.

*Outfoxed* was supported and distributed through an emerging progressive media network. MoveOn organized thousands of house-party screenings and used the film as an organizing tool. The documentary went directly to viewers through DVDs, online sales, political groups, and activist networks rather than relying on a conventional theatrical release.

This does not invalidate its reporting on Fox.

It proves the point.

Fox had an agenda.

The filmmakers had an agenda.

MoveOn had an agenda.

The former employees had reasons to speak.

The current employees had reasons not to.

The audience had a reason to watch a documentary confirming that the other audience was being manipulated.

By 2004, media criticism had become another media product operating inside the same attention economy it criticized.

Fox had learned how to build a profitable audience by telling viewers that conventional news institutions were lying to them.

Its critics built audiences by explaining how Fox was lying to those viewers.

Everybody was trying to outfox everybody else.

The disagreement was no longer only about what had happened.

It was about which machine manufacturing reality could be trusted to describe the other machines manufacturing reality.

This was still before every person had a network in their pocket.

## 2011: The Web Still Had Exits

By 2011, the open web had changed substantially.

Facebook, Twitter, Tumblr, Reddit, YouTube, and other social platforms were becoming central destinations. Recommendation systems existed. Ranking existed. Viral material existed. Brands, campaigns, creators, publishers, and ordinary users were learning how attention behaved online.

The older architecture had not disappeared.

Reddit still functioned strongly as a gateway to material hosted elsewhere. Users submitted links, communities discussed them, and much of the value came from leaving Reddit to visit an article, image, project, store, video, or independent website.

A later longitudinal study described Reddit’s gradual movement from a gateway to the wider web toward a more self-referential system dominated by its own hosted images, text, and internal culture.

StumbleUpon offered another form of algorithmic discovery.

Users chose interests and pressed a button. The service recommended a webpage and sent them directly to it. The recommendation could be strange, inaccurate, delightful, stupid, or forgettable.

The recommendation was an exit.

The service benefited from learning what users liked, but the destination remained somebody else’s website.

That distinction matters.

The problem was never that a computer suggested something.

The problem emerged as recommendation combined with enclosure.

The platform became the destination.

The article became a screenshot.

The website became a preview card.

The video played inside the feed.

The source moved one more click away.

Then it disappeared.

## The Feed Eats the Web

A modern feed does not begin by asking where you want to go.

It begins by deciding what should arrive next.

Meta openly describes Feed ranking as a machine-learning system needed because there is more available content than any person could browse manually. YouTube’s recommendation systems use viewing history, clicks, watch time, likes, dislikes, shares, survey responses, and other signals to predict what a viewer will find valuable or satisfying.

The platform does not need a line of code reading:

```text
PROMOTE BULLSHIT = TRUE
```

It needs a prediction:

Will this person continue looking?

Human beings perform the optimization.

Creators learn which headlines get clicks.

Publishers learn which subjects retain readers.

Political accounts learn which enemies produce replies.

Reaction channels learn which facial expression belongs in the thumbnail.

Engagement farmers learn that an uncertain event performs better once it has a villain.

Research has repeatedly found that false information can spread farther and faster than true information online. Other research has found that misinformation sources evoke more outrage than trustworthy sources, and that outrage increases the chance of material being shared, sometimes without the person opening or reading it first.

“Did you see this shit?” is a perfect distribution protocol.

It contains urgency, emotion, social obligation, and almost no information.

The person receiving it does not need to understand the event.

They only need to recognize what they are supposed to feel.

## Real Footage Wearing Synthetic Context

AI generated media is an obvious kind of unreality.

A synthetic image has no original camera event. A generated voice may belong to nobody. A fake witness can describe something that never occurred.

The harder problem is often real media.

A police bodycam records a real encounter.

A court camera records a real proceeding.

A traffic camera records a real collision.

A news crew records a real protest.

A military records a real strike.

A phone records a real accident.

A broadcaster records a real speech.

The recording enters the internet.

Someone extracts 45 seconds.

They remove what happened before it.

They remove what happened after it.

They crop out the location.

They translate the dialogue.

Another person rewrites the translation.

An account attaches a motive nobody established.

A commentator adds a conclusion.

A publisher describes the reaction to the clip.

Another account clips the publisher.

The clip becomes proof of the narrative built around the clip.

Out-of-context images and footage are among the simplest and most effective forms of misinformation because the underlying media is authentic. Researchers describe these as “cheapfakes”: genuine material paired with a false caption, wrong date, different location, invented identity, or misleading story.

The pixels pass inspection because the pixels are real.

The lie is in the relationship between the pixels and the sentence.

This is the mechanism that the Russian accident clip exposed for me.

A real event occurred.

Someone recorded it.

Local reporting existed in another language.

The footage moved through accounts and countries.

The people were misidentified.

Their motive was invented.

The actions of rescuers were reframed.

A tragedy acquired a portable moral lesson designed for engagement.

Most viewers were never going to search Russian-language local reporting, translate it, compare dates, inspect the location, or reconstruct the repost chain.

They saw it.

They said, “Oh shit.”

They moved on.

The original event did not need to be caused by internet points.

The internet could still convert it into internet points afterward.

Now apply that process to every recorded thing.

A candidate speaks for an hour. The public receives the worst 30 seconds.

A police officer’s bodycam records 40 minutes. One side circulates the moment force was used. Another circulates the moment immediately before it. Each clip is real. Each clip is recruited into a different story.

A courtroom proceeding lasts three weeks. The feed receives a facial expression.

A war produces thousands of recordings from soldiers, civilians, governments, journalists, drones, propagandists, aid workers, and anonymous accounts. Old footage is relabeled. New footage is mistranslated. Game footage is presented as combat. Real destruction is called fake. Fake destruction is presented as real.

The camera may have recorded something real.

The surrounding story remains for sale.

## Label the Robot, Ignore the Human

Social platforms have become extremely interested in identifying AI-generated media.

TikTok requires labels for realistic AI-generated images, audio, and video, and it uses creator disclosures, automated detection, invisible watermarks, and C2PA Content Credentials. By July 2026, TikTok said it had labeled more than three billion videos as AI-generated content.

YouTube requires creators to disclose realistic content that was meaningfully altered or synthetically generated. Videos can receive “Made with AI” or related disclosures explaining how the content was produced.

Meta has also developed labels and provenance systems for AI-generated material across Facebook, Instagram, and Threads.

The effort makes sense. Synthetic media can impersonate people, fabricate evidence, and make events appear to exist without a corresponding event in the world.

But the emphasis produces a strange asymmetry.

The fake image gets a label.

The real video with the false caption does not.

The generated voice receives a warning.

The human pundit selectively quoting a speech receives a salary.

The AI-made photograph of an event that never happened is treated as suspicious.

The human-written headline inventing a motive for an event that did happen is treated as commentary.

A robot that makes a fake pope in a coat leaves technical fingerprints.

A human asshole with an agenda can upload authentic footage, write a false sentence beneath it, and walk through the side door in a necktie.

The origin of a file matters.

The argument built around it matters more.

## Community Notes and the Crowd

X’s Community Notes system is one serious attempt to attach missing context to viral claims.

Contributors write notes, cite sources, and rate whether other notes are helpful. A note becomes broadly visible when contributors who have disagreed in their previous ratings find it useful. X says the notes do not represent the company’s viewpoint and cannot be edited by X’s teams. The system’s algorithm and data are available for public inspection.

This is smarter than asking a simple majority whether a claim is true.

A partisan group cannot necessarily publish a note merely by arriving in large numbers. The system searches for agreement across people who do not usually agree.

Community Notes can work.

Studies have found that visible notes reduce reposts, likes, replies, views, and the later spread of misleading posts. One large 2026 study estimated that displaying a note reduced the spread of affected posts by about 61 percent.

The problem is speed.

A false or misleading post can receive most of its attention before a note is written, sourced, rated, approved, and displayed. Research examining the rollout and operation of Community Notes has repeatedly found that the system can arrive too late for the most viral phase.

The correction reaches the train station.

The train left yesterday.

Meta ended its United States third-party fact-checking program in April 2025 and began replacing it with a Community Notes system across Facebook, Instagram, and Threads. Meta adopted the general X model, requiring agreement among contributors who normally disagree. Notes must include supporting links, and the company initially used X’s open-source algorithm as the basis for its own rating system. Meta presented the change as a way to reduce perceived bias and avoid acting as an “arbiter of truth.”

That explanation is also an agenda.

Professional fact-checking can be inconsistent, selective, ideological, slow, overconfident, or badly applied.

Crowds can be inconsistent, selective, ideological, slow, overconfident, or badly organized.

A platform may prefer crowdsourcing because it distributes responsibility, reduces direct editorial accountability, costs less, and makes accusations of corporate censorship easier to answer.

A platform may also sincerely believe that agreement across opposed users produces better context.

Several motives can be true at once.

Community Notes is not the Ministry of Truth.

It is a potentially useful layer operating inside a platform that still controls distribution.

The platform decides which posts receive millions of views before the community has time to add a footnote.

## Ask Grok

X also places Grok directly inside the platform.

Grok can search current web sources and live material from X. A user can call it into a conversation, ask about a circulating image or claim, request sources, and receive a quick explanation without leaving the post. Grok’s current product emphasizes real-time search, source citations, and answers across more than 30 languages.

That directly addresses part of the problem exposed by the Russian clip.

I may not know what language was originally spoken.

I may not recognize the location.

I may not know which search terms will locate local reporting.

I may have no patience for copying fragments into translation tools, extracting video frames, running reverse-image searches, and digging through foreign-language news sites.

An AI system can do the tedious parts:

transcribe the audio;

identify the language;

translate the dialogue;

extract names, places, dates, and claims;

search across languages;

find earlier copies;

compare captions;

locate reporting near the event;

summarize conflicts between sources.

That is a genuine improvement.

It is also a platform-owned assistant checking the platform that owns it.

X controls the feed, the interface, the available internal data, the rules, and Grok’s integration. The company and its owners have political, commercial, cultural, and reputational interests. Grok can help investigate claims on X, but it cannot become trustworthy merely because X placed the button nearby.

AI systems can also make confident mistakes, accept a false premise, retrieve the wrong event, choose poor sources, or repeat the dominant version because the dominant version is easier to find.

The answer cannot be:

Ask the machine and believe whatever paragraph comes back.

The useful instruction is:

Ask the machine to show its work.

## Official Reality

In June 2026, the United Kingdom proposed a possible “prominence” regime that would make officially recognized or “trusted” news providers more visible on social and video platforms. The government presented the proposal as a response to misinformation and the declining visibility of public-service media. Broadcasters such as the BBC, ITV, and Channel 4 were central examples. The plan remained under consultation rather than enacted law.

The proposal identifies a real problem.

Feeds optimized around attention can bury original reporting beneath reaction, recycled clips, low-quality summaries, propaganda, and material created by accounts with no meaningful standards.

Its answer introduces another problem.

Who qualifies as trustworthy?

What happens to independent reporting?

Does authority come from accuracy, public funding, regulatory status, institutional age, audience size, or government recognition?

Can users turn the preference off?

The British government has not installed the BBC intravenously.

It is consulting on the size of the needle.

Replacing hidden commercial ranking with hidden institutional ranking does not return control to the user.

A major broadcaster may have better reporting resources, access, legal review, archives, professional standards, and correction procedures than an anonymous account.

It also has an institutional agenda.

Authority is a useful signal.

It is not verification.

## The Bullshit Meter

Most people are not going to turn off the internet.

Some will leave particular platforms. Some will subscribe directly to publications, follow trusted writers, use RSS, block recommendations, keep local media collections, or return to smaller communities.

The larger machine will continue.

The practical answer is a verification layer controlled by the person using it rather than the platform distributing the material.

Call it the Bullshit Meter.

It should be a browser extension, mobile share action, or independent service that works on any page, post, image, video, or article.

Click one button.

The system identifies the major claims being made.

It distinguishes what is visible in the recording from what the caption asserts.

It finds the earliest discoverable version.

It extracts video keyframes and searches for previous uses.

It detects the original language.

It generates searches in that language rather than assuming the English-speaking internet contains the answer.

It compares translations.

It looks for the complete speech, bodycam recording, court session, article, livestream, or document from which the fragment was taken.

It identifies whether the footage has been cropped, shortened, rearranged, or reused.

It checks whether reputable fact-checkers have already examined the claim.

It searches mainstream, local, independent, official, archival, and foreign-language sources.

It reports disagreements rather than hiding them.

It tells the user:

**Original source:** Found, possible match, or not found.

**Caption:** Supported, partly supported, contradicted, or unresolved.

**Media history:** Original, copied, cropped, translated, edited, or previously used elsewhere.

**Missing context:** Date, location, speaker, full recording, preceding events, following events, or motive.

**Evidence quality:** Primary source, local reporting, secondary reporting, official statement, partisan commentary, social repost, anonymous claim, or unknown.

**Translation drift:** Which names, actions, motives, or conclusions changed between versions.

**Receipts:** Links, timestamps, quoted passages, matching frames, archived pages, and source history.

It should not return a giant green light reading TRUE or a giant red light reading BULLSHIT.

That would create another authority machine.

It should return an evidence report.

The dial can still shake dramatically for effect.

This tool would use AI without asking AI to become reality’s new owner.

AI performs the work most people will never perform manually:

translation;

transcription;

search-query generation;

source discovery;

timeline reconstruction;

duplicate detection;

claim extraction;

comparison;

summarization.

The user receives the receipts.

A Community Note may eventually appear on one platform.

The Bullshit Meter follows the person across all of them.

Grok may inspect X.

The Bullshit Meter inspects Grok.

It inspects Fox.

It inspects CNN.

It inspects the BBC.

It inspects an independent journalist.

It inspects this article.

My agenda is for it to survive.

## Vacation Into Nothing

In 1898, publishers competed to place the most compelling story around an explosion.

Radio made stories simultaneous.

Military radio showed how news, entertainment, censorship, and morale could share one microphone.

Television gave them faces.

ABC discovered that conflict itself could be a program.

Watergate demonstrated that journalism could still assemble evidence powerful enough to bring down a president.

*Network* showed how television could turn authentic rage into a profitable format.

CNN created twenty-four hours of rectangle.

Fox and MSNBC learned to serve increasingly identifiable audiences.

The 2000 election showed television declaring reality before reality had finished loading.

*Outfoxed* exposed one machine while becoming part of another.

Social media gave everyone a press, clipping desk, commentary program, audience, and ratings dashboard.

A boy holding a photograph became a sick-aunt scam before anyone could locate the original event.

Algorithms automated distribution.

AI made production nearly free.

At each stage, the machinery became faster.

The event remained inconveniently complicated.

In 1996, Yahoo presented categories.
