I found the racetrack at Farmers Market about eighty years after somebody removed it.

I had been to the Original Farmers Market at Third and Fairfax plenty of times. I knew the clock tower, the narrow aisles, the food stalls, the tourists, Television City across the way, and The Grove next door pretending to be an old neighborhood despite arriving in the twenty-first century with its own trolley and a parking structure the size of a minor principality. What I did not know, until I stopped to read one of the historical displays, was that the place had once been surrounded by an oil field, a baseball park, a drive-in theater, an enormous exhibition hall, and a stadium where tiny open-wheel cars ran a dirt oval in front of packed grandstands.

I apparently photographed every strange machine I came across in San Diego and somehow took no picture of the sign that detonated this article. That feels appropriate. Southern California's vanished car culture survives in the same scattered way: a caption at Farmers Market, a race scene in an old movie, a street name, a patch of asphalt, a museum label, a wreck photograph, a grandstand sitting alone in a newspaper archive.

The missing stadium was Gilmore Stadium. It opened in 1934, the same year as Farmers Market, and midget-car racing was its main attraction.[^1] The word *midget* is the historical name of the small open-wheel racing class, and it appears throughout the period record. Gilmore also hosted football, soccer, cricket, motorcycle events, boxing, rodeos, and whatever else could fill seats. In one photograph the place is a glowing bowl of race cars under floodlights. In another, cricketers stand on its infield. In another, a driver climbs the wooden grandstand while a second car passes underneath. By January 1951, stadium manager Gene Doyle was photographed alone in the seats, looking across a field already being dismantled.

A year later, television occupied the parcel.

That single block offers a useful way into the larger story. Southern California once had enough land, mechanical skill, sunlight, oil, military industry, open desert, and cultural permission to build loud public experiments. Cars raced on wood, dirt, dry lakebeds, airport runways, purpose-built road courses, drag strips, city streets, and dangerous little intersections designed to produce collisions for a crowd. When the land became more valuable, the track usually lost.

The tracks disappeared. The culture escaped.

## Before California built racetracks out of wood, it built the road out of wood

The automobile arrived in Southern California before Southern California had finished becoming a place an automobile could cross.

In 1912, San Diego road promoter Ed Fletcher used a publicity race to argue for a direct route east through the Imperial Valley toward Yuma. Los Angeles already had the advantage in rail connections. San Diego wanted an automobile corridor that could help make the city a western terminus rather than a coastal dead end. The problem was the Imperial Sand Dunes, where an ordinary road could vanish under moving sand.[^2]

The first solution looked like a railway designed by somebody who had misplaced the train. In 1915, workers laid two narrow plank tracks across roughly six and a half miles of dunes. Drivers had to keep their wheels aligned on the boards. In 1916, the state replaced that arrangement with an eight-foot-wide sectional wooden road, complete with turnouts so vehicles traveling in opposite directions could negotiate who had the stronger desire to reach Yuma.[^2]

Crews fought the sand until a paved replacement opened in 1926. The corridor belongs to the lineage that became U.S. 80 and, later, the Interstate 8 route between San Diego, Imperial County, Yuma, and the roads toward Phoenix. It was not the exact modern freeway alignment in every detail. It was the same regional argument rendered in lumber: Southern California would connect itself by car, even if it had to lay the road on top of the desert one section at a time.

I photographed a surviving display at the San Diego Automotive Museum in 2019. A Model T sits on preserved planks. Nearby, a huge historical image shows the road wandering into white dunes. The museum turns the thing into a tidy exhibit. The archive photographs show what it became in the landscape: blackened wooden sections, rails bent by heat and time, boards half swallowed by sand.

One of the Coachella Valley Water District photographs records the remnants around 1969. Another shows three men standing beside a disabled car during a 1922 trip toward the Salton Sea. The original caption ends with a line that could serve as the motto of early motoring: “Age 37 on return ought to have known better.”

That trip belongs to a larger Salton Sea story, so this article only borrows it long enough to establish the scale of the problem. Before the highway was ordinary, simply getting a car across this region could become an expedition.

## The toothpick speedways

Once roads made the car practical, speedways made it public theater.

The Los Angeles Motordrome opened at Playa del Rey in 1910. It was a one-mile wooden track, smooth enough for high speed and comparatively inexpensive to build, but the boards decayed and demanded constant replacement.[^3] Newspaper and magazine photographs make the structure look less like a stadium than a giant timber machine. The banking rises out of a forest of supports. The audience sits beside a surface made from thousands of individual failure points.

The term “murderdrome” was not invented by a modern writer trying to make the period sound exciting. It was contemporary language for the early motorcycle board tracks, where steep banking, unreliable machines, minimal protection, and spectators placed close to the action could turn a race into a mass-casualty event.[^4]

The Beverly Hills Speedway opened in 1920 on land that now belongs to one of the most expensive commercial districts in the country. The surviving 1921 film shows motorcycles climbing banking that reached roughly sixty degrees. Otto Walker and Albert “Shrimp” Burns appear in the footage. Burns had already crashed during practice, spent the night in a hospital, returned with injured hands, and set a 102.5 mph competition record on a stock motorcycle.[^4]

That sentence contains an entire vanished standard of acceptable behavior.

Beverly Hills lasted only four years. The land became too valuable. Racing moved to Culver City, where a new board track opened in 1924. More than 70,000 people reportedly attended its first race. Winner Bennett Hill averaged about 127 mph. By 1927, qualifying speeds had climbed higher, maintenance costs remained brutal, and the property was being prepared for housing and a park.[^5]

The pattern had already formed:

> Build the track on cheap land at the edge of town. Attract a crowd. Attract businesses. Let the city reach it. Discover that the dirt is worth more than the noise. Remove the track. Repeat farther outward.

Southern California kept performing this operation for the rest of the century.

## The block that contained a whole Los Angeles

Third and Fairfax did not jump from racetrack to Grove. The actual sequence is better.

The Gilmore family acquired the property as a dairy farm in the nineteenth century. Oil was discovered around 1900. The land became part farm, part oil field, and part fuel business, which placed it inside the automobile economy before the stadium existed.[^1]

Farmers Market and Gilmore Stadium opened in 1934. Gilmore Field followed in 1939 as the home of the Hollywood Stars baseball club. The Gilmore Drive-In opened in 1948 on approximately the parcel later used by The Grove. The nearby Pan-Pacific Auditorium, which opened in 1935, added exhibition halls, sports, concerts, ice shows, car events, and one of the great Streamline Moderne facades in Los Angeles.[^1][^6]

This cluster was sometimes described as “Gilmore Island,” an entertainment district surrounded by a city still assembling itself.[^7] It contained racing, baseball, food, exhibitions, film-adjacent celebrity culture, gasoline, radio, and, eventually, television. It was not a single-use development. It was a loose urban machine built from whatever people would leave the house to see.

A modern visitor can still orient themselves because Farmers Market survived. Nearly everything else changed layer.

Gilmore Stadium occupied the parcel later acquired for CBS Television City. Gilmore Field remained a little longer, until the Hollywood Stars’ arrangement ended and the ballpark was demolished in 1958. The drive-in occupied the future Grove area. The Pan-Pacific stood nearby across Beverly Boulevard, closed in 1972, decayed for years, appeared as the transformed roller-disco exterior in *Xanadu*, and burned in 1989.[^6]

The Grove is part of this history, but it is not the simple answer to “what replaced the racetrack?” Television did that.

That distinction matters because the replacement was culturally logical. Gilmore Stadium gathered thousands of people into one place to watch a spectacle. Television City gathered performers and technicians into a controlled building so millions of people could watch spectacle at home.

One entertainment system ate another.

## What Gilmore Stadium was actually for

Gilmore was built around racing, but the photographs prevent it from becoming a single-note nostalgia object.

A 1941 night image shows the track crowded with cars and the grandstands filled under stadium lights. The surface is dark with tire marks. The tiny machines look almost comic until the crash photographs appear.

On December 14, 1947, Ed Haddad’s car climbed into the wooden seating structure while Johnny McDowell drove past below. In a 1950 three-car wreck, Duane Carter’s number 51 rides over Jim Davis while Manuel Ayulo’s car turns beside them. The images are ugly, immediate, and strangely formal. The photographer caught the machines at the exact moment they stopped behaving like vehicles and became loose industrial parts.

The venue could change personality completely. Soccer was played there in 1942. Cricket appeared in 1945. Professional football advertisements sent the Los Angeles Mustangs and Oakland Giants to the same field. These were not novelty additions to a sacred racetrack. This was one of the city’s available public rooms.

Modern Southern California has many larger, safer, more specialized facilities. That specialization also makes places easier to replace when their single economic purpose weakens. Gilmore could host a race, a football game, a boxing card, a rodeo, or cricket because it was a flexible bowl with seats and lights. The roughness was part of the utility.

The final images have a different weight. Gene Doyle sits alone in the grandstands in January 1951. Another photograph shows the seating structure being pulled apart. The field remains visible while the architecture turns back into lumber.

CBS had purchased the approximately fifteen-acre stadium parcel in 1950. Groundbreaking began late that year, and demolition continued as Television City rose. The first broadcast from the new complex occurred in October 1952, followed by an official dedication in November.[^7]

The stadium’s final season, the 1951 demolition photographs, and Television City’s 1952 opening are all true. The dates only look contradictory when compressed into one caption.

## The postwar speed machine

Gilmore explains where the crowd gathered. The dry lakes explain where the builders learned.

Southern California hot rodding did not begin with a shopping list of chrome parts. It grew from used cars, home garages, engine work, clubs, desert space, and a regional manufacturing economy that taught large numbers of people how machines could be altered rather than merely purchased.

The Southern California Timing Association formed in 1937 to organize dry-lake speed trials and improve safety at places such as El Mirage.[^8] In 1948, an SCTA show at the Los Angeles Armory displayed competition cars and the work of the region’s speed shops. The first issue of *Hot Rod* appeared that January.[^9] Wally Parks later founded the National Hot Rod Association in 1951 with the explicit goal of giving competitors organized places to race.[^10]

This history is often summarized as teenage rebellion, which misses the amount of institution-building involved. Clubs wrote rules. Timing associations measured results. Magazines transmitted designs. Speed shops made specialized parts. Tracks converted informal competition into scheduled events. The culture became durable because people built organizations around the machines.

Lions Drag Strip opened in Wilmington in 1955. Its first event drew roughly 10,000 people and 400 cars, according to the Lions Automobilia Foundation. The strip was connected to local Lions Club charity work and offered an organized alternative to street racing. It closed in 1972 after years of noise complaints and pressure from its surroundings.[^11]

Orange County International Raceway opened in 1967 as a deliberately professional “super track,” with land leased from the Irvine Company. The contradiction was embedded in the project: the lease made racing possible, while the owner’s long-term development plans ensured the land would eventually return to another use. OCIR closed in 1983.[^12]

The museum photographs from San Diego include a 1946 Solar midget racer. The label identifies Solar Aircraft Company as its builder, making the car an unusually literal artifact of the bridge between aircraft manufacturing and racing. The object makes the larger point without a production count. Aerospace knowledge did not stay inside aircraft.

## Disney sunshine and figure-eight hell

Two films made at the end of the 1960s preserve opposite faces of the same regional ecosystem.

*The Love Bug* turns automobile competition into a Disney fantasy. Herbie has moods, loyalties, comic timing, and better instincts than several human characters. The racing world is sunny and legible. Cars line up. Officials wave flags. Crowds understand what is happening. Crashes are physical jokes. California becomes a sequence of roads and tracks that seem to exist for adventure.

Disney’s own production history identifies filming in San Francisco, at Willow Springs, at Riverside Raceway, and around the Monterey Peninsula.[^13] The National Park Service also identifies Paramount Raceway as a location and notes that much of that short-lived course remains traceable at Paramount Ranch.[^14]

The film was not trying to preserve a land-use record. That is why the backgrounds matter. Grandstands, hillsides, pit areas, access roads, and course geometry appear as ordinary production material. A modern viewer sees a cheerful Volkswagen. A location historian sees infrastructure that has vanished or changed beyond recognition.

Jack Hill’s *Pit Stop* uses the same regional obsession without Disney protection. AFI’s production history says Hill chose figure-eight racing at Ascot Park near Gardena and photographed the film around Los Angeles in 1967.[^15] The movie is full of damaged stock cars, dirt, improvised machinery, promoters, hunger, and the knowledge that the fastest route through an intersection may also be the route into somebody else.

The pairing works because neither film is the complete truth.

*The Love Bug* shows the dream sold back to families: California as a sunny machine for freedom, competition, and personality.

*Pit Stop* shows the machinery underneath: drivers who need money, cars assembled to be destroyed, crowds waiting for impact, and a culture close enough to the industrial edge that danger still looks affordable.

Together they preserve a spectrum of late-1960s Southern California racing, from polished studio fantasy to figure-eight hell.

## The land is always worth more later

A racetrack occupies a lot of land while refusing to behave like the highest possible use of that land.

That sentence explains more closures than any theory about Southern California losing interest in cars.

Riverside International Raceway opened in 1957 and became one of the region’s major road courses. It hosted sports cars, NASCAR, Formula One, off-road events, testing, driving schools, and film production. Its final major NASCAR weekend came in 1988, and the course closed completely in 1989. The former footprint is now divided among the Moreno Valley Mall, other retail, hotels, housing, schools, parks, roads, and offices.[^16]

Ontario Motor Speedway opened in 1970 as a grand western answer to Indianapolis. No races were held after 1980. Chevron bought the property that December, and the site became part of a large office, retail, residential, arena, school, and park district.[^17]

Ascot Park lasted longer, from 1957 until 1990, and hosted thousands of events across dirt-track racing, figure-eight programs, motorcycles, sprint cars, and television production.[^18] Its closure removed the exact rough public landscape that *Pit Stop* had used as raw material.

Irwindale Speedway looked like a late correction to the pattern when it opened in 1999. It hosted stock cars, drag racing, drifting, demolition derbies, festivals, and community events. Its final program took place on December 21, 2024.[^19] Even a track built near the turn of the twenty-first century could not escape the old equation forever.

The simple version says developers killed the racetracks. The useful version says the tracks were often built as temporary occupants of land whose future value was already visible. Racing benefited from that cheap edge condition. Development later collected the difference.

The sadness comes from what the replacement usually cannot reproduce. A mall can sell racing merchandise. A condo lobby can hang a historical photograph. A business park can name a street after a driver. None of those things gives somebody a place to build a questionable machine, bring it to a crowd, discover what breaks, talk to the person in the next pit, and try again next weekend.

## The culture gets out

If racing were the only thing holding Southern California car culture together, the culture would have died with the tracks.

It did not.

The skills moved into garages, speed shops, body shops, upholstery shops, clubs, dry-lake meets, street cruising, shows, museums, industrial parking lots, fairgrounds, and swap meets. The car became competition, sculpture, family project, neighborhood signal, business card, rolling archive, and a reason to spend Saturday discussing a part no normal store had stocked for forty years.

That is why lowriding belongs in this article without being forced into a racing genealogy.

Lowriding developed through Mexican American and Chicano communities across Southern California and the borderlands, with distinct histories of style, club organization, cruising, cultural pride, resistance, activism, family, ritual, paint, chrome, upholstery, music, and community service.[^20] The San Diego Lowrider Council formed in 1979 to build unity among clubs and barrios and became connected to Chicano Park, anti-cruising struggles, and community work.[^21]

The shared regional infrastructure is real: old American cars, fabrication knowledge, body shops, roads, parts networks, clubs, and public gathering space. The purpose is different. A drag car is organized around elapsed time. A lowrider can turn the entire automobile into a moving room, mural, family record, mechanical performance, and social presence.

My photographs of a purple 1979 Monte Carlo called *La Muerte* show why one exterior image is not enough. The paint and striping carry one part of the design. The tufted purple interior carries another. The chrome suspension and hydraulic hardware underneath are meant to be seen. The car is not finished at the body line.

The museum label includes a personal biography and community work. That story should only be reproduced if the owner’s permission and preferred identification are clear. The car can already support the article as an object of craft. The person does not become public-domain copy because a museum printed a panel.

## The parking lot becomes an institution

In February 2013, I went to the Big 3 Parts Exchange at Qualcomm Stadium in San Diego. The photographs look ordinary at first: tents, folding tables, trailers, old trucks, custom cars, loose parts, coolers, camp chairs, and people standing around in a parking lot.

That ordinary appearance hides the function.

A large swap meet is a distributed warehouse for obsolete machines. It is also a search engine with human memory. The person selling a pile of brackets may know which one fits a particular model year, which reproduction is junk, what can be modified, who still rebuilds the original part, and which vendor two rows over has the piece that disappeared from catalogs in 1978.

Contemporary accounts describe the Big 3 as a huge annual exchange where vendors camped, cooked, traded, and returned year after year.[^22] Event notices confirm the February 22 through 24, 2013 dates at Qualcomm Stadium.[^23]

The purpose-built raceway had become harder to preserve. The car culture could still temporarily occupy somebody else’s pavement.

That is one reason parking-lot shows, swap meets, cruises, and temporary events matter. They create short-lived public territory for a culture that no longer controls much permanent land.

## The Milwaukee motorcycle built by Hollywood

Motorcycles belong in the larger Southern California machine too, especially where racing, shops, stunts, and screen culture overlap.

The 1949 Triumph used for Fonzie on *Happy Days* seems geographically wrong for this article. The show is set in Milwaukee. The connection is Bud Ekins, the Southern California motorcycle racer, dealer, customizer, and stunt performer who supplied and modified the bike.[^24]

Ekins connects desert competition to Hollywood in a single career. He raced motorcycles, operated dealerships around Los Angeles, and supplied the practical knowledge required when film and television wanted a machine to perform character. The motorcycle’s fictional home was Wisconsin. Its screen identity passed through a Southern California workshop.

This deserves a sidebar, not a detour through every television vehicle ever built.

## The tracks that figured out how to survive

Not every course became a mall.

El Mirage remains useful because the lakebed is the course. The racing organization arrives, measures, marks, governs, and leaves. The landscape does not require a permanent grandstand district.[^8]

Willow Springs opened in 1953 in the high desert and still runs its original 2.5-mile, nine-turn road-course configuration. The track describes itself as the oldest permanent road course in the United States operating in its original form.[^25] Its distance from the most valuable coastal and inner-metropolitan land helped preserve the thing that made it valuable as a track.

Paramount Raceway survives differently. The course operated for only about eighteen months after opening in 1956, but much of its trace remains visible on National Park Service land at Paramount Ranch.[^14] It is an archaeological survivor, a racetrack that stopped functioning before the landscape could completely forget its shape.

Long Beach solved the land problem by declining to own the land permanently. The first Grand Prix was a Formula 5000 event in 1975. Formula One arrived in 1976. The race shifted to CART in 1984 and continued through later American open-wheel eras.[^26] The circuit borrows city streets, builds a temporary racing city, then gives the streets back.

That model is disruptive, expensive, and dependent on civic cooperation. It also avoids leaving hundreds of acres idle between events while developers calculate what else could be built there.

The survivors reveal the new rule. Racing can persist where the land is remote, public, temporary, historically protected, or flexible enough to perform another function the rest of the year.

## Pan-Pacific after the party

The Pan-Pacific Auditorium deserves its own article, which is usually a sign that it belongs in this one only long enough to explain the neighborhood.

Its four green-and-white Streamline Moderne pylons announced a building designed for spectacle before anyone entered. It hosted exhibitions, sports, ice events, concerts, political gatherings, and automobile shows. After closing in 1972, it sat in a long decay that made it useful to filmmakers as a structure already halfway into fantasy.[^6]

In *Xanadu*, the facade becomes the exterior of an impossible roller disco. The transformation works because the building already looked like transportation from a future imagined in the 1930s.

I visited the park long before I knew what had stood there. That is the recurring experience behind this whole article. The current place does not necessarily announce the missing one. Knowledge changes the walk after the fact.

The facade itself supplies the comparison. A Historic American Buildings Survey photograph preserves the building’s real architecture before *Xanadu* turned it into screen fantasy.[^27]

## What replaced what replaced the racetrack?

The obvious ending would blame everything on malls.

Riverside became, in part, a mall. Other tracks became offices, houses, warehouses, parks, schools, arenas, industrial yards, and commercial districts. The Grove is an easy symbol because it is polished, expensive, controlled, and staged to look older than the history it replaced nearby.

The problem is that the mall is not the final form either.

Across the country, retail properties that looked permanent in the 1990s and early 2000s now sit half empty or wait for another round of conversion. A local mall can receive a vast renovation, lose its stores twenty years later, and become a real-estate problem again. The structure that replaced the strange old use turns out to be another temporary occupant.

That deserves a separate article because it changes the question.

The first question is: What replaced the racetrack?

The next one is: What replaces what replaced it?

Housing is the default answer in expensive regions. Logistics space, medical offices, churches, mixed-use projects, schools, studios, and demolition all compete for the rest. The exact future depends on the parcel. The recurring mechanism does not. American land keeps being treated as a sequence of temporary revenue models, while cultural memory gets reduced to a plaque near the parking entrance.

A racetrack is not automatically sacred. It is loud, dangerous, polluting, expensive, and hostile to nearby residents who did not agree to spend every weekend listening to it. Some tracks deserved stronger safety systems. Some were speculative land deals wearing racing numbers. Some replacements supplied homes, schools, parks, and jobs.

The useful question is what a region loses when every unusual public use must compete against the maximum financial yield of the land beneath it.

Southern California produced its car cultures because people had room to experiment in public. They could gather, compare, fail, rebuild, race, cruise, paint, trade, teach, and invent. The region still contains extraordinary car culture. Much of it now survives farther away, for fewer hours, on borrowed pavement, behind museum ropes, inside private garages, or through images from places that no longer exist.

I lived around Southern California from 2006 to 2014, in Los Angeles, San Diego, Santa Ana, Corona, and Salton City. I knew the region after most of the famous tracks were already gone. Their afterimages were everywhere once I learned how to look: in movies, old signs, museum displays, street layouts, custom cars, deserted parcels, and the parts laid out on asphalt at Qualcomm Stadium.

That is the real job of the archive. It does not restore the track. It restores the ability to see the current place as one temporary layer.

Southern California did not stop loving cars.

It kept finding smaller, farther, and more temporary places to love them.

## A compact field guide

### Sidebar: The Gilmore demolition dates are not contradictory

- 1950: CBS acquired the stadium parcel and work began around the end of the year.
- 1951: newspaper photographers recorded the empty stadium and demolition.
- 1952: Television City began broadcasting and held its official dedication.
- 1958: Gilmore Field, the neighboring baseball park, was demolished after its separate use ended.

Source: Los Angeles Conservancy Television City historic assessment.[^7]

### Sidebar: Three overlapping car-culture impulses

| Impulse | Public forms | Main question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | Dry lakes, midgets, drag strips, road racing, street circuits | How fast can it go? |
| Construction and style | Hot rods, customs, paint, upholstery, chopped bodies, show cars | How completely can it be rebuilt? |
| Identity and gathering | Lowriders, clubs, cruising, swap meets, neighborhood shows | What can the car carry about the people around it? |

The categories overlap. They are not an ethnic or mechanical taxonomy.
