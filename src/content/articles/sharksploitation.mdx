## You should write about sharks

Las Vegas is a useful place to think about sharks because the nearest ocean is a long drive away and the city keeps sharks anyway.

At Mandalay Bay, visitors move through an aquarium built around a 1.3-million-gallon tank. Aquarists control the food, medicine, water, and encounter. The shark is real. The shipwreck is a set. The threat is acrylic away.[^vegas-sharks]

People hand me subjects more often than ways to reach the people who might read them. Someone said I should write about sharks. Discovery already has Shark Week.

Sharks are a topic. The article begins when the shark reveals a machine.

The machine begins with a thing that exists outside it. Something frightening happens. News supplies a pattern. A cheap production discovers the image. An audience proves the image works. A larger company turns the proof into a method. The method becomes an empire. The empire becomes expensive enough to fear its own experiments. Then a small production appears outside the walls and tests the old appetite again.

The shark never changes jobs. It remains an animal moving through water.

## The thing

Sharks can bite people. That fact is enough to start the machinery, but it is not enough to explain every bite.

A bear, wolf, or large cat occupies ground a person can see. The mind invents a tree, a door, a fence, a weapon, or a direction to run. Water removes those comforts. A swimmer sees less, moves slowly, cannot breathe normally, and may never know what approached from outside the visible frame.

The International Shark Attack File separates incidents by circumstance and behavior. A quick strike in low visibility may involve mistaken identity. A bump-and-bite encounter can be investigative, antagonistic, or related to feeding. Predatory incidents occur, but they are infrequent. “The shark was curious” is not a universal explanation. Neither is “the shark hunted a human.” Often the honest answer is that the animal left no statement.[^bite-types]

One bite may answer a question for the shark. The question can be catastrophic for the human body.

Film hates that answer.

Film wants intention. It wants the fin to mean the shark has chosen someone.

## Panic gets a calendar

In New Jersey in 1916, five shark incidents occurred in twelve days. Charles Vansant died after an attack at Beach Haven on July 1. Charles Bruder died at Spring Lake on July 6. On July 12, a shark entered Matawan Creek, killed Lester Stillwell, fatally injured Stanley Fisher during the attempted rescue, and seriously injured Joseph Dunn, who survived. Four people died. One lived.[^jersey]

The geography made the story worse. The danger did not remain offshore. It entered a creek. Resort business, bathers, newspapers, local officials, scientists, and armed shark hunters all acquired parts. The species and even the number of animals responsible remain disputed. The certainty came from the headlines, not the water.

<SharksploitationFigure />

The attacks became part of the cultural weather around sharks, but they should not be lazily promoted into the single inspiration for *Jaws*. Peter Benchley’s later account points to a 1964 newspaper item about fisherman Frank Mundus landing a 4,500-pound shark. The 1916 panic is an ancestor of the story’s atmosphere. The documented seed for Benchley’s premise is another clipping.[^jaws-origin]

The lesson is not that newspapers invented the animal. It is that panic gave the animal a role.

## Educational, adults only

The machine existed before television commercials and summer blockbusters.

Kroger Babb’s 1945 roadshow film *Mom and Dad* packaged sex education, childbirth footage, venereal-disease warnings, censorship fights, live lecturers, and lobby sales into a traveling event. It reportedly cost under $65,000. At its peak, as many as sixteen units worked different territories. The film’s rental income reached an estimated $16 million.[^mom-dad]

Babb was not selling a print. He was selling the argument around the print: adults only, important information, somebody may try to stop you, a lecturer will explain what respectable venues will not.

That is exploitation as a delivery system. The audience buys admission to the controversy promised by the advertising.

## Shark sentenced in voice-over

*Mondo Cane* made that delivery system international in 1962. The film travels the world collecting practices, bodies, animals, rituals, jokes, cruelties, and alleged customs under a voice that sounds composed enough to turn arrangement into evidence. It helped establish the “shockumentary”: documentary authority joined to exploitation rhythm.[^mondo-history]

Its shark sequence shows how the transfer works.

The English-language narration first claims that people on unspecified Malay islands bury their dead underwater, where sharks consume the bodies and learn to eat human flesh. It moves to a village it calls Raiputh, says injured fishermen catch sharks and dry the fins for wealthy Chinese buyers, and claims a fisherman disappears every day. Then the voice announces that a twelve-year-old boy has been killed. Men restrain a shark, force what the film calls a poisoned sea urchin into its throat, and return the animal to the sea. The narrator sentences it to a week of suffering.[^mondo-film]

The sequence may contain real fishing, a real shark, and real cruelty. That does not verify the story wrapped around them. I could not find reliable support for the underwater-cemetery explanation, the daily casualty claim, or the medical certainty of the punishment.

The production method does the work. Each shot supplies one usable fragment: bones underwater, beach labor, injured men, drying fins, a captured shark. Montage turns neighboring fragments into a causal chain. The English voice-over supplies the geography, frequency, motive, and sentence. Its own images cannot prove the story the edit has made from them.

The cut also controls time. Reaction shots, close views of tools, and the return to the animal create the impression of one unfolding incident, even when the frame cannot establish when each fragment was filmed or whether every fragment belonged to the same event. Music and narration carry continuity across those gaps. The production does not need to prove its account shot by shot. It only needs the next image to arrive before the audience can inspect the last one.

[Watch or rent *Mondo Cane* through its official YouTube Movies listing.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KPT6RpqhYTU) The relevant sequence begins around 49 minutes; watch how the narration converts adjacent shots into one apparently continuous explanation.

The shark has now crossed a line. It is no longer merely dangerous. It has motive, a criminal record, and a punishment delivered by a voice-over.

## The AIP and Corman machine

American International Pictures learned to build inexpensive movies around audiences the major studios were underserving. The company began as American Releasing Corporation in 1954, aimed directly at teenagers and drive-ins, and sometimes developed a title and poster before commissioning a script. Roger Corman directed more than forty pictures for the company while actors, writers, and craftspeople learned by making movies quickly.[^aip]

The important part is not that every AIP picture followed one formula. It is the industrial inference: when money is scarce, the promise has to become legible early. The title, poster, creature, youth angle, and booking opportunity help decide whether the production can exist.

That method can produce trash. It can also create a cheap place to attempt something the larger system cannot yet justify.

Steven Spielberg was not a Corman protégé. He had directed television at Universal and made *Duel* and *The Sugarland Express*. The next machine was already his employer.

## Universal builds the bigger machine

Universal producers Richard Zanuck and David Brown acquired Benchley’s unpublished *Jaws* in 1973. Spielberg joined after other directors had been considered. The plan was a difficult studio adventure, not a handmade drive-in quickie: real ocean locations around Martha’s Vineyard, a star cast, elaborate mechanical sharks, and a major company trying to put an impossible animal under production control.[^jaws-production]

The control failed. Three mechanical sharks corroded, malfunctioned, and delayed the work. A budget planned around $3 million to $4 million rose to roughly $8 million, with later accounts placing it higher. The broken effect forced the movie to withhold the shark. John Williams’s score, Verna Fields’s editing, the waterline, the barrels, and the reactions carried the threat until the machine could appear.

Universal then made the failure into an event. Television spots ran heavily before release. The film opened in hundreds of theaters rather than moving slowly from city to city. Neither television advertising nor wide release was invented for *Jaws*, but the campaign joined them to a national summer picture with unusual force.[^jaws-business]

*Jaws* taught Hollywood how to turn exploitation into an event.

The low-budget producer asks whether a picture can be sold from a title and an image. Universal asked the same question with national advertising, a bestselling novel, studio logistics, and enough screens to make the answer arrive in one weekend.

## The event becomes an empire

*Star Wars* extended the lesson. Toys, books, records, clothing, lunch boxes, and licensed objects kept the experience running after the audience left the theater. When Kenner could not manufacture figures in time for Christmas 1977, it sold an empty display package with a certificate promising the toys later. The shortage became another product. Merchandise revenue helped support the next films.[^star-wars]

*Jaws* taught Hollywood how to make an event. *Star Wars* taught it how to turn the event into an empire.

The empire eventually learns to exploit the exploitation. Sequels advertise continuity. Reboots advertise recognition. Corporate universes sell the promise that every release matters to another release. Marvel is not the first franchise and does not explain the entire business, but it is an advanced form of the model: character libraries, interlocking stories, release calendars, merchandise, streaming, and theme-park value organized across one portfolio.

Bloat is not simply a long movie or an expensive effect. It is what happens when a production carries so much surrounding value that surprise becomes a liability.

The empire still needs audience proof. It has become too large to seek it cheaply.

## The cheap movie comes back

Curry Barker found proof where the studio was not looking.

He and Cooper Tomlinson built an audience through the That’s a Bad Idea channel, shorts, and sketches. Barker’s 2023 horror short *The Chair* drew industry attention. The feature *Milk & Serial* was made for a reported $800. When conventional distribution did not materialize, they put it on YouTube for free. Viewers did the market test in public.[^barker]

That does not mean Barker personally financed every later step. *Obsession* was independently produced through Under the Shell, Tea Shop Productions, and Capstone, with a reported production budget of about $750,000; one box-office tracker lists $1 million. After its 2025 Toronto premiere, Focus Features acquired it in a reported $14 million to $15 million deal and released it widely in May 2026.

By August 2, the tracker The Numbers reported $262.8 million domestic and $474.9 million worldwide.[^obsession-numbers]

<ObsessionScale />

The worldwide gross is not Barker’s check. Theaters keep a share. The distributor has acquisition, advertising, and release costs. Financiers and participants have contracts the public cannot inspect. No honest profit calculation comes from dividing $474.9 million by $750,000.

The useful number is the audience transition.

An $800 feature could be ignored by distributors and still reach viewers. Those viewers became evidence. Evidence changed Barker’s access to actors, producers, money, festivals, and a studio buyer. A cheap production entered the large system without first asking the large system to predict its audience.

This is not a fairy tale about escaping commerce. *Obsession* became valuable to a corporation. The case for making movies outside the empire is narrower and more practical: a small production can test an idea while failure is still survivable. It can discover an audience before the franchise calendar turns that discovery into a meeting.

## Who actually eats whom

The shark story ends differently when the camera faces the supply chain.

Shark finning has a specific meaning: removing fins at sea and discarding the body. It is not a synonym for every legal shark fishery or every use of a landed animal. The United States banned finning in federal waters in 2000 and later restricted much of the commercial fin trade, with exceptions defined in law.[^finning]

Globally, sharks enter overlapping trades in fins, meat, liver oil, leather, and other products. Hong Kong and Guangzhou have been major fin-market and processing centers, but fleets and consumers are international. Fins can drive value in one market while meat drives fishing pressure elsewhere. The clean villain-country story is another voice-over trying to make a complicated chain easier to sell.[^shark-trade]

The animal that cinema turned into a human hunter is caught, cut, shipped, branded, regulated, and consumed by humans at industrial scale.

## A real shark up north

White sharks are not a historical prop. They move through the same northeastern water that once supplied the 1916 panic.

Maine began an inshore monitoring program after its first recorded fatal shark attack in 2020. In 2025, the Department of Marine Resources deployed thirty-six passive receiver sites from Ogunquit to the Canadian border. The recovered receivers recorded more than 9,500 white-shark detections from nineteen tagged individuals across ninety-five dates.[^maine]

Those numbers are easy to abuse. Two sharks produced about 85 percent of all detections. A receiver hears a compatible tag only when a tagged animal passes within range. More equipment, more active days, and a few repetitive sharks can produce a dramatic count without proving a dramatic population increase.

Farther north, a peer-reviewed study analyzed 260 acoustically tagged white sharks detected in Atlantic Canada from 2014 through 2023. Estimated seasonal presence expanded. Detection probability increased around Halifax and the Cabot Strait, and the monitored season lengthened. The authors describe increasing presence in a region where baseline knowledge remains limited; they do not turn receiver hits into a census.[^atlantic-canada]

<AtlanticSharkMap />

One animal demonstrates both the power and the trap of the new visibility. OCEARCH tagged a mature male called Contender off the Florida–Georgia coast in January 2025. He measured 13 feet 9 inches and an estimated 1,653 pounds. That autumn, his tag transmitted near Cape Breton. He acquired tens of thousands of followers and headlines large enough to make him sound like a weather system.[^contender]

As of August 3, 2026, the public tracker does not honestly place Contender near Nova Scotia. A weak signal can confirm that a tag transmitted without supplying a usable coordinate. “Heading for your beach” is a better headline than “location uncertain.” It is also how monitoring becomes another little exploitation picture: name, scale, map, suspense, repeat.

The research matters. The live thriller is optional.

## Return to Las Vegas

Back in the desert, the sharks at Mandalay Bay move through filtered water while people watch from a manufactured wreck. The casino is not pretending the animals are attacking Nevada. It has done something more revealing. It moved the ocean encounter inland, enclosed it, staffed it, ticketed it, and made the threat safe enough to sell all day.

That is the whole production history in one room.

The thing is real. The frame decides what it means. Panic supplies urgency. Exploitation supplies the promise. The blockbuster supplies the appointment. The franchise supplies the shelf. Bloat protects the shelf. A small filmmaker begins again with a camera, a title, and an audience the empire did not know how to count.

The shark never had to come ashore.

The business kept bringing it inland.
