I was playing one of the stations that had grown out of Donald Byrd when Eugene McDaniels came back around.

The song was “Headless Heroes.” I had heard it before. This was not a discovery story polished into a cleaner shape after the fact. The track came on again, a familiar vocal phrase appeared, and my brain made the same little jump it had made before.

There are the Beastie Boys.

More precisely, there is the piece of Eugene McDaniels that the Beastie Boys used in “Get It Together” with Q-Tip. McDaniels recorded his song in 1971. *Ill Communication* arrived in 1994. History has the order settled. My memory does not.[^mcdaniels]

I knew the later record first. When the older one appeared, part of it sounded like a Beastie Boys song had somehow wandered backward into the seventies.

I think this may have been the first sample connection that made the whole mechanism visible to me. I cannot date that first recognition neatly. I only know that once I started listening farther into the jazz, funk, soul, and fusion records surrounding it, the experience kept happening.

An old song would be playing. A familiar piece would rise out of it. Then another record, made years later, would suddenly be standing in the room.

## The song arrived in the wrong order

Sampling gives a record at least two chronologies.

Historical time is easy. Eugene McDaniels recorded “Headless Heroes.” More than two decades later, the Beastie Boys and their collaborators pulled a small piece into “Get It Together.” Q-Tip added his voice to the new record. The source existed, somebody selected part of it, and the later recording followed.[^ill-communication]

Personal time can run in reverse. The Beastie Boys were already stored in my memory. McDaniels appeared years later. Recognition completed the circuit.

The older recording is still older. I am the part moving backward.

<TwoChronologies />

That reversal changes what a sample credit can do. It stops being trivia underneath a song I already know. It becomes a route into another catalog.

The familiar fragment gets me through the door. Then the rest of the source song begins.

That rest matters. “Headless Heroes” is not a container built to preserve a Beastie Boys vocal. The sampled phrase belongs to a complete Eugene McDaniels composition and a complete album with its own politics, arrangements, musicians, arguments, and historical moment. The record credits Richie Resnikoff on guitar, Harry Whitaker on piano, Gary King and Miroslav Vitous on bass, Alphonse Mouzon on drums, and Joel Dorn as producer.[^mcdaniels]

The later song gave me an address. The older record turned out to be a whole neighborhood.

## Quincy Jones appears by accident

The Quincy Jones connection arrived through a different route.

I already knew the Pharcyde's “Passin' Me By.” Then I was listening to Quincy Jones radio, because that is a normal thing for me to do, and “Summer in the City” came on.

I was not researching the Pharcyde. I was not working through a sample database. I had not asked a streaming service to show me records used in nineties hip-hop. The track simply appeared inside a Quincy Jones trail and the recognition fired on its own.

There it is.

“Passin' Me By” is built from more than one source. The Quincy Jones recording supplies its opening harmonic and vocal atmosphere. The production also draws on other records, including Skull Snaps and Jimi Hendrix. One source relationship does not explain every sound in the later track.[^pharcyde]

That makes the example better.

The later track is not a display case containing one untouched object. Producer J-Swift heard useful parts across recordings, combined them, and made a new environment. My recognition of the Quincy passage opens one route through it.

Years later, I ran lights for the Pharcyde at Brooklyn Bowl Las Vegas.

That detail does not make the sample more important. It makes the timeline stranger. A Quincy Jones record plays at home, my brain jumps to the Pharcyde, and the group at the other end of that connection is also a group I have watched from behind a lighting console.

The records keep folding into my actual life.

## The future gets into the old record

Once the later song is familiar, hearing the source innocently becomes difficult.

The source passage no longer belongs only to the year it was recorded. It has acquired everything that happened to it afterward. I hear Quincy Jones, then I hear the Pharcyde waiting in a piece of music recorded before the group existed.

The later record did not travel through time. My memory is stacking both recordings on the same moment.

That is the part sample lists usually flatten. A database can tell me that Track B used Track A. It cannot reproduce the little perceptual collision of hearing Track A in the wild and realizing that my internal chronology is backward.

The surprise depends on the full song continuing around it. A comparison clip can isolate the evidence. The source recording supplies the room.

## Then Labi Siffre turns into Eminem

Labi Siffre's “I Got The...” gives this experience its clearest broad-recognition example.

The recording begins as its own piece of seventies music and keeps developing. A little after two minutes, the familiar bass, guitar, and keyboard passage arrives. For listeners who knew Eminem first, the reaction can be immediate.

That is “My Name Is.” Except it is Labi Siffre, recorded decades earlier.

The distinction in that note matters. The released Eminem record does not simply drop Siffre's master recording into the track. The liner credit describes an interpolation, meaning the recognizable musical material was replayed for the new recording. Dr. Dre produced it, and Siffre received a writing credit.[^siffre]

It does not need to become a legal seminar. The listening experience is the center.

The Siffre track contains far more than the passage a later audience recognizes. Hearing the setup before that passage gives the familiar figure a different weight. It no longer sounds like the beginning of an Eminem beat. It sounds like one turn inside a longer arrangement that Dr. Dre heard as another possible record.

A later song can make a few seconds of one recording more culturally familiar than the minutes surrounding it. Then somebody eventually walks backward and discovers the rest.

## Bob James is where the map gets crowded

Bob James turns the phenomenon from an occasional surprise into a catalog problem.

For this article, one pair is enough: his 1975 recording of “Take Me to the Mardi Gras” and Run-DMC's “Peter Piper.” James was making an instrumental treatment of a Paul Simon composition. The opening bells and Steve Gadd's drum break became part of the later record's machinery.[^bob-james]

The later track can be so deeply installed in memory that hearing the source feels less like finding an influence and more like locating a machine part after years of seeing the machine run. One song has already moved through several writers, arrangements, records, technologies, and audiences before I reach it.

This is where the article has to stop itself.

Bob James deserves a separate piece. “Nautilus” alone opens several different kinds of usable material, and James has spoken directly about what happened when hip-hop producers found his records.[^bob-james-interview] That is another article: why his seventies recordings behaved like an accidental sample library before anybody was selling sample packs.

Here he serves as the point where one familiar fragment opens a cabinet full of drawers.

## Records already had hyperlinks

Streaming did not invent musical wandering.

A physical record already contained links. The sleeve listed musicians. One musician led to another album. A producer led to a catalog. A label led to a section of the store. A cover song pointed backward. A sample credit pointed backward and forward at the same time. A friend, clerk, radio host, magazine, borrowed record, or handwritten note supplied the next door.

The links were slower and easier to miss. They were still links.

I like old records and I like going to places that sell them. I also have no interest in pretending that inconvenience is the whole virtue. Streaming makes a musical graph enormous and immediately traversable. I can hear a name, open the album, start radio from one track, save what works, and move through fifty years of related music without waiting for a store to locate one clean copy.[^spotify-radio]

The physical and digital systems reveal different things. A streaming service is good at continuing from something I selected. A record sleeve is good at telling me who was actually in the room. A used bin can put two records beside each other because somebody needed shelf space, not because a recommendation model calculated their relationship.

Any of those accidents can help. The problem is the starting point.

I have probably not turned on an over-the-air radio station in twenty years. I had a CD player, then a mobile device, then access to more recorded music than a radio schedule could ever hold. I do not miss advertisements or a small rotation beating the same songs into the ground.

I do miss the useful accident when a system forces an unfamiliar record into the sequence.

On-demand listening solves the control problem. It leaves the discovery assignment with me. That does not mean I need to know what everybody is listening to. [The Number One Song I've Never Heard](/articles/the-number-one-song-ive-never-heard/) follows that fragmentation directly. A chart organizes music by popularity. A useful radio trail organizes it by relationship.

The sample supplies one of the best relationships available because it begins with something already installed in memory. [I Want My MTV](/articles/i-want-my-mtv/) follows a related route through music video, live performance, and playlists, rebuilding a channel after the old broadcast sequence broke apart.

## Then the artists enter the room

My day job has made some of these chains physical in a way I never planned.

I ran lights for the Pharcyde. I stage-managed Rakim and DJ Jazzy Jeff. I stage-managed Digable Planets. Sometimes I have worked the show because it was my assignment. Sometimes I have taken a lower crew call because I wanted to be in the building.

That is enough context for this article. [From Wetlands to the Wash](/articles/from-wetlands-to-the-wash/) carries the longer history of Brooklyn Bowl Las Vegas, the room where some of those paths crossed.

The useful point here is smaller. The source records, the hip-hop songs, my listening trails, and my work history keep crossing one another.

## Find one door and go backward

The old monoculture was difficult to avoid because there were only so many places to hear music. Radio, television, record stores, friends, magazines, clubs, and whatever somebody could physically hand you controlled a large part of the path.

That common exposure created shared references. It also created repetition, advertising, gatekeeping, and long stretches of music somebody else had decided I needed to hear.

The current system gives me control. It also makes it easy to live inside an extremely precise little taste enclosure.

I do not solve that by returning to broadcast radio and accepting the ads. I find doors.

A hip-hop record I know well is a door. A sample credit is a door. A musician on the back of a record is a door. A strange video in a streaming trail is a door. A label, producer, record-store bin, live bill, cover version, and recommendation station can all work.

<DiscoveryLoop />

The loop can run for years.

A Beastie Boys song sends me backward to Eugene McDaniels. Eugene McDaniels returns through a Donald Byrd station. Quincy Jones reveals the Pharcyde. The Pharcyde eventually walks into my workplace. Bob James opens Run-DMC and then another dozen drawers. A record I buy becomes a radio seed. A radio seed becomes another record I want to find.

Historical time keeps moving forward. Listening can go both ways.

The future was already hiding in the old record. I had learned it in the other direction.
