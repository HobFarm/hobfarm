Before the Stone Roses start playing, the screen goes black.

A white dial curves around an empty circle. The words look like they came out of an edit controller rather than a design studio:

**THE STONE ROSES**  
**“LOVE SPREADS”**  
**DUR: 4'56"**  
**STEREO MASTER**  
**14th NOVEMBER 1994**  
**VECTOR TELEVISION**  
**EDIT SUITE 1**

It is not an image designed for Spotify. It is a piece of broadcast plumbing, the kind of slate that told a television operation what tape had just arrived, how long it ran, and what was supposed to happen before the picture appeared.

The tape was made for television in 1994. I am watching it inside Spotify in 2026.

That same song has another television life. Beavis and Butt-Head watched a “Love Spreads” video on MTV in an episode called “Vidiots.”[^vidiots] An official video now sits on YouTube. The old Beavis and Butt-Head episode lives in Paramount+’s MTV library. Spotify has the track and, in my current account and territory, a video.

There are two known “Love Spreads” videos. I have not verified which one Spotify is serving or whether the supplied master slate belongs to either public upload. The honest point is larger: one song’s music-video history has been divided across several services, each holding a different part of what used to arrive through one cable channel.

That is this whole article in one song.

## I Want My Version

“I Want My MTV” used to mean that viewers wanted the new cable channel. Now it means something else to me.

I want my MTV.

I want to start with a Sonic Youth video and let the next hour turn into Pixies, Dinosaur Jr., the Breeders, Violent Femmes, the Stone Roses, Blur, My Bloody Valentine, the Flaming Lips, and something made last year that belongs in the same neighborhood even though the musicians were not alive when some of the older videos were new.

I want to start with Nirvana and get something that feels like the summer of 1992.

I want to start with Dr. Dre and rebuild the hip-hop blocks I remember from the early 1990s.

I want to start with Pearl Jam’s “Jeremy” and get Helmet, Tool, Alice in Chains, Rage Against the Machine, Incubus, and Nine Inch Nails.

I want to start with Snarky Puppy and stop watching promotional clips entirely. I want a filmed performance, then a full concert, then Weather Report at Montreux, then Jeff Beck, Stevie Ray Vaughan, the Grateful Dead, or a Radiohead show.

At midnight, I might want Tinzo and Jojo, Miss Monique, or an Aphex Twin set running on the television while my living room becomes a very small club with a much better bathroom.

That is not one playlist. It is a private music network.

The old MTV slogan works because of the possessive pronoun. I do not want every minute of MTV returned exactly as it was. I already sat through that schedule once.

My hand-built music-video playlist has roughly 400 videos. There is no Mariah Carey in it. She can obviously sing. I respect the machinery in that voice. I also heard and saw enough Mariah Carey on MTV for one lifetime, and I am not a fan.

There is very little of the teen-idol boy-band machine either. New Kids on the Block already got their airtime. The later version gave us Backstreet Boys and *NSYNC, the group Justin Timberlake came from. I do not need them between Sonic Youth and Helmet.

That does not mean every young male vocal group belongs in the same bucket. Boyz II Men can sing their asses off. They belong to an R&B and soul-vocal tradition built around harmony, arrangement, and actual singing. They can be on my channel. The point is not to establish a universal law of acceptable music. Somebody else can make a channel consisting entirely of teen-pop videos and enjoy the hell out of it.

The point is that they can make theirs and I can make mine.

## Before the Countdown Ate the Channel

When I watched MTV in 1988, it was still mostly music television.

It was not an on-demand archive. It was a channel. You surrendered your time to it.

You could wait for a video you wanted. You could leave it running in the background. You could catch the end of something and spend the next week trying to figure out what it was. You could sit through videos you hated because something better might be next. You could change the channel, but you could not edit MTV.

That lack of control had two opposite effects.

First, it was annoying. MTV had advertisements, repetition, label pressure, daytime standards, heavily promoted stars, and long stretches of material I did not care about. A network schedule could beat one song into the ground until the first second of the video made you reach for the remote.

Second, it forced everybody through the same doorway.

The regular rotation sat beside programs that worked like departments inside a magazine. *120 Minutes* went deeper into alternative music. *Headbangers Ball* took over for metal. *Yo! MTV Raps* built a television room around hip-hop. *MTV Unplugged* gave artists a different performance format. *Liquid Television* put experimental animation into American living rooms. Beavis and Butt-Head turned the act of watching and criticizing music videos into a show of its own.

The channel also had people.

VJs introduced the material, interviewed artists, and created continuity. Kurt Loder could interrupt the rotation with MTV News. A strange animated station ID could run between two completely unrelated videos. The Brothers Quay could make a little nightmare for a network bumper.[^quay] A short film, an interview, a promo, a news item, or two idiots on a couch could become the connective tissue.

Spotify can recreate a video neighborhood. It cannot yet recreate the feeling that the whole channel has been temporarily possessed by a stop-motion object made in a dark workshop.

That was the shit between the videos.

It was also part of why the videos mattered.

## Spotify Accidentally Rebuilds the Block

I did not open Spotify expecting to write an article about MTV. I played a music video and watched the queue form around it.

Morphine was playing. The visible trail included Sonic Youth, Pixies, LCD Soundsystem, the Stone Roses, Dinosaur Jr., Violent Femmes, King Krule, Blur, My Bloody Valentine, the Flaming Lips, the Breeders, Lush, Elliott Smith, and bar italia.

It looked like somebody had found one of my old alternative-video afternoons and punched holes through it into several other decades.

Changing the first video changes the channel.

“Jeremy Radio” produced Helmet’s “Unsung,” Tool’s “Forty Six & 2,” Alice in Chains’ “I Stay Away,” Rage Against the Machine’s “Down Rodeo,” Incubus’ “Wish You Were Here,” and Nine Inch Nails’ “Every Day Is Exactly the Same.”

The little video icon matters. Some items have a video available. Some remain audio.

“Forty Six & 2” is not good evidence that Spotify forgot a Tool video. The official Tool material I can find for that particular recording is audio, not a documented official promotional video. Tool has official videos for other songs. This row is a control case, not proof of a missing asset.

That sounds obvious until the same interface presents a famous song whose famous video absolutely exists, then offers only audio because Spotify does not have that video in its current catalog.

The queue is not a complete archive. It is a map of what the platform can currently connect. Spotify’s own documentation says labels and distributors deliver video, and that availability varies by market.[^spotify-video]

Even with those holes, the experience is better than old MTV at several basic jobs. I do not wait for the good video. I do not need the correct program to start at midnight. I can move between audio and video without changing libraries. The same music follows me onto my phone and starts in my car. At home, it becomes television again.

I used to buy a CD for more than fifteen dollars because I wanted one or two songs. I still have a pile of those discs. The CD did not include the video. It did not automatically become a custom radio station. It did not move from a computer to a phone to a car and then grow a screen when I got home.

A streaming subscription can do all of that for about the price of one old album each month.

That is useful enough that I do not need to join every platform-hate campaign that appears online. Spotify has legitimate criticisms. So do YouTube, Google, Paramount, every record company, every concert promoter, and almost every business that has ever stood between a musician and the audience. I work in live music. I know the artist is often the person everybody claims to serve while finding new ways to take a piece.

That larger economic story matters. It is not the only story here.

For this article, the important fact is simpler: Spotify can turn one video into a personal programming block.

The playlist gives me exactly what I chose. Spotify gives me things adjacent to what I chose. MTV gave me whatever MTV chose.

## A Video From 1992 Is Still the Next Video

<MediaWatchCard title="Nearly Lost You" artist="Screaming Trees" videoId="PE5f561Y1x4" note="Official artist upload." />

<MtvSpotifyTrack track="nearly-lost-you" />

“Nearly Lost You” by Screaming Trees came out in 1992. The video is about thirty-four years old now.

In September 1992, *120 Minutes* played it in a block with Sonic Youth’s “100%,” Jane’s Addiction’s “Stop,” Sinéad O’Connor, Throwing Muses, Bad Religion, Ministry, Sugar, and Red Hot Chili Peppers.[^120-minutes] The surviving program record looks remarkably close to the kind of recommendation neighborhood Spotify can rebuild from one seed today.

The difference is that the old block belonged to a date.

The modern queue flattens the shelves.

Sonic Youth can sit beside LCD Soundsystem, King Krule, Wet Leg, Water From Your Eyes, Jamie xx, and a new release without a large sign announcing which music is historically important, which is old, and which is supposed to belong to the present.

That does not erase history. It changes the first encounter.

In the 1990s, I found bands from the 1960s as music, not only as museum pieces. Somebody younger can now find Dinosaur Jr., Violent Femmes, the Toadies, Kim Gordon, or Screaming Trees through the same basic mechanism. The recording has a date. Discovery does not have to feel dated.

Some of these artists are still touring. Some of the bands in my Spotify queue will play the room where I work. The archive is not a graveyard. It is connected to stages, ticket sales, younger audiences, old fans, and musicians who kept going after the television system that introduced them stopped performing that job.

Music used to arrive in eras. Streaming lets the eras occupy the same room.

## The Tapes Are Everywhere, Just Not in One Place

Spotify is not a complete replacement for MTV because Spotify does not have all the videos.

Guns N’ Roses makes the problem obvious.

The band’s official YouTube channel contains the videos that helped define MTV’s late-1980s and early-1990s rock rotation. Sort the channel by oldest and the archive is sitting there. Spotify can play the songs and build radio around them, but the videos I expect to find are currently absent from its music-video catalog.

That is not even the first strange Guns N’ Roses gap on Spotify.

In 2017, users reported that “It’s So Easy,” “Yesterdays,” “Bad Obsession,” and “The Garden” had disappeared from the service. The conspicuous common factor was songwriter West Arkeen. The gap was apparently connected to rights surrounding West Arkeen’s songwriting shares, but I have not found a direct public explanation from Spotify, the label, the band, or Arkeen’s estate. The songs eventually returned.[^gnr]

The publishable fact is the disappearance. The rights explanation remains an informed inference.

The CD does not lose track two because somebody’s publishing agreement changed.

Streaming access can.

Music videos add more layers. A service can have permission to play the sound recording without having the delivered video master, the audiovisual publishing clearance, the correct territorial rights, or every other agreement needed to attach moving pictures to that song in that place.

The button makes the system look simple because all good interfaces hide the paperwork.

Then the catalog produces the opposite surprise.

Massive Attack’s “Be Thankful for What You’ve Got” is a cover of William DeVaughn. Baillie Walsh filmed the video inside Raymond’s Revuebar in Soho, using a striptease and nudity that did not belong in ordinary daytime MTV rotation. In a published first-person account, production designer Jerry Chater says he was at the shoot and that MTV and the BBC did not show the result.[^massive-attack]

Spotify can show it now.

So the modern archive contains videos MTV played endlessly, videos a production participant says MTV would not show, songs without documented official videos, and famous videos that remain missing from a particular service.

A useful interface needs only one symbol for all of that.

The history behind the symbol is a rights department with a hangover.

## I Made a Four-Hundred-Video Channel and Shuffle Broke It

YouTube solves the missing-tape problem more often than Spotify.

Nirvana’s official channel has the videos that were on MTV. Guns N’ Roses has the videos Spotify does not currently show me. Artist and label channels hold decades of promotional films, remasters, alternate versions, live performances, television appearances, and material that never had much chance of appearing in regular cable rotation.

So I did the obvious thing.

I built MTV.

<MtvLinkDeck deck="playlist" />

The playlist has roughly 400 music videos. It is not a historical reconstruction and does not pretend to be one. It contains what I want to watch. It excludes what I do not.

On paper, four hundred videos on shuffle should produce a television station that can run for days.

In practice, YouTube plays it like somebody dropped the programming schedule down a flight of stairs.

The shuffle does not seem to explore the whole list. I might see around twenty videos before material begins repeating. The interface gets slow while trying to load and manage the large playlist. It works, but it does not work like a reliable continuous channel.

I can sort the list in different ways. I can manually move things around. I can build smaller blocks and queue them deliberately. That makes the system useful.

It also means I am doing unpaid database maintenance for my own television station.

Apparently one of MTV’s forgotten technological accomplishments was successfully playing the next video.

The failure reveals something useful. Pure shuffle may be a worse interface than programming once an archive gets large enough.

MTV did not pull hundreds of tapes from a barrel. It had blocks, shows, pacing, transitions, and schedules. I can rebuild that structure with smaller lists:

- Summer of 1992
- *120 Minutes* territory
- Early-1990s rap videos
- Videos MTV played to death that I still like
- Videos MTV did not show me
- *MTV Unplugged* night
- Full concert night
- Book Club Radio
- Weird shit Spotify found this week

The master playlist becomes the tape library. The smaller playlists become programs.

YouTube Music may be the glue Google built for exactly this problem. It puts a music-service interface on top of YouTube’s enormous archive, offers Song and Video modes when paired versions exist, and can expose music playlists created through the regular YouTube account.[^youtube-music]

That does not guarantee it will fix my shuffle problem. It may simply provide another interface capable of repeating the same twenty things.

It still deserves a real test because it sits between Spotify’s channel behavior and YouTube’s archive.

## Music Television Gets Longer

The old three-to-five-minute music video is only one program type.

YouTube can become a full concert channel in one click.

I can watch Phish. I can find Radiohead concerts. I can watch Weather Report at Montreux, Jeff Beck, Stevie Ray Vaughan, the Grateful Dead, and whatever else occurs to me. Official artist channels, festival archives, broadcasters, labels, and fan recordings have created a live-music archive no cable schedule could contain.

MTV had live music, of course. *MTV Unplugged* remains one of its best ideas.

Nirvana’s official YouTube channel has the individual *Unplugged* performances. Alice in Chains has “Nutshell,” “Down in a Hole,” “Would?,” “Rooster,” and other performances from its 1996 set. Eric Clapton’s channel has remastered *Unplugged* material. Paramount+ holds complete or longer program versions where its current rights allow them.

Even MTV’s own production has been split into components.

The artist channel has the song.

Paramount+ has the episode.

Spotify has the audio and sometimes a video.

YouTube has clips, uploads, remasters, and related performances.

The recording belongs to one era. Its current availability belongs to a stack of contracts.

Then there is Book Club Radio.

<MediaWatchCard title="Feel Good Sunset House Mix in a New York Park" artist="Tinzo and Jojo / Book Club Radio" videoId="0bpQIBjnvdk" note="Official Book Club Radio upload." />

Book Club Radio turns a filmed DJ set into something between a house party, a club broadcast, a social document, and a television program. I can put Tinzo and Jojo on the screen and have a DJ night at home. I can switch to Miss Monique or an Aphex Twin AV set. The format can run for an hour or two because no program director needs to make room for the next four-minute single.

The same structure exists in physical rooms.

At Brooklyn Bowl, themed DJ nights create temporary channels for a crowd. Club 90s can make the entire night 2000s or 2010s. House of Heavy can take one lane. Emo Night can take another. Somebody programs the block, people choose that particular room, and a shared experience returns.

Broadcast MTV gave millions of viewers one channel.

A private playlist gives one viewer complete control.

A themed venue night gives a self-selected crowd one temporary channel.

The communal experience did not vanish either. It became an event you choose to enter.

## The Private Film Program

A recommendation trail also stops being only a music-discovery system.

It becomes a short-film program.

LCD Soundsystem’s “oh baby” is a Rian Johnson science-fiction tragedy with Sissy Spacek and David Strathairn. Jamie xx’s “Gosh” uses Tianducheng, an Eiffel Tower replica, mass choreography, and architectural scale. Bonobo’s “Cirrus” lets Cyriak turn consumer imagery into an infinitely mutating machine. Salvatore Ganacci’s “Horse” is a surreal comedy that arrived through a random Spotify video trail and became one of my favorite videos.[^film-program]

<MediaWatchCard title="Horse" artist="Salvatore Ganacci" videoId="NkRkuI0ZgX0" note="Official OWSLA upload." />

These videos are not merely illustrations for songs. They are narrative shorts, collage animation, choreography, production design, performance, visual effects, and jokes compressed into the length of a single.

MTV helped teach audiences to accept that density. Personalized discovery now lets the form wander far beyond whatever one network would have scheduled.

That is another article. It needs a larger sample from my actual watch history rather than a generic best-of list.

For this article, the important point is that my channel can move from a remembered 1992 rotation into a new short film I did not know existed, then into a two-hour DJ set, then back into Nirvana *Unplugged*.

Cable MTV could not give me that much control or depth.

Cable MTV made me want it.

## MTV’s Cable Afterlife

Paramount+ is where a substantial amount of surviving MTV programming now lives.

Its current MTV hub includes selected series and specials, while individual pages hold *MTV Unplugged*, Beavis and Butt-Head, *Æon Flux*, and other programs under the MTV brand. The library is not complete and can vary by market and subscription.[^paramount]

Paramount+ is MTV’s cable afterlife.

That is where I can put some of the surrounding programs back into the mix. I can watch Beavis and Butt-Head comment on videos. I can watch an *Unplugged* episode rather than only the separated performances. I can find material that belonged to MTV as a network rather than to an artist’s label channel.

<MtvLinkDeck deck="paramount" />

The old channel has become a set of organs stored in different jars.

Spotify has the records and some videos.

YouTube has more of the tapes.

YouTube Music tries to make the tapes behave like a music library.

Paramount+ has some of the shows.

The artist channels have individual performances.

The viewer has the schedule.

## The VJs Escaped Too

The people did not remain frozen in 1994.

Kurt Loder continued writing film criticism and conducting interviews long after MTV News stopped being a daily interruption in the video stream.

Carson Daly is now a co-host of NBC News’ *TODAY* and remains host and producer of *The Voice*.[^vjs] The host I associate with *Total Request Live* now appears on morning news television.

Kennedy, who became an MTV VJ in 1992, is a Fox News Media contributor and podcast host. That is a strange path to encounter from the far side of an old alternative-music block, but it is the path.

Martha Quinn remained in radio.

The VJs were unbundled too. News, interviews, commentary, radio, personality, and music fandom moved into separate outlets and personal brands.

Spotify can give me a queue. It does not give me Kurt Loder walking in to explain why something just happened.

Whether I miss that depends on the day.

## TRL and the Beginning of the End

MTV did not suddenly stop being MTV on one afternoon.

The network had been adding non-music programming for years. Some of that programming was excellent. *Liquid Television* and Beavis and Butt-Head are part of what I am trying to reconstruct now.

Still, *Total Request Live* is my marker for the beginning of the end.

That is an opinion, not a neutral historical verdict.

TRL premiered in 1998 and compressed the channel into a countdown, a studio crowd, celebrity appearances, promotional momentum, and the particular pop culture surrounding Carson Daly.[^trl] Music videos were still present, often in fragments. The program was successful because it understood where the audience was moving.

For me, it felt like MTV had stopped being a place where I might stumble onto something strange and become a place where I watched the promotion of things I already knew I did not care about.

The shared channel was narrowing at the same moment the larger media system was beginning to fracture.

Napster arrived. Broadband grew. YouTube would eventually turn the archive into a searchable warehouse. Streaming would separate music access from ownership. Social platforms would create their own fragments of attention. The schedule lost its monopoly.

TRL did not single-handedly kill MTV. It is simply where the version I wanted began to end.

Then Carson Daly walked into our campsite.

## Woodstock ’99

In 1999, my friends and I went to Woodstock.

At some point, the person I identify as Carson Daly came over to our camp with an MTV crew and interviewed us. I do not know whether that footage aired or where it would live now. I have a photograph.

Years later, from a different account in a different phase of the internet, I tweeted the image to him. As I remember it, he retweeted it, replied, and his show account followed me.

Now that show is gone. The account is gone. Carson Daly is on morning television. MTV’s music-video channel is gone. The event has been repackaged through documentaries, retrospectives, arguments, and memories.

The photograph remains.

It is currently a shitty phone picture of an old print sitting on a table. I have more Woodstock ’99 photographs and plan to scan them properly. That is a future article, once I scan the photographs.

What I have now is a rough artifact from the moment my MTV was beginning to disappear.

I can still watch almost everything that made it valuable.

I can begin with Sonic Youth and build an alternative block. I can find every Nirvana video on the band’s official channel. I can fill Spotify’s Guns N’ Roses gap with YouTube. I can watch Alice in Chains *Unplugged*, then a full Radiohead concert, then Weather Report at Montreux, then Tinzo and Jojo. I can add Beavis and Butt-Head from Paramount+. I can leave Mariah Carey and New Kids on the Block out of my schedule. Somebody else can build the opposite channel.

I built a better MTV than cable could ever give me.

I could not have built it without cable MTV.

MTV did not disappear. It became raw material.
