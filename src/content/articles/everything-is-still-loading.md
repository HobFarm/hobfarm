[*Take Me to Phobos*](/articles/take-me-to-phobos/) ended in 1993.

The cave had become visible. *Doom* replaced the parser with a gun, a rendered hallway, and immediate physical control. Mosaic was helping turn the Web into something ordinary people could recognize as a new medium.

That was also roughly where my own computer history began.

Before I owned a machine capable of running *Doom*, I played *Wolfenstein 3D* on my mother's boss's computer. This was still an era when access to the computer was part of the game. The software might exist, but the machine powerful enough to run it belonged to somebody else.

My first computer was a Packard Bell 486 with four megabytes of RAM and a 2400-bits-per-second modem. The computer could display graphics, but at that modem speed my online world was mostly text. My friends were already calling bulletin board systems, trading phone numbers, leaving messages, downloading files, playing door games, and moving through dozens of tiny independent worlds connected to ordinary telephone lines.

I wanted *Doom*.

So I bought another four megabytes of memory and upgraded the modem to 14.4k.

The game was ready. My computer needed work.

Thirty years later, I have a computer powerful enough to simulate cities, weather, economies, crowds, physics, cinematic lighting, and every individual hair in a fictional beard.

Now the computer is ready.

The game often isn't.

Four megabytes was technically within the original territory for running *Doom*, but it was also the bare edge of usability. Memory consumed by drivers and system configuration could leave too little available for the game. Eight megabytes gave the machine room to breathe. ([Aptiva: Tips on running Doom](https://ps-2.kev009.com/pcpartnerinfo/ctstips/f4a2.htm))

That distinction matters because this is not an argument that old PC gaming worked perfectly.

It absolutely did not.

Games had bugs. Hardware conflicted. Drivers failed. Sound cards disappeared. Memory managers had to be rearranged. Configuration files became small religious texts. A game might technically support your computer while still running like it had been lowered into a swamp.

The difference was that I usually understood the contract.

A game existed. The requirements were printed on the box. My computer either met them or did not. When it did not, I could identify the missing component and replace it.

I bought memory.

I bought a modem.

The computer became capable of something new.

The upgrade belonged to me.

The improvement remained installed whether the developer stayed in business, whether a server remained online, whether an account recognized me, or whether somebody changed a support document five years later.

The machine was a thing I could open.

## The PC becomes a workshop

By the late 1990s, *Diablo* had turned the dungeon into a machine for compulsive repetition. The levels changed. The loot changed. The character improved. The same basic descent could keep producing slightly different stories.

The computer was also becoming more than a game machine.

I started editing video through workflows that would sound insane to somebody who has always been able to record, edit, upload, and watch video on the same phone.

The camera recorded onto DV tape.

The tape entered the computer through FireWire.

The computer captured the footage in real time.

I edited it, exported it, burned it onto a DVD, put the DVD into a standalone player, and connected that player to a projector with RCA cables because the picture looked better through that path than it did when I ran VGA directly from the computer.

That was normal problem-solving.

The route was ugly, but it was visible:

Camera. Tape. Cable. Computer. File. Disc. Player. Cable. Projector.

When something failed, I could walk through the chain and find the problem.

Modern systems are easier until they are not. Then the failure may live inside an account, compatibility layer, cloud service, driver policy, security requirement, unsupported operating system, expired certificate, abandoned app, or platform decision I cannot inspect.

Computers became easier to operate while becoming harder to control.

## Worlds arrive on the computer

In the summer of 2007, I was in Poland.

I was hanging around with actual Polish fans of Andrzej Sapkowski's Witcher books, people who knew Geralt before he became an international game character, television property, action figure, streaming thumbnail, and general piece of global fantasy inventory.

They introduced me to the books.

Then the first *Witcher* game came out that October.

It felt like an absurd coincidence. I had encountered this strange Polish fantasy world through people who already cared about it, then watched it materialize on my computer a few months later. CD Projekt records the first game's Polish premiere as October 26, 2007. ([CD Projekt](https://www.cdprojekt.com/en/capital-group/history/))

That series became one of the major lines through my gaming life.

So did *Dragon Age: Origins*.

The first *Dragon Age* had the things I still want from a role-playing game: a party with opinions, a camp where relationships developed, tactical combat, consequential decisions, different origins, and a world that appeared to notice what kind of person I was becoming inside it.

*Baldur's Gate 3* would later revive a lot of that feeling on a much larger technical scale. It did not copy *Dragon Age: Origins*, but it touched many of the same nerves: companions who could approve, disapprove, leave, fall in love, become worse, become better, or discover that I had made a decision they could not forgive.

Between them came the *Mass Effect* trilogy, the later *Dragon Age* games, *The Old Republic*, *Skyrim*, *Fallout 4*, the rest of *The Witcher* trilogy, and both console and PC versions of *Grand Theft Auto V*.

I bought a PlayStation 3 so I could play *GTA V* when it came out.

Then I bought the game again when the PC version arrived.

Rockstar already sold me that game twice. It can finish the PC version of *Grand Theft Auto VI* before it sells me the first copy. As of July 2026, Rockstar lists the game for November 19, 2026 on PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S, with no PC version announced. ([Rockstar Games](https://www.rockstargames.com/VI/))

I am not holding my breath.

## The disc that installed Steam

The major transition arrived on September 21, 2010.

I bought *Civilization V* on a physical disc the day it came out. I brought it home, put it into the computer, and discovered that it required Steam.

That was how I got Steam.

This was not an accident or a strange condition attached to one retail edition. Valve and 2K announced that all PC versions of *Civilization V* would use Steamworks. The game launched in North America on September 21, 2010. ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/news/3792/))

The disc was still physically present.

It was no longer the whole product.

It contained files, an installer, and a key that connected the game to an account, a storefront, an authentication system, an update service, a community, downloadable content, and a digital library that would eventually become the place where nearly all my PC games lived.

Steam solved real problems.

Automatic updates are useful. Digital distribution is useful. Cloud saves are useful. Community tools, sales, mod support, old-game availability, and one manageable library are useful.

Steam was not the villain.

It was the hinge.

Once every customer remained connected to an automatic patching system, a release stopped being final. A broken quest could be repaired. A bad interface could be rebuilt. A thin system could be expanded. Missing content could arrive later. A disastrous launch could become the first chapter of a redemption story.

The repair channel was useful.

Its existence also made shipping the damage easier.

## Civilization has to beat Civilization

When *Civilization V* came out, I was comparing it with *Civilization IV* plus *Warlords* and *Beyond the Sword*.

That comparison was technically unfair.

It was also inevitable.

I was not comparing the new game with a preserved memory of what *Civilization IV* had looked like on its own launch day. I was comparing it with the complete game installed on my computer the day before.

The old version had years of balancing, patches, mods, expansions, systems, refinements, and accumulated expectations behind it. *Beyond the Sword* was itself the second major *Civilization IV* expansion and added civilizations, leaders, scenarios, espionage, corporations, events, and other systems. ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/8800/Civilization_IV_Beyond_the_Sword/))

Then the sequel arrived with new ideas and less accumulated depth.

The same thing happened again.

Mature *Civilization V*, with *Gods & Kings*, *Brave New World*, years of patches, and a huge mod ecosystem, was replaced by launch-day *Civilization VI*.

Then mature *Civilization VI* was replaced by launch-day *Civilization VII*.

The new game does not compete with the previous game as it existed at birth.

It competes with the previous game after a decade of becoming itself.

That is why I wait.

I no longer buy a new *Civilization* when it comes out. I give it at least two years. By then, the worst technical problems have usually been patched, the design has settled, the community understands what is missing, the first wave of additional content has arrived, and the price has stopped behaving like I am financing the invention of agriculture.

*Civ VII* made the decision easier.

It changed major parts of the series, launched into a fan base that was not universally looking for those changes, and offered editions originally priced at $69.99, $99.99, and $129.99. Its official site was still foregrounding the major Test of Time 1.4.0 update and follow-up fixes in July 2026. ([Civilization VII update notes](https://civilization.2k.com/civ-vii/game-update-notes/))

I am not paying a hundred dollars to find out whether a game becomes something I want in 2027.

Civilization has taught me patience.

Not the patience required to finish a long campaign.

The patience required to wait until the product catches up with its price.

> I don't buy Civilization when it comes out.
> I buy it after it becomes Civilization.

Saying that every modern game is broken would be an embellishment.

The more accurate problem is that the meaning of a release has fragmented into several different contracts.

### The Witcher 3: complete with defects

*The Witcher 3* had bugs, patches, technical problems, and later expansions.

It still felt like the intended game had arrived.

There was a beginning, a middle, an ending, a huge world, functioning quest lines, and a complete central experience. The patches repaired the product. The expansions expanded it.

A complete game can contain defects.

Completion does not mean perfection.

### Baldur's Gate 3: construction site with a sign

I bought *Baldur's Gate 3* on the first day of Early Access.

I knew what I was buying.

The game was unfinished. Most of it was unavailable. Classes and systems were missing. Saves could become useless. Large parts of the design were still changing.

Larian did not pretend otherwise.

It was a construction site with a ticket booth, but the sign said **CONSTRUCTION SITE**.

I enjoyed the arrangement. I played Act I repeatedly as the systems changed, then played the whole game after release.

That process also created an uneven final shape.

Act I is astonishingly dense. Almost every corner contains another route, conversation, character, hidden interaction, or ridiculous solution somebody at Larian anticipated.

By the time I reached Baldur's Gate itself, the game was still enormous and still very good, but it felt thinner and less stable. Quests collided. Performance deteriorated. Reactions failed to appear. The ending did not have the same amount of breathing room as the beginning.

Act I had three years of players.

Act III had a release date.

Larian later acknowledged that Act III performed worse than the first two acts and needed substantial additional work. The first major patch fixed more than a thousand bugs. Patch 5 later added an entirely new epilogue section and more Act III performance improvements. ([Baldur's Gate 3](https://baldursgate3.game/news/community-update-24-looking-to-the-future_88))

That does not turn *Baldur's Gate 3* into a scam.

It shows the honest version of permanent development. The incompleteness was disclosed. Participation was voluntary. The process produced one of the best games I have played, along with visible evidence that ambition eventually collided with time.

### Cyberpunk 2077: construction site without a sign

*Cyberpunk 2077* reversed that arrangement.

The marketing sold a completed fantasy.

The customer discovered the construction process after entering it.

Eight days after release, Sony removed the game from the PlayStation Store while arranging refunds for customers who wanted them. ([CD Projekt](https://www.cdprojekt.com/en/investors/regulatory-announcements/current-report-no-66-2020/))

The game eventually improved.

That improvement is part of the argument.

CD Projekt could collect launch revenue, spend years repairing the product, release a major expansion, and gradually rebuild both the game and its reputation.

The game became much closer to what customers believed they had purchased.

The product and the public memory of the product were both patched.

### Fallout 76: service opens before the ride is ready

*Fallout 76* was another arrangement.

The customers were not only players. They were also the population required to make the world feel occupied, the stress test, the feedback system, the ongoing content demand, and the audience expected to remain while the service developed around them.

Todd Howard later described the launch directly: Bethesda let people down, and there was very little the company did not screw up. ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/todd-howard-there-was-very-little-we-didnt-screw-up-on-fallout-76-launch/))

The game also improved.

Modern game history is full of redemption stories because the commercial system permits a release to be both a disaster and an opening chapter.

A bad launch no longer has to remain the final state.

It only has to survive long enough to become something else.

### Civilization VII: pay now, revisit in two years

*Civ VII* is not the same kind of technical catastrophe as launch-day *Cyberpunk 2077*.

It represents something more ordinary.

A premium sequel launches as the foundation of the game it may eventually become. Systems get reworked. Interfaces improve. Features return. Content collections arrive. The community spends months explaining what the design is trying to do while the developers spend years deciding which parts of that design should remain.

That may work.

I will check later.

## The release date changed meaning

Patches are not the problem.

Early Access is not automatically the problem.

Downloadable expansions are not the problem.

The problem is the collapse of release as a meaningful promise.

There is a real difference between:

> Here is a complete game with defects we will repair.

And:

> Here is an unfinished game. Support its development if that sounds interesting.

And:

> Here is the completed game you were promised. Please do not look behind the wall marked "future roadmap."

Modern games are larger, more complex, more interconnected, and harder to test across thousands of hardware and software combinations. Research into more than twelve thousand bug fixes across hundreds of Steam updates found recurring problems tied to testing difficulty, code quality, and bug reproduction. ([We'll Fix It in Post](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03997))

That explains why perfect launches are unrealistic.

It does not explain why the customer should accept every unfinished launch at full price.

A release date once meant the developer believed the game was ready for the player.

Now it can mean the player is finally allowed to begin testing the game.

## The computer still works

I assembled my current computer in 2018.

It has an Intel i9-7980XE, 64 gigabytes of RAM, and an RTX 2080 Ti. It still performs the work I built it to perform.

Then Windows pushed me toward Windows 11.

After the upgrade, the GPU could manage only about 15 frames per second in the Heaven benchmark. I tried everything I could find. Nothing fixed it.

Eventually I reformatted the computer and reinstalled Windows 10.

That fixed the GPU problem. It also cleared up a pile of smaller problems that had accumulated over the years. The workstation has been running fine ever since.

While working on this article, I went looking for old game screenshots. I did not discover a lost archive I desperately needed back. I barely cared about the screenshots themselves.

The search reminded me why they were gone.

They had been buried inside Steam's folders when I wiped the failed Windows 11 installation. I remembered the important project files and applications. The screenshots were collateral damage.

One image had survived somewhere else.

Then, the other day, I opened Premiere.

Adobe warned me that Windows 10 no longer met the current operating-system requirement.

The warning was accurate. Adobe lists Windows 11 version 24H2 as the minimum for Premiere 26.0 and 26.2. Its June 2026 Premiere 26.3 release notes say Windows 10 support will be removed in a future release and tell Windows 10 users to upgrade to Windows 11 24H2 to avoid interruption. Adobe still lists Windows 10 22H2 for the older Premiere 25.x line. ([Premiere 26.x requirements](https://helpx.adobe.com/premiere/desktop/get-started/technical-requirements/adobe-premiere-pro-technical-requirements.html), [Premiere release notes](https://helpx.adobe.com/premiere/desktop/whats-new/release-notes.html), [Premiere 25.x requirements](https://helpx.adobe.com/premiere/desktop/get-started/technical-requirements/adobe-premiere-25x-technical-requirements.html))

So the computer is working. Windows 10 is working. Premiere still opens. The current product line has already moved the supported floor to the operating system that broke my machine.

Microsoft ended ordinary Windows 10 support on October 14, 2025 while acknowledging that the computers themselves would continue functioning. ([Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/end-of-support))

The same thing happened to my old phone.

The screen worked. The processor worked. The radio worked. The camera worked. Then applications stopped updating for its version of Android. The phone's physical function remained normal while the surrounding software ecosystem withdrew.

This is a different kind of hardware failure.

Nothing has to break.

The doors close around it.

## The upgrade that does not belong to me

My Packard Bell needed more memory, so I installed more memory.

The improvement was direct.

Today an upgrade may mean replacing working hardware because a platform changed its requirements. It may mean buying another phone because applications abandoned the operating system. It may mean rebuilding a stable editing workstation because the latest software no longer recognizes its environment as current.

At the same time, the price and availability of the next machine are being shaped by industries much larger than personal computing.

I watched graphics cards become speculative equipment during the cryptocurrency boom. Then pandemic-era supply failures made ordinary electronics difficult to obtain. Now AI data centers are consuming advanced processors, storage, and memory at industrial scale.

Micron said in late 2025 that AI-driven data-center demand had surged enough for the company to exit its Crucial consumer business and redirect supply toward larger strategic customers. ([Micron](https://investors.micron.com/news-releases/news-release-details/micron-announces-exit-crucial-consumer-business))

That does not mean AI is solely responsible for every expensive component.

It means ordinary computer users are no longer the most valuable customers in the hardware market built around computers.

The personal computer now competes with mining farms, hyperscale data centers, enterprise contracts, cloud services, and whatever industrial demand arrives next.

RAM used to feel like the cheap part.

Now every part of the machine looks like it has been placed behind glass at an airport electronics store.

I am still excited about where games can go.

That is important, because this is not an article about wanting everything to return to 1993.

I do not want to configure extended memory again.

I do not want to download files over a 14.4k modem.

I do not want every game delivered in a cardboard box containing six floppy disks, three of which fail after the store closes.

I want the future.

A convincing open world in VR, capable of remembering what I did, responding to choices, changing while I am away, and generating new routes through a persistent story no longer sounds impossible.

The pieces already exist.

VR can create presence.

Modern engines can create enormous environments.

Role-playing systems can track relationships and consequences.

Procedural systems can produce variation.

Language models can allow characters and worlds to respond beyond a fixed dialogue menu.

The difficult part is making those pieces behave like one intentional game rather than a pile of demonstrations connected to a subscription service.

The future needs better hardware, but it also needs better contracts with the people buying it.

A generated world is useless if the characters cannot remember what matters.

A persistent world is useless if the server disappears.

A VR game is useless if the headset becomes unsupported.

A digital library is useless if ownership means permission that can be withdrawn.

A brilliant game is difficult to celebrate when its first two years are treated like paid rehearsal.

The future is visible.

Access is the difficult part.

## Everything is still loading

My computer life began with understandable limitations.

I wanted to play *Doom*.

I needed four more megabytes.

I wanted to move through BBSs faster.

I needed a better modem.

Later I needed a FireWire port, a DVD burner, a projector, a Steam account, another platform, another copy of the same game, another patch, another expansion, another operating system, and apparently another computer.

Each layer made more possible.

Each layer also moved part of the experience farther away from me.

With *Doom*, I upgraded the computer because the game had arrived.

With *Civilization V*, the game installed the platform through which its future versions would arrive.

With modern releases, the platform is already running before I buy anything. The account exists. The store is open. The update service is waiting. The roadmap is published. The deluxe edition offers early access to the launch that begins the patches that eventually build the game people thought they were purchasing.

I still want to see what comes next.

I have spent my entire computer life watching impossible things become ordinary.

The future keeps getting closer.

Everything required to reach it keeps moving.

Everything is still loading.

The same thing happened to the internet.

The Web stopped being somewhere I went and became something that never stopped arriving.

That is the next article: *Vacation Into Nothing*.
