## The photograph inside another article

I was going through photographs from a 2016 cross-country drive while building [yesterday’s article about the American Dream](/articles/fear-and-loathing-after-the-american-dream/). Almost ten years after taking it, I found a photograph of the full *Bangor Daily News* front page from August 5, 2016.

The big headline is **Trump talks Somalis in Maine**.

The small headline is **Hermit does not have to pay for repairs**.

The small headline wins.

I had seen the paper at a friend’s house while I was living near Castine after reaching Maine. Castine has enough history for its own article, so I am not dragging the Penobscot Expedition into this one.

The Trump story also belongs to a longer Maine history. I had lived and worked around African immigrant communities in southern Maine during the early 2000s. The Somali community growing in Lewiston was shaped largely by secondary migration: people initially resettled elsewhere in the United States chose to move again, often looking for safety, schools, housing, family connections, and a different scale of city. By 2016, a presidential candidate had converted that history into campaign menace.[^somali] That deserves a separate article.

This article belongs to the other column.

## What the repair story says

Christopher Knight had lived for twenty-seven years in the woods near Rome, Maine, surviving through burglaries of nearby cabins and camps. When police found his site, they repeatedly drove a private woods road to collect evidence, remove accumulated material, and restore the land. Their vehicles damaged the road. The Maine State Police paid $1,125 to repair it, then the State asked the sentencing court to make Knight reimburse the agency through criminal restitution.[^knight-case]

The trial court agreed. On August 4, 2016, the Maine Supreme Judicial Court did not.

The 4–2 majority did not decide that Knight had nothing to do with the operation. It decided that restitution could be paid only to a statutorily eligible recipient. The police were not victims of the burglary and theft convictions. The private road owner was not a victim of those crimes either. Because the owner did not qualify, the police did not become an eligible collateral claimant by paying for the repair. The dissent argued that Knight’s crimes caused the recovery operation and that the repair was a legitimate cleanup expense.[^knight-case]

The cops tore up the road, paid to fix it, tried to send the hermit the bill through restitution, and lost 4 to 2.

That tiny headline raises a larger question. What does it mean to live outside society when society is still stocking the pantry, filling the propane tank, maintaining the road, and eventually repairing it?

The duration is the hook. The supply chain is the article.

## Two men compressed into one story

My father was a Marine on Guam in the early 1970s. He told me about the Japanese soldier found there, and over the years my memory compressed that man with another soldier into one family story: the man on Guam who thought World War II was still happening.

That is two different men.

Shōichi Yokoi was found on Guam in 1972. Hiroo Onoda surrendered in the Philippines in 1974 after his former commander formally relieved him of duty. Their timelines almost match. Their conduct does not.

## Shōichi Yokoi made a system

Yokoi was a tailor before the Imperial Japanese Army drafted him in 1941. He reached Guam in 1943 and served in the supply corps. When American forces retook the island in 1944 and the Japanese command collapsed, he disappeared into the Talo’fo’fo River basin with other holdouts.[^yokoi]

Two CHamoru hunters, Manuel Tolentino De Gracia and Jesus Mantanona Duenas, found him while checking fish traps on January 24, 1972. Yokoi had known since 1952 that the war was over. He also knew what year it was. Military conditioning, fear of capture, and shame kept him hidden.[^yokoi]

He survived through a working jungle economy. He caught shrimp, fish, eels, toads, rats, and wild pigs. He gathered coconuts, breadfruit, and papayas, and learned to process toxic fadang nuts safely. He made traps and utensils, used metal from wartime wreckage, wove clothing from hibiscus bark fiber, fashioned sandals, and repaired fabric with dried toad skin. His underground shelter was roughly three feet high, nine feet long, and seven feet below the surface, reinforced and lined with bamboo.[^yokoi]

For years he lived near two other holdouts. After finding them dead around 1964, he spent the rest of his hiding period alone.

Yokoi returned to Japan on February 2, 1972. He married Mihoko that November, appeared regularly on television, spoke about survival and simple living, wrote a bestselling account, and ran unsuccessfully for the upper house of parliament in 1974. He died of a heart attack in Nagoya on September 22, 1997.[^yokoi-return]

No bakery. The tailor became a public survival figure.

## Hiroo Onoda kept fighting

Onoda was an army intelligence officer trained for guerrilla warfare. Sent to Lubang Island late in 1944, he carried orders to disrupt the approaching enemy, avoid surrender, and wait for a Japanese return. After U.S. and Philippine Commonwealth forces retook the island, he remained with Yūichi Akatsu, Shōichi Shimada, and Kinshichi Kozuka.[^onoda-philippines]

Akatsu left the group and surrendered in 1950. Philippine forces killed Shimada in 1954. In 1972, local residents found Onoda and Kozuka burning harvested rice; Kozuka died in the resulting gunfight. Onoda escaped and spent the next year and a half alone.[^onoda-philippines]

Leaflets, newspapers, family voices, and search parties did not end the campaign. Onoda treated them as enemy constructions. His group took rice and cattle, burned crops, exchanged fire with police and residents, and killed people. Reports place the number of Filipino civilians killed as high as thirty, but that figure is an estimate, not a neat audited count. Families on Lubang remember individual victims, including farmer Emilio Viaña, who was shot in 1951 and died from blood loss.[^onoda-victims]

Norio Suzuki found Onoda on February 20, 1974. Onoda said he would surrender only under orders from a superior. Former major Yoshimi Taniguchi returned to Lubang and relieved him on March 9. Onoda came down from the mountain on March 10. The next day, President Ferdinand Marcos granted him a full pardon for violations committed during and after the war; Onoda returned to Japan on March 12.[^onoda-philippines]

Japan welcomed him publicly. He published the memoir *No Surrender*, moved to Brazil in 1975, became a cattle rancher, and married Machie Onuku in 1976. He later divided his time between Brazil and Japan and founded the Onoda Nature School in 1984. He died in Tokyo in 2014 at 91.[^onoda-after]

That later life should not turn the private war into a charming loyalty story. Yokoi hid from the population around him. Onoda continued to operate against it.

## Christopher Knight lived off lake houses

Knight entered the woods in 1986 at age twenty. His camp was not in the remote North Woods. It sat in the Belgrade Lakes and North Pond area, close enough to cabins, dirt roads, and Pine Tree Camp to make a supply route possible.

He did not live off the land. He lived off lake houses.

Knight said he never lit an outdoor fire because smoke could expose him. He moved at night, cooked on stolen propane, kept watches and thermometers, stockpiled food and fuel before snow, and maintained an emergency cache in case he had to abandon the main camp. During winter he rarely left his clearing because tracks could reveal him. He layered sleeping bags, woke during the coldest part of the night, paced to keep blood moving, and valued dry socks over almost everything.[^knight-gq]

That is not slack. Concealment was exacting. Twenty-seven Maine winters without a fire is a brutal achievement.

The food system was still comparatively slack. Knight stole prepared food, propane, clothes, batteries, tools, books, bedding, and hygiene supplies. He told investigators he committed about forty burglaries a year, more than one thousand in total. Those are his claims and investigators’ estimates, not one thousand separately proved cases.[^knight-gq]

People around North Pond changed locks and installed alarms. They found marks near windows, discovered propane tanks emptied or swapped, and lived with the feeling that somebody unseen might be watching. The irony is almost professionally composed: before entering the woods, Knight had installed home and vehicle alarm systems.[^knight-gq]

Game warden Terry Hughes finally caught him leaving Pine Tree Camp on April 4, 2013, after hiding a motion detector behind an ice machine. Knight pleaded guilty to thirteen burglary and theft charges. After completing a specialty-court program, he received five years with all but seven months suspended, three years of probation, and $1,894.75 in restitution to victims of charged and uncharged crimes. The separate $1,125 road order is the part the high court later removed.[^knight-sentence]

The latest reliable public accounts are old. In 2017, Michael Finkel said Knight was living at his mother’s house and wanted privacy. A 2020 *Bangor Daily News* article described Finkel’s book ending with Knight working in his brother’s junkyard. I found no verified recent public update, which appears to be how he wants it.[^knight-now]

## *Alone* is the control experiment

The HISTORY series *Alone* puts experienced survivalists in remote locations with approved clothing, safety equipment, camera gear, and ten selected survival items under the standard format. They build shelter, acquire food, and document themselves. They can request extraction. Production also conducts medical checks and can remove a participant.[^alone]

Those checks measure weight, temperature, blood pressure, heart rate, and blood oxygen while looking for injuries, frostbite, organ or digestive problems, scurvy indicators, and changes in mental condition. The show is produced and medically monitored television. It is still a useful demonstration of the arithmetic: shelter work burns calories; cold burns calories; failed fishing burns calories; and an expert can become medically unsafe while doing nearly everything right.[^alone-safety]

Knight did the hard part psychologically and the easy part calorically.

Stealing required planning, nerve, timing, and the constant risk of capture. It also removed the central burden that breaks *Alone* contestants: producing enough food to replace the energy spent staying alive.

## *Jeremiah Johnson* is the myth

Sydney Pollack’s 1972 film supplies the American mythic version of the man who walks into the wilderness. Johnson arrives unable to fish or build a proper fire. Other people teach him. He makes a home and family. The Army later uses his knowledge, then crosses a Crow burial ground against his warning. His family is killed, and the story becomes a long revenge cycle.

The wilderness is never empty. It contains nations, graves, trade, families, soldiers, customs, and consequences.

The movie is not a straightforward biography. The American Film Institute traces it to the legend surrounding John Johnston, *Crow Killer*, and Vardis Fisher’s novel *Mountain Man*. Pollack and the writers also made the burial-ground crossing the cause of the retaliation, giving the revenge story a specific violation.[^jeremiah]

## The small headline

The question is not how long somebody can live outside society. The question is what system keeps supplying him while he appears to be gone.

Yokoi built a survival system from jungle ecology, craft, and remnants of a destroyed military world. Onoda maintained an armed campaign through training, weapons, gathered food, stolen food, and violence against the people around him. Knight converted nearby camps and lake houses into an involuntary grocery store, propane supplier, library, wardrobe, and hardware department. *Alone* shows what happens when most of that external supply disappears. *Jeremiah Johnson* shows the myth that the land was waiting empty.

Twenty-seven years in the woods became a series of burglaries, a hidden camp, a police recovery operation, a repaired dirt road, a six-justice statutory argument, and a small front-page headline beside a presidential campaign story.

**Hermit does not have to pay for repairs.**

Knight escaped jobs, bills, conversation, television, the internet, and ordinary schedules. He did not escape infrastructure. He did not escape the people stocking the cabins. He did not escape the law.

He escaped the road-repair bill because four judges read the restitution statute more narrowly than the other two.
