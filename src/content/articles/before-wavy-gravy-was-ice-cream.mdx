Wavy Gravy turned 90 in May 2026.

The birthday produced the kind of celebration his life seems to attract. There was music. There was a film screening. There were old friends. There was a benefit attached to it, because apparently even turning 90 can become another way to raise money for something useful. San Francisco proclaimed May 16 Wavy Gravy Day. Seva Foundation celebrated one of its co-founders. The Presidio Theatre screened *Saint Misbehavin'* for the Camp Winnarainbow scholarship fund, followed by a conversation with Wavy, Jahanara Romney, Larry Brilliant, and the filmmakers.

Jahanara had turned 85 a few weeks earlier. Some public records still list her under Bonnie Beecher, the name she used while acting and singing for television. Camp Winnarainbow now describes both founders as retired from official leadership. It also reports that they still come to performances.

They are both still here.

More important, some of the things they built are still here.

There is also the ice cream, which is the joke tucked inside this article's title. Ben & Jerry's **Wavy Gravy** was explicitly named for Hugh Romney's Wavy Gravy persona. The flavor did not give him the name. Ben Cohen came looking for the man who already had it. In the company's account, Cohen approached Wavy in San Francisco nearly two years before the 1993 launch, while Wavy was performing for homeless children. Test pints soon began arriving in smoking dry-ice boxes.

Ben & Jerry's introduced the flavor in 1993. It mixed caramel and cashew Brazil nut ice cream with hazelnut fudge and roasted almonds. The company retired it in 2001, then briefly returned it to scoop shops in 2005. Ben & Jerry's says it admired Wavy's ideals, so the choice was not random celebrity branding. The pint honored a performer whose public identity already joined jokes, music, and nonprofit work.

Wavy Gravy belonged to a recognizable freezer aisle. **Cherry Garcia**, named for Grateful Dead guitarist Jerry Garcia, had debuted in 1987; Ben & Jerry's calls it the first ice cream named for a rock legend. **Phish Food** followed, along with **Bohemian Raspberry**, **Imagine Whirled Peace**, and **Bonnaroo Buzz**. Some survived. Others went to the Flavor Graveyard.

Ben & Jerry's had its own Vermont version of countercultural show business: a renovated gas station, a Cowmobile giving away scoops, music fandom, punning names, public causes, and a foundation funding community projects. It was a company selling pints, not a commune running a camp or feeding a festival. Still, the resemblance helps explain why Wavy's name fit on the carton. Both had learned that humor could draw a crowd and that the crowd could be pointed toward useful work.

A man became a nickname, the nickname became a pint, and the pint went to the company's Flavor Graveyard while the man kept working.

This story takes place before the pint and mostly before the nickname.

The cheap version begins in the mud at Woodstock. A funny-looking man with a microphone promises an impossible breakfast for four hundred thousand people. He talks about frightening drug experiences. He calls the Hog Farm's security crew the Please Force. The crowd laughs. The movie remembers him. The clown nose arrives later and makes the whole life easy to package.

That version starts too late.

By Woodstock, Hugh Romney had already spent more than a decade moving through poetry, theater, comedy, jazz, folk music, improvisation, light shows, communal living, buses, benefits, campuses, and public events. Bonnie had already moved through the Minneapolis folk scene, early Bob Dylan recordings, network television, and a haunting song from *The Twilight Zone*. Bill Graham had already discovered that he could take a room full of strangers, artists, cops, permits, tickets, lights, sound, bathrooms, apples, and trouble and turn the whole mess into a show. Ken Kesey and the Merry Pranksters had already been turning parties into experiments. Chet Helms and Family Dog were building dance culture. The Grateful Dead were moving among these people before anybody knew how many books, documentaries, shirts, box sets, bus tours, and museum displays would eventually be required to explain them.

“The Sixties” makes all of that sound like one thing.

It was not one thing. It was a crowd of people doing different jobs before the historical label made the work look inevitable.

## The street sign comes later

I took two photographs at Haight and Ashbury in the early 2010s. I did not get over to the Grateful Dead house. I got the intersection, the street signs, the white corner building, the storefronts, the overhead wires, and people standing around a place that had already spent decades functioning as a symbol.

The signs are clean and readable. They are also wonderfully useless as an explanation.

By the time an address becomes a destination, the thing that made it important has usually moved, changed shape, become an industry, or died. The Haight was already being sold as a sightseeing route in 1967, while the culture outside the bus windows was still in motion. The people inside it had not finished inventing their work before somebody figured out how to package the tour.

The history was never confined to that corner anyway.

One path came through Greenwich Village. Another came through Minneapolis. Bill Graham came through New York, acting, restaurant work, the Army, and the San Francisco Mime Troupe. Kesey came through Oregon, Stanford, hospitals, writing, psychedelics, and a bus. The Hog Farm took shape near Los Angeles. The ballrooms were in San Francisco. The Shrine was in Los Angeles. The buses crossed the country. Woodstock happened in New York. The network moved because the people moved.

Haight and Ashbury became a useful label after the wires were already crossing.

## Hugh Romney has a weird day

Before Wavy Gravy, there was Hugh Romney.

He served in the Army and studied theater in Boston and New York. His official biography says he graduated from the Neighborhood Playhouse in 1961, but his real education seems to have included every room where a performer could stand up, take a risk, and discover whether strangers would follow him.

He began reading poetry at the Gaslight Café in Greenwich Village in the late 1950s and eventually became its poetry and entertainment director. The Gaslight was a basement room in the same cultural weather as Dave Van Ronk, Bob Dylan, folk revivalists, Beat poetry, comics, jazz musicians, monologists, and people who had not settled on a category.

Hugh figured out that the story surrounding a poem could be more entertaining than the poem. His biography reduces the method to a clean production decision: lose the poems and tell the room about the strange day that produced them.

That move contains much of the later Wavy Gravy mechanism. He could read a crowd, improvise, lower the temperature, and make practical information sound like part of the show. According to his biography, he opened for musicians including Thelonious Monk and John Coltrane. Lenny Bruce briefly managed him. Tiny Tim and Moondog passed through his Phantom Cabaret at the Living Theatre. Bob Dylan was around the Gaslight. Wavy's version even includes Dylan borrowing his typewriter for an early draft of “A Hard Rain's A-Gonna Fall,” which is exactly the kind of claim that belongs to the person telling it instead of a bronze plaque.

The useful point is not that Hugh Romney knew everybody before they were famous. Early-scene biographies become unbearable when every room is treated as a future celebrity holding pen.

The useful point is that the categories were porous. Poetry could become comedy. A jazz bill could hold a monologist. A basement coffeehouse could be a workplace, school, booking office, social network, and temporary home. Performance was not a finished product delivered by a cleanly defined profession. It was something people were working out together in public.

Hugh was already learning how to operate inside that kind of room.

## Come wander with Bonnie

Bonnie Beecher had her own route into the story.

Bob Dylan's official site lists a May 1, 1961 recording at the home of Bonnie Beecher in Minneapolis. The set includes folk songs, traditional material, talking songs, and the kind of loose early performance record that survives because somebody had a machine running in a room where history had not announced itself.

Bonnie is sometimes named as the woman behind “Girl from the North Country.” Other women have been named too. The song does not need to be assigned to one person for Bonnie's place in the early Dylan story to matter. His official archive puts the recording in her home. That is enough.

Then she went into television.

Her screen career was short and unusually concentrated. She appeared in dramas, westerns, science fiction, and prime-time series during the middle of the decade. The credits include *The Fugitive*, *The Invaders*, *Gunsmoke*, *Peyton Place*, and *Star Trek*. In “Spectre of the Gun,” she plays Sylvia inside the episode's broken, dreamlike reconstruction of Tombstone.

Her best role for this article came earlier.

In 1964, Bonnie appeared in *The Twilight Zone* episode “Come Wander with Me.” She plays Mary Rachel and performs the title song, a slow ghost transmission that sounds as if it had been waiting in the woods longer than the television set receiving it. The episode aired near the end of the original series. The song kept traveling after the show ended.

The title is almost too perfect. Come wander with me. Bonnie moved from Minneapolis folk circles into television, then into a life with Hugh Romney, the Hog Farm, buses, community work, Camp Winnarainbow, and Seva. A line written for a television episode became an accidental caption for the next sixty years.

It is tempting to treat television as a strange contrast to the communal life that followed. One is respectable network entertainment. The other is an actual hog farm full of performers, Pranksters, children, buses, animals, and whatever else showed up.

The cleaner reading is that the skills traveled.

Camp Winnarainbow's own history says Jahanara's television acting helped shape its emphasis on performance. Television did not disappear from her life as a discarded false self. It became part of the equipment. Acting, timing, character, music, and the ability to hold attention could be used somewhere besides a studio set.

Bonnie Beecher did not vanish when Jahanara Romney appeared. The two names describe one long working life.

## California was already moving

Hugh went west and worked with the Committee, the San Francisco improvisational theater group. Bonnie was working in Los Angeles. Wavy's biography says they met in 1965 at the restaurant where she worked, after she put peanuts in his hamburger. They married and ended up in a one-room cabin in Sunland, outside Los Angeles.

This is where the geography matters.

Public memory compresses the whole culture into San Francisco, then compresses San Francisco into Haight-Ashbury. Hugh and Bonnie's story keeps slipping out of that frame. New York mattered. Minneapolis mattered. Los Angeles mattered. The San Fernando Valley mattered. Theater mattered as much as rock for a while. The actual Hog Farm did not materialize behind a Victorian house on Haight Street.

At the same time, San Francisco was producing its own unstable machinery.

Bill Graham had become business manager of the San Francisco Mime Troupe. When authorities arrested troupe members after a disputed public performance, Graham organized a legal-defense benefit on November 6, 1965. Lawrence Ferlinghetti, Allen Ginsberg, the Fugs, Jefferson Airplane, John Handy, and others were on the bill. Graham later described watching strangers enter the room and begin dancing together as one of the most significant moments of his theatrical life.

He had discovered his job.

Chet Helms and Family Dog were developing another branch of ballroom culture. Kesey and the Merry Pranksters were staging Acid Tests, events that were party, performance, sound experiment, social experiment, and unstable laboratory. The band becoming the Grateful Dead was part of that environment, though the exact first Acid Test performance carries enough dispute to make a clean origin sentence suspicious.

<SceneNetwork />

No one person created this. Graham did not invent the culture. Kesey did not invent the culture. The Dead did not invent the culture. Haight-Ashbury did not contain the culture. The Summer of Love did not start the culture.

The public label arrived after enough separate behaviors had begun to look connected.

That is also where Hunter S. Thompson enters at the edge of the frame. During the middle years of the decade, he was spending time around California Hells Angels for the reporting that became *Hell's Angels*. That is another part of the room, and it needs more than a clean good-bikers/bad-bikers sentence. For now, it is enough to note that Thompson was standing in another corner of the developing world, taking notes while Hugh Romney was figuring out what to do with a microphone. The later HobFarm article [*Fear and Loathing After the American Dream*](/articles/fear-and-loathing-after-the-american-dream/) follows Thompson into a different landscape.

## Forty people and forty hogs

Then the bus arrived.

Wavy's official history says Hugh and Bonnie were living in their one-room cabin when the Merry Prankster bus showed up and deposited roughly forty more people into their lives. The number has the shape of a story polished by repetition, but the operational problem is clear enough. A small place suddenly contained far too many humans.

A nearby mountaintop farm needed caretakers after the man tending it suffered a stroke. The group could live there without rent if they looked after forty hogs.

The people eventually outnumbered the pigs.

The name stayed.

The Hog Farm was a commune. It also became an entertainment organization, light-show crew, traveling event, antiwar performance, temporary family, food system, and rolling answer to the question of where everybody was going to sleep.

Wavy's biography says the group performed light shows and Energy Games at the Shrine in Los Angeles. The biography connects those events to major bands, but the available source does not provide show-by-show production records, so the list can stay in his account rather than becoming a definitive venue calendar. The larger transformation is visible without it. The farm was becoming portable. Communal life and event production were feeding each other.

The buses carried the system across the country. The Hog Farm performed on campuses and moved through antiwar culture, music culture, public happenings, and whatever category could hold a painted school bus full of people trying to make the next stop work.

This is the part that makes Woodstock comprehensible.

The Hog Farm did not arrive in New York as a random group selected because it looked sufficiently colorful. It had spent years learning how to move, improvise, gather people, stage things, feed people, and absorb chaos without immediately becoming another source of chaos.

### A small farm-name problem

There is a stupid little reason I have always liked the name Hog Farm: HobFarm rhymes with it.

That is not where HobFarm comes from. In folklore collected around the North York Moors, a hob is a household or farm spirit known for helping with the work. When I named this place, that fit the AI tools and automation systems I was building around. The metaphor has become less central since I figured out that I am not trying to become a SaaS engineer, but the name still works and I am keeping it.

There is no direct lineage. There is one letter, a rhyme, and an accidental sympathy with the idea that somebody or something still has to do the work.

## Somebody has to make the thing work

Bill Graham is important here because he represents another answer to the same developing problem.

How do you make public assembly function?

His foundation's biography is useful because it does not describe the answer only in terms of genius, money, or famous bookings. It describes work. Graham booked the room. He dealt with permits and police. He sold tickets. He improved sound and lights. He put up posters. He built a ticket-distribution network through local stores. He took tickets at the door. He cleaned bathrooms between sets. He put apples at the entrance.

The apples matter.

They put Graham closer to the subject of this article than the usual promoter mythology does. He was not simply monetizing a spontaneous culture from outside it. His first major organizing breakthrough was a legal-defense benefit for radical theater. Benefits stayed inside his work. Hospitality stayed inside his work. Control stayed inside his work too. He could be demanding, combative, paternal, theatrical, generous, furious, and professionally obsessed with the room.

The Hog Farm asked how a community could care for people inside a chaotic event.

Graham asked how the event could reliably become a show.

Those are different questions. Modern live culture needed both.

This is also why the story eventually reaches people like John Scher and Peter Shapiro, though not here. Graham built a professional West Coast concert system. Scher became a major East Coast promoter with deep Grateful Dead history. Shapiro later built Wetlands and Brooklyn Bowl work from another generation of the broad live-music ecosystem. [*From Wetlands to the Wash*](/articles/from-wetlands-to-the-wash/) follows that line into a room on the Las Vegas Strip.

Here we are staying earlier, while the jobs were still becoming jobs.

## Take the farm on the road

A culture becomes durable when it learns how to move.

The Hog Farm's bus caravan carried more than people. It carried a reproducible event grammar. Arrive. Find the room or field. Unload. Build a show. Feed somebody. Make contact with local people. Turn the audience into participants. Deal with the person who is frightened, lost, high, angry, hungry, or simply standing in the wrong place. Leave enough of a system behind that the next thing can happen.

Not every stop worked. Communal history is full of broken arrangements, gendered labor, money problems, health problems, bad decisions, interpersonal damage, and mythology built by the people who survived long enough to narrate it. A painted bus is not proof of a frictionless utopia.

What matters for the Woodstock story is accumulated practice.

The Hog Farm had already been functioning at the unstable boundary between performance and logistics. It had learned that a crowd is not one creature. It is thousands of separate problems arriving at once. The person on the microphone may need to be an announcer, comic, navigator, rumor-control system, and temporary authority within the same minute.

Hugh Romney had been training for that role since the Gaslight.

## My name is Hugh Romney

When Hugh appears in the *Woodstock* film, he introduces himself plainly by name.

> “I'm with the Hog Farm.”

He was not Wavy Gravy yet.

The festival had asked the Hog Farm to help with security. The group renamed the assignment the Please Force. Hugh became the Please Chief. The joke was good enough to survive. The work behind it was better.

The Please Force was not a conventional security company. It was part of a larger volunteer operation dealing with crowd movement, information, food, distress, and an event that had exceeded the scale of its plan. The Hog Farm ran or helped run free kitchens. Smithsonian food-history material credits Lisa Law and Hugh Romney with organizing volunteers who fed people granola, brown rice, and vegetables. The group helped people through frightening drug experiences. Hugh made stage announcements. Volunteers tried to get useful information into a crowd so large that every basic task had become a distribution problem.

The famous breakfast speech contains the model in miniature. The joke gets attention. The next sentence explains the constraint. It will not be steak and eggs, but it will be food. Then Hugh names the Hog Farm, the Ohio Mountain Family, the Pranksters, and everybody volunteering in the free kitchens. He does not claim one group is rescuing a helpless audience.

> “We're all feeding each other.”

That sentence is less sentimental when you remember that people actually had to cook, carry, divide, and distribute the food. A communal idea becomes real only when somebody finds the pots.

Then Hugh acknowledges the event for what it is:

> “There's always a little bit of heaven in a disaster area.”

The line works because he does not deny the disaster area. Woodstock had music and extraordinary collective behavior. It also had failed planning, mud, shortages, traffic, medical problems, sanitation strain, and far more people than the site was prepared to hold. The Hog Farm's contribution becomes more impressive when the infrastructure failure remains visible.

The hamburger aside is part of the same method. A vendor's stand had burned, but he still had some food. Hugh routes a few hungry people toward a person with hamburgers left to sell. It is not a manifesto. It is a man using a microphone to solve the small problem in front of him.

That practical looseness is one reason he remains interesting. The available solution mattered more than ideological purity.

### From distress to capacity

The strongest mechanism may be the least famous one.

Hugh talks about people arriving at the Hog Farm's area in frightening psychological distress. He gives their experience a gentler name, then describes what happens after they settle down. Within roughly half an hour, he says, some of the people leaving the section begin caring for others arriving in the same condition.

The language is comic and loose. The mechanism is serious.

A person enters as a problem. The system stabilizes that person. The person regains agency. The former patient becomes part of the support network.

<PleaseForceLoop />

It is tempting to call this harm reduction. Parts of the behavior fit the modern term, especially reducing panic, providing a safer setting, and meeting people where they were. The Hog Farm was not operating from a current clinical protocol, and Hugh's acid announcement makes that limit obvious.

He tells the crowd that alarming reports about tainted acid are wrong and that people should stop panicking. It is a startling statement to hear now. It may have calmed people. It also communicated certainty he could not possess across an enormous festival.

That moment belongs in the record because it keeps the Please Force human. Improvisation can be compassionate and wrong at the same time. A person working a disaster-size crowd makes decisions with partial information, limited tools, and no clean way to reach everyone.

The Please Force was not magic. It was a social patch applied by people with unusual experience to an event whose formal systems were failing.

Humor was one of the tools.

## Then B.B. King names him

Two weeks later, at the Texas International Pop Festival in Lewisville, Hugh Romney became Wavy Gravy.

At least, that is how Hugh told it.

The Texas State Historical Association dates the festival to August 30 through September 1, 1969. Its paid stage stood by the Dallas International Motor Speedway. A free stage operated several miles away near Lewisville Lake. Wavy's account in Seva's anniversary book places him lying on that free stage when B.B. King arrived to play. King addressed him with the new name and, instead of making him move, set him against the amplifier and played around him. Hugh kept the name. Years later, he said, children in an improvisation class responded to it so quickly that their professors urged him not to give it back.

The story is a recollection, not a recording of the exchange. It still has a secure frame: the named festival happened in Lewisville on those dates, B.B. King was on its bill, and local history also associates the naming with the event.

It is a perfect historical accident because it clarifies how late the public character arrived.

Woodstock did not summon Wavy Gravy. Woodstock documented Hugh Romney near the end of a long apprenticeship. The name came after the field test.

## The work keeps changing clothes

A historical era ends neatly only in documentary editing.

The people keep waking up the next morning.

Wavy used clowning in hospitals, political theater, protests, benefits, and public appearances. The clown became a way to reach children, lower defenses, attract attention, and sometimes change the temperature around authority. He ran Nobody for President campaigns. He made art. He kept organizing musicians around causes.

The larger proof of continuity is institutional.

Seva Foundation was founded in 1978 by a group that included Wavy and Larry Brilliant. It turned parts of the same network of service, medicine, benefits, spiritual practice, and musician relationships toward restoring sight and supporting eye care.

Camp Winnarainbow grew from Wavy and Jahanara's performance histories and communal work. Camp's official history dates its founding to 1975. Wavy's own current biography says 1979. The institution's date is the stronger choice because it is the camp's account of its own operating history, and a 2026 Camp dispatch repeats 1975. The four-year disagreement is preserved here instead of silently blended.

The important point is that the camp has lasted roughly half a century.

<WorkContinues />

Camp teaches circus and performance skills, but the skills are attached to community, timing, attention, confidence, cooperation, and the practical fact that a group of people has to share a place. Jahanara's television background and Wavy's performance history both became part of the curriculum.

The Hog Farm's old operating logic keeps reappearing:

- make the gathering work;
- use performance to hold attention;
- turn participants into helpers;
- connect musicians to practical needs;
- make service feel like an event instead of a lecture;
- leave behind an institution when possible.

That is why this article should not end by asking whatever happened to Wavy Gravy.

Nothing happened to him in the historical-biography sense. He did not vanish when the calendar stopped saying 1969. He kept doing things.

Jahanara did too.

In 2026 they appeared together at a birthday film screening and benefit connected to an institution that grew from their working life. Wavy was 90. Jahanara was 85. The people around them were not reenacting Woodstock. They were raising money for work still happening now.

## The brochure comes later

Today, nearly every musical culture can have its own festival lane. There are destination festivals, urban festivals, camping festivals, touring packages, nostalgia packages, cruises, branded weekends, and gatherings built around one band's audience. Different machines arrive already legible as things a person can buy a ticket to attend.

That is not a complaint. A named format can make a culture repeatable. It can create work, safety plans, production standards, communities, archives, and places for people to find one another. It can also become expensive, corporate, overbuilt, nostalgic, extractive, or indistinguishable from tourism. Usually it becomes several of those things at once.

The mistake is believing the label created the behavior.

Before the festival category, people put incompatible things in the same room to see what happened. Before the tourist intersection, people rented halls, printed handbills, ran cables, painted buses, fed strangers, argued with authorities, made bad decisions, learned from some of them, and discovered jobs that did not have stable names.

[*From Wetlands to the Wash*](/articles/from-wetlands-to-the-wash/) ends inside a modern concert venue on the Las Vegas Strip, where the room has a corporate structure, technical systems, scheduled touring acts, hospitality systems, a direct history through Wetlands, and a flood channel running under the property.

This article goes backward from that machine.

Bill Graham was learning how to make the room work. Hugh Romney was learning how to make the crowd listen. Bonnie Beecher was carrying performance from a television studio into a community. The Hog Farm was learning how to turn a group of people into a moving support system. Kesey and the Pranksters were learning how to make the audience part of the event. Musicians were learning how long a song could become when the room changed around it.

None of them knew exactly what all of this would become.

The label came later. The brochure came later. The tourist sign came later. The ice cream came much later.

First somebody had to book the room, drive the bus, rig the lights, calm the panic, cook the rice, and feed the crowd.
