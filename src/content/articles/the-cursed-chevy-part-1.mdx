I do not believe in curses. I do own a 1969 Chevrolet van that came from a hot rod shop after the shop burned to the ground.

In 2026 it is white, or mostly white, with broad patches of paint peeling away to reveal blue underneath. The windshield is covered from the inside. The tires are old. Chocks keep it where eight years of neglect have already put it. It sits in a Henderson driveway looking cursed before anybody hears the story.

The evidence has been accumulating for thirteen years.

## The van next door

In 2013 my uncle-in-law ran a motorcycle shop in San Diego. He was a Harley mechanic. Next door was a hot rod shop, and the owner had an old Chevrolet van sitting around.

My reasons for wanting it were not complicated. It was a Chevrolet. It was a 1969. It had a V8. It was cool.

I kept asking if he would sell it. He kept saying no.

Then the hot rod shop caught fire and burned down. My uncle-in-law's shop was spared. The van was not damaged by the fire either. I do not claim that the van caused the fire. I will only note that after the fire, its owner finally decided to sell it.

This should have been a warning. Instead, it looked like an opportunity.

The van did not run. Its California registration had not been paid since 2006. There was no title.

Buying it was the inexpensive part. Making it legally mine required repeated trips through the California paperwork system and repeated payments measured in hundreds of dollars. Eventually I had a legitimate title, current registration, insurance, and a street-legal vehicle. Before I parked it in 2014, I put it on California non-operation status.

Every curse so far has eventually turned out to have an invoice.

The paperwork was only one layer. A previous owner, before the man who sold it to me, had tried to turn the panel van into a camper. Large holes had been cut into both sides and filled with 1970s-style camper windows. I did not want them. I removed the windows and closed the openings, returning the body toward a panel-van configuration.

That does not make every panel or seam on it factory original. It means I reversed one particularly obvious piece of camper surgery.

The engine was another inheritance. It is not original to the van. Years ago I ran the identifying numbers and remember tracing the V8 to a 1971 El Camino. I no longer have a casting number or suffix code here that would let me verify the exact engine, so that remains my recollection rather than a factory fact.

General Motors' [1969 G-Van information kit](https://www.gm.com/content/dam/company/no_search/heritage-archive-docs/vehicle-information-kits/chevrolet/1969-Chevrolet-G-Van.pdf) shows that G10 panel vans were offered in 90- and 108-inch wheelbases, with six-cylinder and V8 models in the range. It cannot tell me what this particular van left the factory with after decades of swaps and alterations.

## Promoted from dead van

I did enough work to get it running. That phrase sounds more complete than it was.

Once the engine ran well enough to attempt a trip, I drove the van from San Diego to Santa Ana. It was titled, registered, insured, and legal by then. It was also a vehicle that had only recently been promoted from dead van.

It sputtered. It stalled. Sometimes it did not want to restart. The brakes and steering were old. Every change in engine sound felt like information I did not want to receive at freeway speed.

It made the trip with relatively little actual catastrophe.

That drive established a distinction that has followed the project ever since. Getting an old vehicle to move and fixing it are different jobs.

## The Stanton episode

I found a hot rod shop in Stanton and tried to do the next stage properly. Before I brought the van over, I sent the owner photographs, diagrams, and explanations of what I wanted. He seemed receptive.

I also suggested a trade arrangement, which was a mistake. The shop needed a website. I could build websites and produce video and other media about the work. On paper, I could contribute the thing I knew how to do while the shop contributed the thing it knew how to do.

This is how a project acquires two unfinished projects.

The shop used an impressive classic Mustang as evidence of its custom work. I saw the Mustang and heard about the project while I was there. I later learned information suggesting that its owner was unhappy with how long the build had taken and what it had cost. I cannot independently settle that customer's story, and I do not need to. My experience with the Chevrolet supplied enough evidence of its own.

I provided plans. I spent money. Progress was slow and incomplete. The van remained partially disassembled while the working relationship deteriorated. Eventually I decided to remove it and take it somewhere else.

Then came the dashboard.

The shop owner had once told me about paint mixed for another customer's car. The customer wanted blue. The result was too purple, so the customer rejected it.

The day before I picked up my van, the dashboard was painted with that rejected paint. I had not asked for a purple dashboard. The surrounding interior was blue. Apparently something visible had to happen before pickup, and another customer's unwanted color was available.

After Stanton, I had the van towed to another shop. That shop was good to work with. They actually helped. The van became functional and drivable again, even though it still ran badly much of the time.

That shop belongs in the story because the problem was never that every mechanic was crooked or every shop was incompetent. The problem was this particular chain of old machinery, old modifications, limited resources, and people who did or did not complete the work in front of them.

## Four years in Corona

In 2014 my partner and I decided to travel to Vietnam. The van went into storage in Corona, California. It stayed there until 2018.

After I returned in 2016, I tried again. A shop in Corona worked on the stalling and running problem. That period produced the Holley four-barrel carburetor and Edelbrock intake manifold now on the engine. The stalling improved, but the engine never became fully sorted. The Holley has continued to pop.

That symptom could come from more than one cause. The fuel system, ignition, engine health, and valvetrain all deserve inspection by somebody working from measurements instead of a thirteen-year story. The diagnosis belongs in a later article, after there has been a diagnosis.

In 2018 I had the van transported from Corona to Henderson. It went into the driveway.

When I moved into the house where it later sat, the property manager asked whether the old van was also going to become a feature in the driveway. I said probably not.

It became a feature in the driveway.

Across the street sits another similarly old, similarly dormant Volkswagen van, so the Chevrolet arguably conforms to the neighborhood aesthetic.

For eight years my van has performed one job reliably: making the house look like it probably is not worth robbing. It makes it look like I spend all my money on old cars. I actually spend all my money on computers, which is why the van still looks like this.

## Project entropy

I build software, websites, AI systems, and other things that can become technically complicated very quickly. An old Chevrolet can seem refreshingly legible by comparison. Get the part. Remove the bolts. Install the replacement. Connect the hoses or wires. Adjust it. Test it.

Much of the van really is understandable in those terms. Understanding the work is not the same as having the physical capability to do it.

Vehicle work can require a lift, heavy jack stands, specialty pullers, presses, brake hardware, alignment equipment, an engine hoist, fabrication tools, welding equipment, and enough space to leave a 57-year-old van disassembled while the next problem is resolved. It requires time and a body willing to do the work. At several points it requires knowledge from people whose trade this actually is.

I have more garage space now than I did in 2013. I have friends with useful skills. Henderson and the larger Las Vegas area have a substantial automotive ecosystem. I can learn and do a meaningful part of the work. Some jobs should still go to people who already own the equipment and know how to use it.

That is the real mechanism under the curse. An old project vehicle depends on money, tools, workspace, transportation, specialized labor, time, and other people's competence. If any one of those disappears, the project waits. While it waits, fuel ages, seals dry, tires deteriorate, wires corrode, and every dormant year adds work to the eventual restart.

The curse is project entropy.

## Why I kept it

People have offered to buy the van. Old Chevrolet panel vans are visible even when they do not move, and people who see this one tend to like it. I have come close to selling it several times.

I keep deciding against it.

I rarely see another van like it. It is a 1969 Chevrolet panel van with a V8, rear barn doors, a bizarre history, and a lot of possibility. Selling it would solve the immediate problem by permanently eliminating the project. I do not want to eliminate it. I still want the van.

There is some historical support for blaming imaginary creatures. The [Royal Air Force Museum's account of gremlin folklore](https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/midlands/things-to-see-and-do/exhibitions-2/aviation-lucky-charms-mascots/lucky-gremlins/) describes the little spirits air crews blamed for inexplicable aircraft failures during the Second World War. RAF pilot Roald Dahl used them in *The Gremlins*, published in 1943.

My gremlins have specialized in intermittent fuel trouble, questionable wiring, California paperwork, shop delays, storage bills, purple paint, and timing. I do not actually think the van is cursed. Its failures have physical causes. Its delays have human causes. The curse is simply a useful name for all of them arriving in sequence.

## The van I want

I finally have a clearer idea of the finished vehicle.

I want a mild, useful high-clearance 4x4 conversion. It does not need to be an extreme off-road machine. It should handle normal road travel and fuel stops, rough roads, desert tracks, campsites, and snow without turning every trip into another rescue operation.

Inside, I want a modular sleeper and hauler. The van can be a bedroom, a small living room, or an open cargo box depending on the trip. The rear barn doors can open into an outdoor living space. A small trailer may eventually carry infrastructure such as a kitchen, bathroom, and supplies, instead of permanently filling the van with RV equipment.

That is an aspiration, not an engineering specification.

A useful precedent already exists. Machines Inc documented [a family-owned 1969 G10 converted to V8 power and four-wheel drive](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zjl04zcyJpQ). [Engine Swap Depot's report](https://engineswapdepot.com/?p=93681) lists a 350 small-block Chevrolet, TH350 automatic, NP205 transfer case, Dana 44 front axle, GM 12-bolt rear axle, and 31-inch tires in that build.

I am not adopting that list as my shopping cart. The reference van proves that a nearly identical body can become the kind of useful overland vehicle I have in mind. My van will need its own inspection, measurements, budget, tradeoffs, and engineering choices.

It turns 60 in 2029. That gives the project a destination: make the Cursed Chevy function as a real vehicle again by its 60th anniversary.

The preferred result includes the 4x4 conversion and a substantial version of the sleeper system. The minimum success is less glamorous and more important. It should start reliably, run reliably, stop safely, steer safely, have working lights and electrical systems, wear usable tires, and make a meaningful road trip.

## First, make it movable

The immediate sequence is tires, then the fuel system, then enough engine diagnosis to learn what I actually own. The aging wiring and lights need inspection. Brakes and steering need a competent shop with a lift and the correct equipment. Once the van is safely mobile, I can start evaluating the larger chassis and 4x4 work.

I may do some of that with friends. I may put tires on it and have it transported directly to a shop for fuel and brake work. Different specialists may handle different stages. I do not know yet, and pretending otherwise would be how this project accumulates another elaborate plan instead of motion.

There is no blank-check restoration budget waiting for the weather to change. This will happen in stages. It is also August in the Henderson desert, when a driveway and a metal vehicle can turn ordinary work into a heat problem. Substantial outdoor work can wait until the season stops trying to cook whoever touches the van.

For now, the Cursed Chevy remains in the driveway. First it needs tires. Then fuel. Then electricity. Then brakes and steering. After that, we find out how much van is still in the van.
