Alice found the table before she found the words for what was wrong with it.

It sat exactly where the hedge maze gave up trying to be a hedge maze — four mismatched chairs, a service for six, and a tablecloth so white it looked like it had never once touched Wonderland's dirt. Beyond the table: more hedge, more looping green, more Wonderland doing what Wonderland always did, which was insist that it went on forever in every direction.

Except due east, if you counted carefully, if you counted at all, there were exactly forty more feet of hedge before the color ran out.

"There's a desert back there," Alice said, not for the first time.

The March Hare, presiding over a pot of something that had stopped being tea around the third refill, did not look up. "There's no such thing as 'back there.' There's only 'here,' and 'more here,' and occasionally, for people who insist on being tiresome about it, 'somewhere you should not be asking about.'"

"I've seen it. Cobalt sky. Rust-colored rock. A wind that smells like a struck match."

"You've seen the inside of your own skull, more likely. Eighteen does strange things to a Wonderland girl. Have a seat. Have a cup. Have literally anything except opinions about geography."

Chester, who had been quiet in the way he got quiet before he said something that mattered, set his own cup down without drinking from it. "She's not wrong, you know. I've smelled it too."

That got the Hare to look up. Just for a second. Just long enough for Alice to see something pass behind his eyes that wasn't whimsy at all — something closer to the look of a man who has been told, very firmly, not to mention the fire behind the curtain.

"You," the Hare said to Chester, "are new. New things believe all sorts of nonsense before Wonderland teaches them better."

"I've been here as long as she has."

"Then you've had exactly as long to learn better, and exactly as little success at it."

Alice stood. The tablecloth didn't move — nothing at this table ever moved unless the table allowed it, which was its own kind of answer — and she walked the forty feet due east with Chester half a step behind her, the way he always was: close enough to matter, far enough to let her be the one who got there first.

The hedge did not thin. It ended. One moment there was green, structured, agreeable Wonderland, groomed into shapes that had opinions about how a garden should behave. The next moment there was heat, and distance, and a horizon the color of a bruise just starting to heal, and underneath all of it, unmistakably, sand.

Behind them, faint over the too-tended hedges, the Hare's voice carried on entertaining a table that no longer had anyone at it worth entertaining.

"There," Alice said. "That's the part nobody wants to explain."

Chester didn't answer right away. He was looking past her, past the boundary, at the rust and the cobalt and the long flat nothing that any sensible resident of Wonderland would have sworn, under oath, did not exist.

"Well," he said eventually. "I suppose we'd better go explain it to ourselves, then."

They stepped through.
