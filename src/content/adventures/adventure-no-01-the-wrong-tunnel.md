Ciryl Spade was smoking chive when the giant Clubs smelled the colony.

They came through the outer forest nose-first, three enormous bears walking upright beneath mismatched armor. One wore a rusted casino sign across his shoulders. Another had built a belt from luggage straps and small brass doors. The largest carried no weapon at all. He was carrying half a rotten tree because something living inside it smelled edible.

They were not looking for Spades.

They were not looking down.

Ciryl sat behind a swollen root above the mycelium bed, his boots hanging over the far side where the supervisor could not see them. He drew green smoke through a short clay pipe packed with dried purple chive flowers.

The hollow stems went into soup. The flowers went into pipes. Hedgehogs had arranged things this way before anyone Ciryl knew had been born.

Below him, eleven Spades worked inside the opened colony. They trimmed heat caps, patched ruptured water threads, and scraped pale larvae from the nutrient bulbs before the larvae grew teeth. The colony breathed beneath them in slow white pulses. Each pulse pushed water uphill toward the forest settlements and carried waste back down through a different set of roots.

The first warning came when every filter mushroom snapped shut.

The second came when the ferns parted around a broad brown chest.

The Spades stopped working.

One curled.

The others followed.

Heads tucked down. Legs vanished. Quills rose. Within seconds, the entire maintenance crew had become a collection of spiked brown balls scattered across the glowing fungus.

The giant Clubs continued walking.

The one in the casino sign crouched and sniffed the foreman. He nudged him with one thick finger. The foreman rolled half a turn and stopped against a nutrient bulb.

The Club tried again.

A quill went through the leather around his knuckle.

He grunted, sucked the blood from his hand, and lost interest.

The others reached the same conclusion after several similarly unsuccessful experiments. Hedgehogs were not food. More importantly, they were unpleasant to move.

The Clubs worked around them.

One tore open a bulb and drank the warm green fluid inside. Another dug both hands beneath a fungus shelf and came up with a fistful of larvae. The largest bear dropped his tree, found the workers' lunch baskets, and ate two baskets before discovering they were not part of the lunch.

Ciryl took another pull from the pipe.

No one had noticed him.

No one would be repairing anything while the Clubs remained in the colony and his coworkers remained shaped like weatherproof luggage.

The bears did not appear to be leaving. They had found a dense patch of sweet rot beneath the cooling bed and begun discussing whether it belonged to anyone. Their discussion involved eating it while they decided.

Ciryl tapped the pipe against the root and watched the last purple ember fall into the moss.

He would come back when everybody stopped being round.

He climbed down the far side of the root and entered the nearest maintenance burrow.

That was the whole decision.

It was not brave. It was not cowardly. It was the decision of a worker whose shift had become impossible and whose lunch had just been eaten by a stranger wearing a building.

The tunnel had another opinion.

Ciryl knew the colony routes. Every Spade on the crew did. The first fork ran toward the water cisterns. The second bent beneath the larval beds. The third climbed around a root knot and opened near the workers' shelter.

The route in front of him did none of those things.

He crawled past the first fork because it smelled too strongly of iron. He avoided the second because the wall appeared to be breathing in a judgmental way. At the third he stopped, listened to the vibration in the mycelium, and chose left.

A sober hedgehog would have chosen right.

Ciryl was not severely impaired. The chive had merely improved the confidence of several bad ideas.

He kept moving.

The living tunnel narrowed around his shoulders, then widened into old white growth that had not been maintained in years. The mycelium here pulsed out of rhythm with the colony. Its light arrived in small groups, as though signals from somewhere far away were getting lost and reaching him together.

Ciryl should have turned back.

He heard the dull impact of something heavy aboveground and imagined the giant Clubs entering the burrows after him. Bears were not known for tunneling, but the chive suggested they might have learned.

He used his folding shovel to widen a crack between two root plates.

Cold air touched his face.

Ciryl stopped.

Nothing in the outer forest produced air that cold without first producing a smell. Rain smelled of copper leaves. Ice pools smelled blue. Winter fungi smelled like old paper and milk.

The air beyond the crack smelled of nothing.

He pushed the shovel through.

The root wall opened without tearing. Its soft white fibers folded away from the blade and turned inward, revealing a hole full of hard black-blue sky.

Ciryl stared at it for a while.

Then he crawled through.

He emerged onto a narrow shelf of dark stone high above anything he recognized.

Wind struck his wet coat and passed through it. The cold did not settle on his fur. It entered every space between the hairs at once.

Ciryl pressed himself against the mountain.

Wonderland lay below him.

The outer forest spread beneath the ledge in a dark green mass, larger than any territory shown on the maintenance maps. Beyond it, other forests climbed and folded through valleys. Farther still, the labyrinth-city filled the low country with wet light. Its towers bent over one another. Its streets wound like exposed nerves. Bridges, resorts, gardens, walls, pipes, and luminous fungal districts continued until the distance turned them into a single breathing surface.

At the center, the Queen's House burned dark red and gold.

Ciryl had always known the House was the center of Wonderland.

He had never understood that a center required an edge.

A sound passed through the mountain. It was too low to hear properly, but his quills felt it.

Ciryl turned.

On the far side of the peak, the world continued without Wonderland.

He saw a plain stripped of softness. Pale ground reached toward a horizon drawn too straight. Structures repeated across the distance in arrangements he could not read. Some looked built. Some looked grown. A hard white light moved through them in perfect bands.

Nothing dripped.

Nothing swelled.

Nothing appeared to breathe.

Ciryl blinked until his eyes watered.

The chive was good, but it had never invented a horizon before.

Something struck the stone beside him with a clear musical crack.

A transparent shard stood upright in the rock.

Another landed beyond it. Then six more. They did not fall like rain. They arrived from several directions at once, turning in the air before choosing where to enter the mountain.

The storm made no clouds.

Glass needles passed across the peak in bright sheets. Some vanished into older layers of crystal already buried beneath the dark surface. Others branched where they landed, building small clear structures that joined the mountain as though returning to a larger body.

Ciryl felt a sting between his shoulder quills.

He reached back.

A crystal branch had formed across them.

It looked like ice. It did not behave like ice.

The branch divided into six precise arms. Each arm found another quill and attached itself. A second structure grew near his collar. His damp coat stiffened as transparent lines organized the moisture in its fabric.

Ciryl slapped at the growth.

The crystal answered with a thin ringing note.

Across the peak, several buried structures rang back.

The entire mountain was listening.

Ciryl threw himself toward the hole.

The opening had moved three feet to the left.

He crawled across the ledge while more glass struck around him. His breath hardened in front of his mouth and remained there, a small geometric cloud suspended in the air.

He reached the root-lined opening, shoved his head through, and pulled until the mountain released his coat.

The tunnel folded.

Ciryl fell through white fibers, warm mud, red vapor, and a darkness full of distant machinery. For a moment he was certain he had become too large for his own skeleton. Then the ground struck him from below.

He came out beneath a split root near the colony.

The giant Clubs were gone.

So were the Spades.

The mycelium bed lay open and damaged. Nutrient fluid pooled in the moss. Broken transport threads continued pulsing weakly, sending water into nowhere. Several edible caps had pushed themselves free of the ruptured network and were beginning to spoil.

A green girl in a purple dress was cutting them from the bed.

She worked with a narrow fungal knife, removing the false gills before each mushroom could close around her fingers. Her pale apron was clean except for damp soil along one edge. Melting hearts, diamonds, clubs, and spades softened into the stitching of her clothes. A large purple bow sat above her short black hair.

Beside her, occupying most of an empty harvest basket, sat the largest blue-gray cat Ciryl had ever seen.

The cat looked at him.

Ciryl looked at the cat.

The cat's amber eyes moved to the crystal growth between Ciryl's quills.

His expression became slightly less bored.

The girl noticed.

She turned and found Ciryl half beneath the root, wet with mud, smoking faintly from one pocket, and carrying a glass structure across his back.

"Don't move," she said.

Ciryl froze.

"I wasn't moving."

"You were trying to crawl backward."

"That is a kind of not being here."

She set down the mushroom and approached him.

Ciryl pulled his folding shovel against his chest.

"Those belong to the colony," he said.

"They belonged to the colony. The feeding threads are cut. In twenty minutes they belong to rot flies."

She pointed toward his shoulder.

"That belongs nowhere I've seen."

Ciryl tried to look over his own back. The movement tightened the crystal around his quills.

"It is ice."

The girl crouched beside him.

"Ice melts."

"It was very cold."

"Lots of mistakes are cold."

She opened her basket and selected an orange heat cap. Its underside glowed with a low steady warmth. She brought it toward the crystal.

"Don't," said the cat.

Ciryl stared at him.

The girl's hand stopped.

"You could have said that sooner," she told the cat.

"You had not yet begun doing the wrong thing."

The cat stepped out of the basket. He landed with enough weight to press water from the moss around his paws. Up close he was even larger, broad-faced and plush, with a short thick tail and the settled authority of something that had never been required to hurry.

He sniffed the crystal.

His whiskers disappeared.

Ciryl made a sound he would later deny.

The whiskers returned.

"Mountain weather," the cat said.

The girl looked at him. "There are no mountains close enough for this to be mountain weather."

The cat's mouth curved into a slow sarcastic grin.

"Then he must have gone farther than close."

Ciryl clutched the shovel tighter.

"I took a tunnel."

"Most people do," said the cat.

"It opened on a peak. I could see the city. All of it. And there was something beyond the mountains."

The girl looked back at the crystal.

"How much chive did you smoke?"

"A normal break amount."

Smoke rose from the pocket of his work coat.

She pointed at it.

Ciryl slapped both hands against the fabric until the ember died.

"The mountain was real," he said.

"The freezing was real," she replied. "The rest is pending."

The cat placed one heavy paw against the ground beside Ciryl and began to purr.

The sound passed through the moss and into Ciryl's bones. The crystal answered. Thin notes trembled through its branches. Several joints brightened where one geometric arm met another.

"Those," said the cat.

The girl drew her knife. "Cut them?"

"In that order."

"What order?"

"The one I am about to indicate."

His tail tapped the first joint.

She slipped the blade beneath it and twisted. The crystal snapped with a sound like a tiny bell. Half the structure loosened from Ciryl's quills.

The cat indicated the next point.

"How do you know this?" the girl asked.

"I have been cold before."

"This isn't ordinary cold."

"Neither are the mountains."

She broke the second joint, then a third. The structure sagged. Ciryl felt his quills separate for the first time since the peak.

"Do the storms happen often?" he asked.

The cat looked at him.

"Often enough to make mountains."

The girl paused.

"Snow makes mountains look larger."

"Snow eventually leaves."

The final branch came free in one transparent piece. The girl lowered it onto a broad mushroom cap. The cap curled away from it.

Ciryl touched the bare patch between his quills.

"You believe me now."

"I believe you were somewhere this grows," she said. "That is not the same thing."

"It tried to build me into the mountain."

The cat sat down.

"Mountains are rarely finished."

Ciryl looked around the ruined colony. "Where is everyone?"

The girl's attention shifted from the crystal to the empty work site.

"How many?"

"Eleven. Twelve with me."

She stood and wiped the knife on a strip of moss.

"No bodies. Three drainage burrows used recently. Quill marks under the north root. Somebody dragged a tool case uphill."

Ciryl stared at the ground. He saw mud, broken fibers, and the green stain left by the Clubs.

"You can see all that?"

"You maintain systems. I forage through them."

She returned the knife to her apron.

"I'm Alice. That is Chester."

The cat closed his eyes.

"Ciryl Spade."

"We know," said Chester.

Ciryl looked at the melting spade stitched into his work coat, then decided not to argue.

Alice lifted her basket. "We'll find your crew before the scavengers arrive."

"Why?"

"Because they're missing."

"You don't know me."

"I know where six of them went."

She started toward the north root.

Ciryl followed because the alternatives were remaining alone beside a damaged colony or explaining his mountain to the next person who happened to be gathering lunch.

Chester followed several moments later, after making it clear that he had chosen to do so.

The trail moved through the outer forest in fragments. A broken fungus shelf pointed toward a drainage channel. A cluster of listening cups had folded themselves around the memory of heavy footsteps. One of the hedgehogs had left quill grooves in a patch of soft bark while squeezing through a narrow gap.

Alice read each sign without ceremony.

She knew which roots were sleeping, which pools were mouths, and which pale flowers only pretended to be flowers when strangers were nearby. At one stream she held up a hand before Ciryl stepped onto a flat stone.

The stone opened one eye.

Ciryl stepped around it.

"Does everything here eat people?" he asked.

"No. Some things wait for animals."

"That is not better."

"It is if you're not an animal."

Chester gave her a look.

Alice reconsidered.

"It is occasionally better."

They found the first two Spades inside a hollow log that had grown a temporary door over its opening. One had a bruised shoulder. The other had remained curled so long that his legs refused to unfold.

They had seen the rest of the crew head toward an old pumping station farther uphill.

"We thought you were behind us," said the injured Spade.

Ciryl looked at the forest floor. "I took another route."

"You always take another route."

"This one was more another than usual."

Alice glanced at him but said nothing.

They continued uphill.

The pumping station had once been a living reservoir. Its outer shell resembled a fallen tree trunk, but inside, rows of translucent sacs still lifted groundwater through capillary roots. The remaining Spades had gathered in a dry chamber beneath the central bladder.

The foreman was there, no longer round and increasingly angry about his missing lunch.

He saw Ciryl and crossed the room.

"Where were you?"

Ciryl opened his mouth.

The foreman noticed the bare, damp gap across his shoulder quills.

"What happened to your back?"

"Weather."

The foreman stared at him.

Alice set the crystal structure on an old worktable.

Every Spade in the chamber became quiet.

An elderly gardener approached it. Her quills were silver at the tips and threaded with small green mosses. She did not touch the crystal.

"Where did you get mountain glass?"

Ciryl looked at Alice.

Alice looked at Chester.

Chester had found a warm pipe and was lying across it.

"He took a wrong tunnel," Alice said.

The elderly Spade studied Ciryl's face.

"There are wrong tunnels, and there are tunnels that object to being called wrong."

"Do you know the mountains?" Alice asked.

"I know the roots stop before them."

"Why?"

"Roots have sense."

The foreman folded his arms. "The crew is accounted for. The colony is damaged. The Clubs will have eaten the cooling beds by now. We are returning when the signals clear."

No one asked Ciryl to explain the mountain in front of the whole crew. He was grateful until he realized that Alice was still watching him.

They left the pumping station near evening.

Ciryl expected Alice to return to her mushrooms. Instead, she walked beside him until the others were out of hearing.

"You said you saw the whole city."

"I think so."

"There is no ridge in the outer forest high enough to see the whole city."

"I know."

"And you saw something beyond the mountains."

Ciryl rubbed the place where the crystal had attached.

"Maybe."

"What did it look like?"

"Dry."

Alice waited.

"Very dry," he added.

"That is not a description."

"It had a straight horizon."

That stopped her.

Wonderland had many horizons. None stayed straight for long.

Chester emerged from the shadow beneath a root, though Ciryl had not seen him enter it.

Alice looked at him. "You know where he went."

"I know several places he did not go."

"Was he outside Wonderland?"

Chester's grin appeared again, broad and insulting.

"Outside is such a confident word."

Ciryl pointed at him. "He has been doing that all day."

"For years," Alice said.

She turned back to Ciryl. "Show me the tunnel."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I nearly froze into part of a mountain."

"Chester removed the glass."

"That does not make the mountain less interested."

Alice looked toward the distant pumping station where the other Spades had found shelter.

"I helped you find your crew."

"That sounds like a debt when you say it that way."

"It was meant to."

Ciryl considered refusing again. Then he considered spending the rest of his life uncertain whether the mountain, the city below, and the impossible dry world had been real.

"We look at the hole," he said. "We do not go through it."

"Agreed."

Chester opened one eye.

"She has changed the meaning of 'look' before."

Ciryl led them back toward the damaged colony.

The giant Clubs had gone. So had the larvae, most of the edible fungus, both lunch baskets, and one section of the supervisor's portable shelter. The hedgehogs had begun repairs along the main bed, but Ciryl kept to the far edge and entered the old maintenance burrow behind his break root.

The tunnel was too small for Alice.

Ciryl crawled a few feet inside, then turned around.

"This is where I went."

Alice knelt beside the opening. Cold air moved faintly through the roots.

It carried no smell of soil, rain, fungus, fur, smoke, stone, or water.

Chester sniffed the opening and sat down.

For once, he did not disappear through it.

Alice noticed.

"The portal is still there," Ciryl said. "Farther in."

"You won't fit," he added.

Alice reached beneath her apron and removed a narrow metal tin.

Chester's amber eyes opened fully.

Ciryl leaned closer as she lifted the lid.

Two dried fragments of mushroom rested inside, separated by a brass divider. One was pale and fibrous. The other retained a dark blue edge.

Ciryl frowned. "What are those?"

Alice pinched a measured piece from the pale fragment.

Deep inside the tunnel, something rang like a glass bell buried beneath the world.

"Food," she said.

"What does it do?"

Alice raised the mushroom toward her mouth.

"This side makes the tunnel larger."

Chester's sarcastic grin appeared.

Alice looked at the little hole, then at the fragment between her fingers.

"Relatively."
