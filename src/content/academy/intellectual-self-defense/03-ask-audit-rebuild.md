The most useful AI research work often happens before the conclusion.

A chatbot can suggest search terms, expand acronyms, identify institutions, build a chronology, translate a passage, separate claims, and locate disagreements. These jobs reduce access friction. They also give you a chance to inspect the route before the prose hardens into an opinion.

The method in this lesson is simple: ask, audit, open, compare, rebuild, decide.

## Historical ancestor: notes before synthesis

Researchers have long kept bibliographies, excerpt cards, timelines, and comparison tables. The tools varied, but the middle stage mattered. Evidence had to be collected and sorted before it could carry a conclusion.

The chatbot can accelerate this middle stage. It can also skip it on your behalf and deliver a conclusion with the scaffolding hidden. Your job is to make the scaffolding visible again.

## Step 1: ask for a provisional map

Do not begin with “write my final answer.” Ask what kinds of sources could answer the question, which terms may be ambiguous, what dates matter, and where credible disagreement exists.

Call the result provisional. The label prevents the first framing from pretending it is final.

For a question about why a public project failed, the map might include budgets, official goals, audits, participant accounts, outcome measures, local reporting, and later analysis. That list is not the explanation. It is the search plan.

## Step 2: audit the claims

Break the provisional answer into individual claims. Small claims are easier to verify than one smooth paragraph.

Mark each claim:

- retrieved fact;
- direct quotation;
- model summary;
- inference;
- speculation;
- missing evidence.

If a sentence contains two claims, split it. “The program exceeded its budget because managers ignored warnings” requires evidence for the budget, the warnings, the people who received them, and the causal connection.

## Step 3: open the important sources

Open the sources that carry the central decision. Confirm the title, author, date, and relevant passage. Save enough context to understand the passage later.

Do not let the chatbot's excerpt become the permanent copy of the source. Go to the source itself whenever possible.

## Step 4: compare supporting and contrary evidence

Ask what a competing explanation would predict. Look for material that could weaken the current account, not only more copies of the same claim.

Comparison can reveal that two sources do not actually disagree. They may describe different dates, populations, or definitions. A useful contradiction table names the exact point of conflict.

| Source | Claim | Evidence | Date or scope | Actual disagreement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Outcome improved | Survey result | First year | Measures satisfaction |
| B | Outcome failed | Administrative data | Three years | Measures completion |

Both accounts may be accurate within different measurements. The job is to stop them from being flattened into a fake yes-or-no fight.

## Step 5: rebuild what the evidence supports

Close the provisional answer. Write a new note from the sources you opened.

Keep the categories visible:

- what is directly established;
- what you infer from the evidence;
- what remains unknown;
- what would change the interpretation.

This is the point where AI can help organize or edit. Give it the opened material and tell it not to add facts beyond that packet. Then compare the draft with your source map.

## Step 6: decide and state confidence

The opinion comes last.

Confidence should match the decision. A low-stakes reading list can tolerate more uncertainty than a medical, legal, financial, or public accusation. “Enough to keep reading” and “enough to publish” are different thresholds.

State confidence in ordinary language. For example:

> The official record confirms the date and budget change. The cause remains disputed. Two later accounts point to staffing, but neither can establish that it was the main cause.

That sentence is less dramatic than a total explanation. It is also usable.

## Worked example: unfamiliar topic

Suppose you encounter a claim about an old media technology you do not know.

1. Ask for terminology, date range, original manufacturers, trade publications, and archive collections.
2. Extract the factual claims in the first answer.
3. Open the archival catalog and one credible history.
4. Preserve original wording beside any translation.
5. Compare dates and definitions.
6. Rebuild a short note with direct support, inference, and open receipts.

The chatbot helped you enter the subject. It did not become the subject's source.

## Exercise: build one verified note

Choose a question with a real consequence but a manageable scope. Run the six stages. Keep a research log containing search terms, opened sources, rejected leads, and remaining gaps.

Use the Receipt Report as the container. Finish with:

- the question;
- sources opened;
- direct support;
- contrary or complicating evidence;
- inference;
- uncertainty;
- confidence;
- what would change the conclusion.

## Learner output: verified research note

Save the note with a date. A verified note is not a permanent truth certificate. It is a record of what the available evidence supported when you checked it.
