## Typography can wear a lab coat

A link, footnote number, DOI, or familiar institution can make an answer look finished. None of those marks prove that the source exists, that the link reaches the original, or that the source supports the sentence beside it.

The receipt has several checks:

1. Does the cited object exist?
2. Is it the original source or a summary of one?
3. Is the author, publisher, or institution represented accurately?
4. Does the relevant passage support the claim?
5. Does the answer stay inside the source's limits?
6. What inference did the answer add?

A real source can be used badly. A correct statement can arrive without a visible source. A nonexistent citation can look perfect. These are different failures and they need different responses.

## What the cutaway shows

The figure begins with one polished answer card. Opening it reveals four compartments: sources, inference, supported material, and unknowns.

The source compartment asks whether the item exists, whether it is original, and what it supports. The inference compartment asks what the answer joined, interpreted, or assumed. The supported compartment requires an opened source and a relevant passage. The unknown compartment keeps missing evidence and contradictions visible.

This is the point of a Receipt Report. It does not reward suspicion for its own sake. It records the load each source can carry.

## Earlier mechanism, current interface

Footnotes have always been vulnerable to decorative use. A writer could cite a respected work that only touched the subject, bury a qualification, or send the reader into a maze. The web made the route faster. Generative systems made the surface faster still.

NIST warns that a model may generate citations or logical explanations that appear to justify an incorrect answer. The practical repair is old-fashioned: open the material.

Lateral reading adds another move. Instead of staying inside an unfamiliar page and judging its design, author bio, or About page, leave it. Find what other credible sources say about the publisher, author, or claim. The page's own presentation is evidence about presentation, not an independent reputation check.

## Four answer cards

Imagine four claims:

### Card A: supported and relevant

The answer links to an official report. The report exists. The cited section states the fact, gives the date, and uses the same population the answer describes. This receipt can carry the claim, subject to the report's own limits.

### Card B: real source, wrong support

The linked study exists, but it covers adults while the answer makes a claim about children. The citation is real. The support is not.

### Card C: citation costume

The title, author list, journal, and page range look plausible. The record cannot be found in the journal, a DOI registry, the authors' publication lists, or a library catalog. Mark it unverified. Do not invent a correction that merely sounds more likely.

### Card D: correct-looking statement, no receipt

The sentence may be true. You still need a source if the fact matters to your decision or publication. “Probably right” and “supported” are separate statuses.

## Guided exercise: one important citation

Choose the most important factual claim in a chatbot answer.

1. Copy the claim into your Receipt Report.
2. Open the cited link.
3. Identify whether it is primary, secondary, or a lead.
4. Find the exact passage that supports the claim.
5. Record any mismatch in date, population, jurisdiction, definition, or certainty.
6. Leave the site and check the source's identity or reputation laterally.
7. Mark the claim supported, partly supported, unsupported, contradicted, or unresolved.

Do not grade the source by whether you like the conclusion. Grade the fit between evidence and claim.

## Independent exercise: build a source map

Map one claim through at least three layers:

```text
claim in the answer
→ citation or link
→ original source
→ relevant passage
→ what the passage can establish
→ what remains inference or unknown
```

If the answer links to a news report about a study, locate the study. If the study cites an earlier dataset, note that handoff. You do not need to descend forever. Stop when the evidence is strong enough for the decision at hand and the remaining limits are visible.

## Translation is a route, not proof

Translation can unlock material you could not otherwise inspect. Keep the original beside the translation, preserve names and technical terms, and mark uncertain passages. A fluent translation does not prove the original claim. It makes the claim available for inspection.

## Check your work

- [ ] I opened the important source.
- [ ] I identified whether it was original, secondary, or only a lead.
- [ ] I found the passage that carries the claim.
- [ ] I recorded what the source cannot establish.
- [ ] I checked the source outside its own presentation.
- [ ] I marked inference and unknowns separately.

The receipt is open when another person can follow your route without borrowing your confidence.
