## The old route was visible

Research used to announce its machinery. You started with a question, went to a library, searched a catalog, copied a call number, found a shelf, opened a book, checked the index, took notes, compared accounts, and wrote something the material could support.

That route was slow. It was also imperfect. A library collection reflected budgets, cataloging rules, institutional priorities, missing books, and the limits of whoever built it. A published book could still be wrong. A neat card did not certify the truth.

The useful feature was visibility. The catalog was not pretending to be the book. The book was not pretending to contain every account. Your notes were visibly separate from the conclusion. Each handoff left a seam you could inspect.

The card catalog started talking back.

A chatbot can now compress discovery, explanation, comparison, translation, organization, and drafting into one friendly surface. That compression is powerful. It can also hide which job the system performed at each sentence. A link may be a source, a lead, or decorative citation furniture. A summary may contain retrieved statements, plausible connective language, and an inference that nobody marked as an inference.

AI does not eliminate research friction. It relocates it.

## What the diagram shows

The left side of the figure shows an old visible route. The right side opens the answer box into stages that may include instructions, retrieval, model memory, source selection, compression, inference, generated language, formatting, and human verification.

Not every system uses every stage in the same way. Some tools browse. Some answer from model weights. Some combine both. Interfaces and features change. The operating question stays useful: what job happened here, and what receipt would let me check it?

The fast answer shifts effort into six places:

1. Defining the actual task.
2. Choosing the evidence standard.
3. Inspecting important sources.
4. Separating fact from inference and invention.
5. Controlling scope.
6. Making the final judgment.

The course follows those six jobs through three lanes: using AI to learn, using AI to make, and living around AI. The third lane gets easier after you have seen the first two from inside a workflow.

## A quick cutaway

Take a confident answer to a factual question. Do not decide whether you agree with it yet. Mark each sentence or claim with one of these labels:

- **Source visible:** you can open the original material.
- **Summary:** the answer compresses material you can inspect.
- **Inference:** the answer joins evidence into an interpretation.
- **Unknown:** you cannot tell where the statement came from.
- **Decision:** the answer recommends what you should believe or do.

If the answer includes citations, opening them is part of the exercise. A citation can exist and still fail to support the sentence beside it. A source can support one clause and not the conclusion built around it.

This is not a ritual of distrusting every sentence. It is a way to put the parts back on the workbench.

## Historical ancestor, current mechanism

The Library of Congress describes subject, author, and title cards as routes into a collection. The card tells you where to continue. It does not finish the investigation.

NIST's Generative AI Profile uses the word *confabulation* for confidently presented false or erroneous content. It also notes that generated citations and explanations can mislead people into trusting an incorrect answer. That does not make a chatbot useless. It gives you a reason to identify the job before treating the output as a source.

## Guided exercise: draw your checkpoints

Choose one answer you used recently. Reconstruct the path as far as you can:

```text
my question
→ tool or platform
→ sources shown or not shown
→ summary
→ inference
→ my decision
```

Put a question mark where the chain disappears. Then write one checkpoint that would have made the route more inspectable. Examples include “open the original report,” “find the publication date,” “compare a contrary account,” or “ask what evidence would change this answer.”

## Independent exercise: baseline self-audit

Make a one-page map with three columns:

| Learn | Make | Live around AI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How I research now | How I use tools to create | How I judge posts, clips, headlines, and summaries |

Under each column, record one habit you want to keep and one place where the route disappears. This is a baseline, not a confession. You will return to it in the final lesson.
